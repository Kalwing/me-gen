Onto Grissom Academy — you want the joke first, fine, here it is: a woman who spent her whole life treating every fence, every wall, every locked door as something to put a hole through, ends up on the payroll of an institution, teaching kids how to stand inside a classroom instead of running. I know how that sounds. I heard it plenty from myself before anybody else got the chance.

Here's the difference, and I need the people in this room who still think biotic kids are hardware to actually listen instead of nodding along drunk. Before Grissom, the Alliance ran a program called BAaT — Jump Zero, the kids there called it "Brain Camp," like it was summer fun instead of a company shipping traumatized children to a station in the middle of nowhere and cutting them off from their families so nobody would ask questions. They didn't even know how to train biotics, so instead of eating the embarrassment of asking the Citadel for help, they quietly hired turian mercs to run it like a boot camp for weapons that happened to still be kids. One of them, Vyrnnus, used hunger and thirst as training tools, and when he broke a girl's arm for being too tired to lift a glass with her own hands instead of her biotics, a student snapped and killed him with a kick to the skull. Some others didn't survive the strain at all. That's a file somebody sealed in 2169 because burying it was cheaper than admitting the Alliance ran a torture camp for its own biotic children and called it acclimation. Ascension Project, the program that came after, at least let families visit, at least let a kid say no. Kahlee Sanders ran it like it mattered. I signed on to that one, not because I trust institutions now — fuck no — but because somebody who'd actually lived what I lived needed to be the one deciding what "training" meant this time. Not a turian with a stopwatch. Not Cerberus with a scalpel. Me.

And then Cerberus went and made the argument for me better than I ever could, because subtlety isn't their thing. After Palaven, Traynor and EDI pulled apart a rescue signal answering our kids' evac call and found it was fake — the same kind of signature that once dragged Shepard's whole ship into a Collector ambush. Cerberus was sitting on Grissom with a cruiser and a dozen fighters, not to kill anybody. To take them. My kids, most of them still learning to hold a field steady without breaking their own wrist, and Cerberus wanted them as stock, same math they ran on me at Teltin, scaled up to a whole graduating class. There was a window, a real one, before it closed for good, and if Shepard had let it slide chasing something shinier, every one of those kids gets "subverted" — the polite word for turned into me. I know exactly what that conversion looks like from the inside. Wasn't letting it happen twice under my own name.

He went. Too many ships to fight straight, so Joker ran a diversion loud enough to buy Kahlee a cargo hold to crack open from the inside — I got all this part after, pieced together from Kahlee and whoever would still talk about it, because I was already three decks over holding my own line. Cerberus had some officer on the intercom telling kids to stop resisting, it'll be painless — I've heard that voice before, doesn't matter which uniform's wearing it that week — while Shepard cut a straight line to Reiley Bellarmine, sister bleeding out in his arms, and kept going. He got to Orion Hall where my kids were holding a barrier against troopers and an Atlas, and I was already there, because I wasn't leaving that room to anybody else's judgment. I hit him in the face the second the shooting stopped. Then I kissed him, because apparently that's allowed to be the same motion now. We fought Centurions and Guardians and two more Atlases clear across that station, using their own dead mechs against them, Octavia and the tech kids laying down cover the whole way, David Archer vouching for us at a shield door he had every reason not to trust anyone standing behind. Rodriguez went down right at the shuttles, and I put my hand through a wall of glass instead of leaving her two more seconds of Cerberus fire. Everybody got out. Every single one. If I hadn't been standing in that hall, it's a different name I'm saying tonight — it would've been Prangley smashing that glass and Prangley's grave instead of Rodriguez walking off that ship on her own legs.

Back on the Normandy, the brass wanted my kids running as another artillery unit — cheaper than training real gunners, I guess. I said no — support units, barrier work, ammo mods, keep them alive long enough to grow into whatever they're going to be instead of feeding them into a war that doesn't care what they could've been otherwise. Shepard didn't debate me on it. Didn't ask the brass to make their case again, didn't split the difference to look fair. Just said all right and made it an order — 103rd Marine Division, support, done — and let me have the thing I was too proud to say out loud I needed: one decision in this whole war where people who'd survived Cerberus once got to decide they weren't doing it again. Kahlee kept coordinating the fleet's leftovers after that, holding pieces together nobody else bothers to name. What matters is my kids get to be people tonight, not weapons somebody else got to point.

So here's my real opinion, since I promised the room I'd give it. Anybody who still looks at a fifteen-year-old throwing a biotic field and sees a rifle instead of a kid — Conatix saw that, Cerberus saw that — get the fuck out of this wake right now, because Shepard didn't, not once, and I'm not spending his funeral standing next to people who'd have made the opposite call.
