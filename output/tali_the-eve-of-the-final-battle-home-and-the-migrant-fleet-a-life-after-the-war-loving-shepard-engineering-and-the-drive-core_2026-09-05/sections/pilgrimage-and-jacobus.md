You saved my life on Haestrom. That's true, and I'll go on saying it, but it isn't where we actually start, Shepard, whatever the mission logs say. We start earlier. Every quarian leaves, Shepard. Outsiders hear "exile" and think it's punishment, all seventeen million of us paying forever for what our ancestors did to the geth. It isn't only that. Every quarian who comes of age leaves the ship they were born on and comes back with something worth a berth on a new one — raw materials, salvaged tech, data nobody else could get, anything the Fleet can't grow or build for itself in a hold that's recycled the same air and metal for three hundred years. You leave nar your birth ship. You come back vas the one that took you in, if it takes you in, if you're good enough, if you're lucky. I was nar Rayya when I left. My father sat on the Admiralty Board, and everyone assumed his daughter's Pilgrimage would be a tidy formality. Instead I chased geth activity past the Perseus Veil and found something that got everyone I traveled with killed.

I was aboard the Honorata with a friend, Keenah'Breizh, and we tracked the geth to an ice world in the Crescent Nebula where they were digging up Prothean ruins for someone. Geth don't dig for curiosity. Someone was paying for it. I found a data core — a recording of a geth transmitting straight to a Council Spectre named Saren Arterius, mentioning something called Sovereign, something called the Conduit — words that meant nothing to me yet and mean everything to me now. That data core is the reason any of us are standing here tonight.

Saren had a turian mercenary running security on that dig, a man named Jacobus, who knew what Saren did to people who let evidence walk. So he came after us. Not in a rush, either — he had time, and he used it. His men caught the Honorata at Illium and killed the whole crew but Keenah and me.

Keelah. Listen to me, I'm giving you dates and ship names. Sorry. He followed us both to the Citadel after that and put a shot in each of us doing it. Keenah died in a maintenance corridor near an incinerator before I could do anything for him that mattered, and I was alone with a body and a killer still coming. I sprung the trap myself, sealed him in, and burned him alive. I was nineteen. Nobody on the Board ever asked what that did to my hands, only whether the data survived. It did. That tells you what a Pilgrimage teaches you to prioritize.

I limped into a clinic with two bullet wounds and evidence that could topple a Spectre. A human doctor, Chloe Michel, patched me up and pointed me toward a fixer called Fist, who worked the underworld out of a club in the Wards and could supposedly find me a buyer. I reached out to the Shadow Broker myself — asked for protection, got told no deal unless I met him in person, which I couldn't do with Saren's people already circling. I learned much later the Broker had ordered Fist to kill me once the handoff was done. Fist decided selling me to Saren directly was worth more. Everyone on that station wanted a piece of a nineteen-year-old with a stolen file and nowhere safe to stand.

The Citadel that year did not go easy on anyone the galaxy had written off. I heard, secondhand, while I hid a few decks over, about a human woman — a Mindoir survivor, years a batarian slave, taught to call herself an animal — who broke down at a dock with a weapon, and a human Alliance commander talked her down rather than let C-Sec's sniper take the shot. I remember thinking that station does that to people. Grinds them down until they'll say whatever about themselves. And then somebody takes the slow expensive way with one of them instead of the clean one, and it's news.

Because that's what happened to me too. Word reached Fist's people that a human commander was hunting the same evidence I carried, and instead of another buyer looking to bury me, I got you — a stranger with no reason to trust a quarian he'd never met, who decided the honest thing was to believe her. You got the recording to the Council. Saren's Spectre status was revoked because of it. Somewhere in an unfinished letter I still haven't sent, there's a boy named Keenah who never knew any of that mattered, and a girl who came home vas Neema instead of nar Rayya — not because she was brave, but because she was the only one left standing to carry it home.
