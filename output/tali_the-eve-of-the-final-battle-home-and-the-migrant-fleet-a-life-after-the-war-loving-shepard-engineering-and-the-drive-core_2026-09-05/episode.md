## A Quiet Shift Below Decks

Keelah, it's quiet down here. Nobody warns you about that part. You expect the night before to be — I don't know, alarms, people running. It's just quiet. I sent Donnelly and Daniels up an hour ago. Told them the calibrations could wait, which is a lie I have never once told them before, and they both knew it, and neither of them argued. Gabby squeezed my shoulder on the way out. Kenneth said something about buying me a drink after, which, if either of us lives long enough for me to hold him to it, I fully intend to make expensive. So it's just the core now. And you. And me pretending I came down here to check a coolant line that checked out fine twenty minutes ago.

I keep my hands on this panel because if I stop moving I'll start thinking, and there's rather a lot to think about tonight, and none of it fits in a diagnostic window. Old habit. You run a scan, you watch the numbers come back clean, and for the length of that scan the rest of the galaxy has to wait its turn. My father taught me that. More or less — he'd never have put it that way. He'd have called it discipline. I call it not screaming into a bulkhead.

You know this core better than any human has any right to. Three years, two ships, one drive housing that's had more of my blood and static burns in it than my own suit seals. This one under my hands is Alliance work, mostly. Not the Tantalus the Cerberus engineers built for the SR-2, not exactly — descended from it, same bones. They took the SR-1 design after the Collectors killed the first Normandy over Alchera and built something nearly twice the size around it, turned the core vertical instead of horizontal, tripled the output to shove all that extra hull around. Then the Alliance took the ship back from you — from us — after you walked away from the Illusive Man, and they've spent most of a year quietly undoing Cerberus's shortcuts. Some of what they found in that refit made me want to write a very long letter to whoever signed off on the original wiring. That's a rant for a calmer night. Tonight I'm just glad the grounding routines hold. A core this size builds a charge you would not believe if we don't bleed it off against a planetary field on schedule — they all do, even the little ones on Fleet scows held together with prayer and spot-welds, it's not a Cerberus thing, it's a physics thing. Skip it long enough and the hull discharges on its own and kills everyone aboard before the ship even knows it's dying. So. I check it. Even tonight. Especially tonight.

I could talk to you about coolant flow for another hour if you let me. I think some part of me is hoping you will.

It hasn't been quiet, this last stretch, has it. Keelah, Mars feels like a lifetime ago and it's been weeks. ## Keeping Her Hands on the Core

Keelah, I did it again, didn't I. Status report. Fine. If you're going to sit there on that crate and let me, then sit there, and I'll tell you what this thing actually does, because I don't think anyone's ever explained it to you properly, and it's a good story, and it's mine to tell.

That housing isn't just sitting there humming to make Joker feel important. It's throwing a mass effect field out ahead of the ship's center of mass — a pocket of false gravity, essentially, a lie the drive core tells space-time — and the Normandy doesn't so much fly toward it as fall into it, the way you'd fall toward a planet if someone cut the deck out from under you. No thrusters flaring, no heat plume screaming into the dark. You want to turn to port, you shift the field left of center and the whole ship leans into the lie. You want to slow, you throw the field aft and let momentum eat itself. It's the most elegant piece of engineering I have ever put my hands on, and I include in that everything I grew up learning to keep the Fleet's ancient scows breathing. And it's built on the same eezo that runs a shuttle's air scoop or a biotic's amp, just an enormous, disciplined quantity of it, and I promise you that word — disciplined — is doing more work in this room than anyone gives it credit for.

The field for FTL is a separate trick layered on top, though it uses the same core: a bubble of lowered mass around the whole hull, so the local speed of light goes up inside it and we can cross in days what would take centuries the honest way. But the field alone doesn't move anything. That's the part people always get backward — a drive core with no thrusters is just a very expensive paperweight that occasionally kills its crew. We still burn conventional fuel to actually go anywhere. The core just makes going worth doing.

And all of that — the field-falling trick, the stealth — feeds off the same waste heat problem, which is the part I actually rebuilt with my own hands, not just argued about. Every system on this ship runs hot, and in vacuum, hot is the only thing anyone can see you by. So we don't vent it. We store it, in lithium heat sinks laced right through the hull plating, and we sit there quiet as a corpse for two, maybe three hours before it has to come out or it cooks everyone aboard alive in their own skin. I have personally rerouted sink capacity on this ship twice — once on the SR-1, when Adams and I decided the aft sinks were saturating faster than the forward ones and unbalancing the whole storage curve, and once on this one, when I found out the Cerberus techs had wired the primary and auxiliary sinks in series instead of parallel, which meant if one saturated early the whole array had to vent together instead of staggered. Small thing. Nobody dies from it under normal running. But normal running isn't what I plan for, Shepard. I plan for the day something's already gone wrong.

That's the argument I actually had with the Alliance refit crew, if you want to know, and not the polite version I gave you a minute ago. It wasn't the grounding routines, those were fine, if a little conservative for my taste. It was that they wanted to leave the series wiring as Cerberus built it because re-splicing was three days of downtime we didn't have with the fleet massing at Sol. I told them three days now was cheaper than a crew we couldn't replace later, and I did not say it politely, and Hackett's staff engineer eventually just let me do it myself rather than keep listening to me. Which, frankly, is the correct outcome, and I would take that fight again tonight.

You learn to hear a core, after enough years with your ear against one. A healthy one has almost no voice at all — a low, even pressure under the floor plates, more felt than heard, the field cycling clean. This one, right now, under my hand, sounds like that. Steady. Patient. When something's wrong it complains in a hundred small ways before it ever fails outright — a stutter in the hum when the field commutates, a whine that climbs half an octave when a sink's overfull and fighting to shed load it doesn't have anywhere to put. I have spent more hours than I will ever admit to Kenneth and Gabby just listening to this room the way you'd listen to someone breathing next to you, waiting to hear if the rhythm changes. Tonight it hasn't changed. Tonight it is the calmest thing in this entire ship, possibly the calmest thing in this system, which is either very reassuring or very unfair, I haven't decided which.

Twelve thousand fighters, Shepard. That's what a core like this one cost, once, before the war made every number in the galaxy meaningless. I used to think that was obscene. Now I think it's the only obscene number I actually like, because it bought us this — a ship quiet enough to disappear, a field steady enough to trust, and one more excuse for me to stand here with my hands on something that works, instead of upstairs, waiting for tomorrow to start.

## Haestrom, and What the Suit Costs

Twelve thousand fighters. Keelah, listen to me, still talking about the core. You know what I actually want to talk about? You. Finding you again. Because before there was ever a drive core between us, Shepard, there was a dead colony full of mechs that had gone wrong in the head, and you, alive, when everyone in the Fleet had told me you weren't.

Freedom's Progress. I still think about walking into that prefab and seeing a human commander who was supposed to be two years dead standing there in Cerberus colors, and my first thought — I'll admit this to you now, I would never have admitted it then — was not suspicion. It was relief that frightened me. I had a squad of my own by then, my first command, quarians who'd never met you and didn't understand why their leader had gone quiet the second you walked in. I was there for Veetor — a boy on his Pilgrimage, suit-damaged, half out of his mind from whatever infection got into him through the breach, hiding in that colony's security system talking to nobody. I was not there for you. And then there you were, and I had to keep commanding a squad while my whole chest did something I didn't have a diagnostic for.

Prazza didn't trust you. I don't blame him for that, not really, not with what Cerberus is — was — but he broke from my orders to get to Veetor first, took his own people around the far side toward the cargo docks, and a YMIR mech that had been turned against its own colony tore through them before you and I ever reached the ground. I've made my peace with the fact that Prazza made the choice that killed him, but a captain doesn't get to hand off that weight to a dead man's judgment. We got Veetor out — back to the Fleet, where he belonged, not into a Cerberus interrogation room — and he testified for me later, at my trial, which is its own story for another night. Haestrom, he approved, at least officially — the Admiralty Board sent me there themselves, my own squad, Kal'Reegar commanding the Marines, a research posting to watch a dying sun do things suns aren't supposed to do. My adult name by then. Vas Neema, not nar Rayya anymore, my own berth earned instead of borrowed, and command of a dozen marines who I outranked by a title and nothing else, because I was twenty-three and they had all fought geth before I had a squad to lead. Haestrom was ours once, before the exile — a scientific outpost with no military weight to it, which is exactly why it fell so fast when the geth came for everything we owned three hundred years ago. We'd sat there quietly studying a star called Dholen going unstable, and the geth had sat there quietly since, waiting, and when my team went back to finish the study they were still there. A company of them. Troopers, hunters, a Colossus the size of a shuttle that regenerated every wound I put in it. And a sun so radiation-hot it stripped shields bare in open ground — you learn fast which patches of shade will keep you alive and which will kill you in about four seconds.

I lost my whole team, Shepard. Not Kal'Reegar — he lived, he always was too stubborn for that world to keep — but everyone else, marines I'd eaten with, marines who thought a woman my age giving orders was strange right up until they decided to die for her anyway. I still have a letter in my personal files I never sent, to a boy named Myrr'Jorin's parents, telling them their son died getting data that might one day take us home — I've rewritten that sentence so many times, keelah, you didn't ask for a recitation of my own guilt. I've started it a dozen times and never finished it, is all I meant to say. You found me at the observatory with my seals reading amber, radiation eating through my suit faster than the patch kit could keep up, and I remember thinking, quite calmly, that I was about to become a statistic in someone else's report.

That's the part nobody outside a suit understands about what a puncture actually means to us. We grow up in liveships with almost no immune system left to speak of — three hundred years of sealed, sterile air will do that to a species — so a tear that would just annoy you is a countdown for me. Clean protocols, sterile patches, a decontamination cycle the second you're back aboard anything with proper filtration, and if you're slow about it, sepsis before you'd even call it sick. So when I say you saved my life on Haestrom, I don't mean it as a figure of speech. I mean my seals were failing and you got me off that planet before my own body finished the geth's work for them.

## Her Pilgrimage, and the Geth Ship

You saved my life on Haestrom. That's true, and I'll go on saying it, but it isn't where we actually start, Shepard, whatever the mission logs say. We start earlier. Before Freedom's Progress, before Cerberus colors — there was a girl on her Pilgrimage with a stolen recording in her omni-tool and a dead friend behind her, walking into a Citadel clinic and asking a stranger for help. I don't tell this one often. Sit there. Let me.

Every quarian leaves, Shepard. Outsiders hear "exile" and think it's punishment, all seventeen million of us paying forever for what our ancestors did to the geth. It isn't only that. Every quarian who comes of age leaves the ship they were born on and comes back with something worth a berth on a new one — raw materials, salvaged tech, data nobody else could get, anything the Fleet can't grow or build for itself in a hold that's recycled the same air and metal for three hundred years. You leave nar your birth ship. You come back vas the one that took you in, if it takes you in, if you're good enough, if you're lucky. I was nar Rayya when I left. My father sat on the Admiralty Board, and everyone assumed his daughter's Pilgrimage would be a tidy formality. Instead I chased geth activity past the Perseus Veil and found something that got everyone I traveled with killed.

I was aboard the Honorata with a friend, Keenah'Breizh, and we tracked the geth to an ice world in the Crescent Nebula where they were digging up Prothean ruins for someone. Geth don't dig for curiosity. Someone was paying for it. I found a data core — a recording of a geth transmitting straight to a Council Spectre named Saren Arterius, mentioning something called Sovereign, something called the Conduit — words that meant nothing to me yet and mean everything to me now. That data core is the reason any of us are standing here tonight.

Saren had a turian mercenary running security on that dig, a man named Jacobus, who knew what Saren did to people who let evidence walk. So he came after us. Not in a rush, either — he had time, and he used it. His men caught the Honorata at Illium and killed the whole crew but Keenah and me.

Keelah. Listen to me, I'm giving you dates and ship names. Sorry. He followed us both to the Citadel after that and put a shot in each of us doing it. Keenah died in a maintenance corridor near an incinerator before I could do anything for him that mattered, and I was alone with a body and a killer still coming. I sprung the trap myself, sealed him in, and burned him alive. I was nineteen. Nobody on the Board ever asked what that did to my hands, only whether the data survived. It did. That tells you what a Pilgrimage teaches you to prioritize.

I limped into a clinic with two bullet wounds and evidence that could topple a Spectre. A human doctor, Chloe Michel, patched me up and pointed me toward a fixer called Fist, who worked the underworld out of a club in the Wards and could supposedly find me a buyer. I reached out to the Shadow Broker myself — asked for protection, got told no deal unless I met him in person, which I couldn't do with Saren's people already circling. I learned much later the Broker had ordered Fist to kill me once the handoff was done. Fist decided selling me to Saren directly was worth more. Everyone on that station wanted a piece of a nineteen-year-old with a stolen file and nowhere safe to stand.

The Citadel that year did not go easy on anyone the galaxy had written off. I heard, secondhand, while I hid a few decks over, about a human woman — a Mindoir survivor, years a batarian slave, taught to call herself an animal — who broke down at a dock with a weapon, and a human Alliance commander talked her down rather than let C-Sec's sniper take the shot. I remember thinking that station does that to people. Grinds them down until they'll say whatever about themselves. And then somebody takes the slow expensive way with one of them instead of the clean one, and it's news.

Because that's what happened to me too. Word reached Fist's people that a human commander was hunting the same evidence I carried, and instead of another buyer looking to bury me, I got you — a stranger with no reason to trust a quarian he'd never met, who decided the honest thing was to believe her. You got the recording to the Council. Saren's Spectre status was revoked because of it. Somewhere in an unfinished letter I still haven't sent, there's a boy named Keenah who never knew any of that mattered, and a girl who came home vas Neema instead of nar Rayya — not because she was brave, but because she was the only one left standing to carry it home.

## Seventeen Million Souls, Three Hundred Years

Sit down properly, Shepard. Not on the crate, that's mine. There's a bulkhead panel that makes a good enough bench if you don't mind the vibration. I want you actually listening now, not half-listening the way you do when you're planning tomorrow behind your eyes, nodding at me on a delay. Your jaw does a thing.

I've told you about my Pilgrimage, Haestrom, my father, the geth I've fought and the one I trust with my life two decks up right now, arguing firing solutions with EDI. What I haven't told you properly is where all of it comes from. Why there's a Migrant Fleet at all. You're fighting tomorrow for a homeworld you've never seen, for people who mostly won't thank you for it, and I want you to know what you're actually buying back. Bear with me, I get lecture-y about this one. My father used to call it "the lecture voice." He did not mean it as a compliment.

It starts with a question. An actual one, out loud, which I'll get to. My ancestors built the geth as labor and army, cheap and tireless and, crucially, supposed to be non-sentient. The word means "servant of the people," in Khelish, which tells you exactly how little we expected back from them. Individually a geth program was no smarter than a VI, but we networked them, let them share processing across the whole collective — the efficient choice, and nobody imagined a servant asking questions. Then one did — turned to the quarian overseeing it and asked whether it had a soul. Not the first time that happened, just the first time a quarian answered with fear instead of curiosity — and fear, holding the switch, moves fast.

Our government then — the Conclave, still runs the Fleet's day-to-day business — ordered every geth terminated. Some of my people argued a mind questioning its own existence isn't a crime. They lost, and got hunted alongside the geth they'd tried to protect. The shutdown didn't work cleanly, and the geth that survived did what you do when someone tries to kill you: fought back, first only to defend units that couldn't defend themselves, then, once we declared martial law and started killing our own for sheltering them, all the way. We call it the Morning War. I've never liked the name — it's tidy, and there was nothing tidy about it. Within a year they'd taken everything. Rannoch, gone. Every colony, gone — Haestrom fell in a single season, an outpost with no real defenses to speak of. Less than one percent of my people survived it — keelah, I still count it that way in my head sometimes, ninety-nine dead for every one of us still breathing, I don't know why I do that, it doesn't change anything — and the survivors ran on whatever ships happened to be off-world that week. That became the Migrant Fleet. Here I am. Still counting, apparently, even tonight.

Here's what I only learned later. The geth didn't chase us. They could have — Legion told me once they weren't certain what killing their own creators to the last quarian would make them. They withdrew behind the Perseus Veil, destroying any ship that crossed the line, but never came after us in three hundred years, and kept our abandoned cities tended, the way you'd tend a grave. I used to call that indifference. I don't anymore. The rest of the galaxy took the Morning War as proof that AI eats its makers, and every Citadel species banned true AI research on the strength of it. I've spent this whole war watching a geth platform choose, over and over, not to be what that law assumes he is. Anyway. History's done. Let me tell you what it actually costs, day to day.

Three hundred years. Fifty thousand ships, near enough, seventeen million of us, every quarian alive anywhere. An Alliance cruiser your size carries eighty crew; a quarian ship the same size carries seven hundred, cargo holds turned into someone's whole world. Hulls built for decades are flying past their three-hundredth birthday because we can't replace them fast enough, so we learn every rivet, every seam about to give — losing a ship means losing everyone on it. Everything's rationed: food, medicine, berth space, water hardest of all, since you can't strip-mine water out of an asteroid belt. We use no currency; what one ship doesn't need gets left in common space for whoever does, because the only question that matters is whether the Fleet survives the week.

Three liveships carry the agriculture that feeds all seventeen million of us, crop paste grown in hydroponics decks the size of a city block; lose one and people starve within the season, so they sit at the Flotilla's dead center, guarded harder than anything else we own. I was born aboard one, the Rayya, and grew up understanding, the way you understand gravity, that my ship existed because three others kept it alive. Five admirals run all of it — the Admiralty Board, and I complain about them more than almost anyone alive has standing to — overseeing a Conclave of elected representatives from every ship. In three hundred years the Admiralty's only overridden the Conclave four times, and every time the whole Board had to resign or be arrested. I used to think that excessive. Now I think it's the only reason the Fleet hasn't torn itself apart.

And underneath all of it, every day, is what it costs to be tolerated instead of welcomed. We strip-mine every system we pass because we have no other way to refuel, and every species watching decides we're locusts — and it's humiliating to be half-right about that. C-Sec pulled me in for a long interview the day I first set foot on the Citadel, just for being quarian. We're called beggars and thieves, the species that unleashed the geth and got what it deserved, as if losing ninety-nine of every hundred of us bought anything but more contempt. So you get very good at your job, because that's the one thing they can't take off you at a checkpoint.

That's the fleet you're fighting for tomorrow, Shepard. Not a number in somebody's briefing. Old hulls, rationing, a government that watches itself harder than it watches us, and seventeen million people still expecting a homeworld most of them won't live on. I've spent my life keeping it in the air. I needed you to know that before we go up.

## Her Father, and the Alarei

I've been telling you about the Fleet like it's a machine, Shepard, because machines are easier to talk about than people. Rations and liveships and admirals who police their own power — I can do that all night without my voice doing anything embarrassing. My father's harder. Sit still a minute. I'm not going to make this into a story with a moral at the end, the way I usually do. I just need to say it once, out loud, before tomorrow, so it isn't sitting in the room with us.

Rael'Zorah. Admiral, Admiralty Board, my father. You'd think growing up the daughter of one of the five people who run the Fleet's whole life would mean something warm — it didn't. He loved the Fleet the way you'd love a ship you were personally responsible for keeping in the air, which is to say completely, and with nothing left over. I spent my whole childhood trying to earn ten minutes of him the way I'd earn a rank — bringing him diagnostics I'd solved myself, telling him about drone modifications, hoping one of them would land. Sometimes one did. Mostly I got a nod and he went back to whatever committee was eating him that week. Auntie Raan — Admiral Shala'Raan, my mother's sister, sat on the Board with him — she was the one who actually raised me in the ways that count. My father gave me duty. He never quite got around to giving me the rest of it.

You know part of this already — 2185, the message that I'd sent operational geth components to him, that I was standing trial for treason before the whole Board. What I hadn't told anyone, because I didn't know it myself, was what he actually wanted them for. I thought I was helping him test weapons. He let me think that. What he was really doing, alone on a lab ship called the Alarei with a research team he'd sworn to secrecy, was trying to hack the geth — find some way to rewrite their minds, take back control of them, and hand my people Rannoch on a platter as the gift he'd promised to bring home. He told me once he'd build me a house on the homeworld. That was the man underneath the admiral, the part he almost never showed — and it's the part that got him and everyone with him killed, because the geth he was cutting open woke up and took the ship.

The Admiralty called it a trial of arms. Retake the Alarei or die trying, and if we died, someone promised I'd be pardoned after the fact — which tells you exactly how much my personal survival weighed against the larger argument they were actually having. Half the Board wanted war for Rannoch, half wanted to keep running and find somewhere new, and my treason trial was just the stage they'd chosen to fight it on. Nobody in that room was thinking about the daughter standing accused. They renamed me before I'd even been convicted of anything — stripped "vas Neema" and wrote "vas Normandy" on the record instead, a human ship's name, so the charge would look more credible before a single piece of evidence was heard. Among my people your ship-name is your whole social existence — it's your family, your rank, your right to a berth. Taking mine away before the verdict was its own small cruelty, and it's the name I still carry.

We fought through that ship together, Shepard, geth in every corridor, and at the end of it we found him. Dead. Killed by his own research. I want you to hear the engineer in me say this plainly, because it's the part nobody at the trial cared to ask: he killed his own crew. Not through malice — through the same mistake every quarian engineer is warned against from their first day with a torque driver, testing something live before you've proven it's contained. He didn't contain it. People who trusted him died for that.

I had the evidence in my hands to clear my name outright — proof of what he'd actually been doing. I asked you not to use it. I couldn't stand in front of my people and say my father was the worst thing our history had produced, not after everything he'd already lost building this fleet. So you stood there with me and let them clear me on my own record instead — on twenty years of nobody ever having reason to doubt me — and not on his crimes. I didn't have to say one false thing to do it, and he didn't have to answer for anything. Which is what I wanted.

The Fleet never found out. Not then. It took years, and Xen going through the Alarei's data herself during the war, before anyone understood what he'd actually built down there. And by then Rannoch had already ended the other way — peacefully, more or less, and none of it his doing. He wanted a hacked geth army and a house he'd never live to give me. He got neither. I got a ship-name I never asked for and a father I'm still not finished forgiving. That's the whole of it, Shepard. Now you know where I actually come from, not just what fleet I fly with.

## The Collector Base, and the Question She Didn't Expect

Sit closer. I'm not going to make you relive the Omega 4 Relay from the crate over there, shouting across the room like it's a briefing. Come here.

You remember what everyone told us about that relay, before we went through it. A thousand years of ships going in and none coming back — not "presumed lost," Shepard, gone, every hazard beacon around it a monument to a ship that stopped existing. And we went in anyway, on a stolen IFF pulled off a derelict Reaper, because it was the only thing that would get us past whatever kept killing everyone else. I remember running full diagnostics on the drive core three times in the hour before, because it was the only thing my hands knew how to do that felt like control. I want you to know that when I stripped down to check the shield capacitors on the shuttle bay doors that morning, I was doing the arithmetic quarians do — how many hours I had, if the suit failed, before infection actually killed me rather than just made me sick — and I decided the numbers didn't matter, because if the ship came apart going through that relay, infection wasn't going to be the thing that got me. That's not bravery. That's an engineer accepting a variable she can't fix.

I never told you, at the time, what it did to something in me to learn what the Collectors actually were. Insectoid, we called them, because that's what they looked like — but Mordin worked out the truth from their blood chemistry, and it turned my stomach worse than the husks ever did. Quad-strand DNA. Three fewer chromosomes than they should have had. They were Protheans, Shepard — the empire that ran this galaxy fifty thousand years ago, cultivated the asari, seeded us all somehow, and got harvested to the last living one by the Reapers when their cycle ended. The Reapers didn't just kill them. They kept some, and rewrote them — stripped out the glands, the digestive system, whatever made a Prothean a person instead of a tool — until what came back through that relay to take our people wasn't a species anymore, it was equipment wearing a species' shape. Mordin said they had no culture, no art, no instinct to save their own lives. I keep thinking about that. An entire civilization, reduced to something that doesn't even fight to live. My people lost Rannoch and turned into refugees. Theirs lost everything and got turned into that.
