# Liara — Mass Effect (Part 2 of 3)

## The Goddess Was a Guise

I said I would meet the oravores twice, and this is the second time, and I am afraid it is rather less comfortable than the first, because this time they are standing in my own house.

Consider what I told you. A spacefaring people, contemporary with the empire, who came for Thessia because Thessia is rich: element zero in the soil, in the water, in the very bodies of the animals, which is why so much of the life there leans biotic. And the Protheans drove them off. Now take that sentence and hold it up beside the thing every asari child is taught before she can read properly. A goddess, Athame, who protected the world from jealous rival gods, and who carried a sword. I had that story at my mother's knee. I had the sword in a glass case, in a temple, on a school visit, with a docent explaining the shine. And the record, such as I trust it, says that the jealous gods were the oravores, and the sword was a Prothean defence of Thessia, remembered as divinity. Ha. I have read that sentence perhaps two hundred times and I still feel the little drop in the stomach one gets on a staircase with a step missing.

I should be careful here, in the way I try to be careful. What I am giving you is an account, assembled from the Prothean record and from what Javik let fall, and from the second-hand digests that a person in my position is obliged to cross-read. I did not dig it out of the ground myself. Or rather, I did, in a manner of speaking, and I will come to that. But let me lay out the claim first, plainly, and then you can watch me flinch.

The claim is that the Protheans found us. At the height of the empire they looked at the primitive peoples, the asari, the hanar, the humans, the turians, and weighed them, and they judged that the asari might make leaders for the next cycle against the Reapers. So they invented a goddess. Athame, matriarch of the pantheon, maiden, matron and matriarch in one, which is, I might note, uncomfortably tidy as a flattery of a species with exactly three life stages. And she came with two guides. Janiri taught agriculture and the seasons. Lucen taught the stars. Athame herself taught mathematics and medicine. And Janiri and Lucen were Protheans. Two ordinary Protheans, walking about on my world, being worshipped. I think of them sometimes, in rather ridiculous detail. Did one of them mind it? Did they take turns?

And there is a thing the myth keeps, the way a wall keeps the shape of a door that was bricked up. In the stories Lucen begs for pottery and forging and biotics, and later tries to steal prophecy out of Athame's silver jars, and is struck down. A teacher who reaches for the one gift that was not hers to give, and is punished for it. I do not know what to make of that. A stern editorial, perhaps, from whoever wrote the legend down, or the memory of an actual dispute among the ones who were playing the gods. I flag it as speculation, and you will have noticed I have flagged it quite loudly.

Now the part that matters to me personally, and that I have never said aloud in this order. Biotics. The gift. The thing every asari is born with, and which we carry about as though it were a feature of the species, like the thousand years, like being so sure of ourselves. The summary I trust says it was the product of years of Prothean genetic manipulation. Not luck. Not a lucky world. A project. I have used my biotics to throw men through walls, Shepard, you have seen it, and I confess I have since thought about whose hands arranged the nerves that let me. I think of Harbinger's list, which you described to me, from the Collector base, when it went through the squad species by species: the asari, it said, relied on other species to reproduce, and that was a genetic weakness. I had put that down to the usual contempt. I am less sure. It is rather a pointed thing to say about a people who were, on this account, redrafted once already.

And here is what the whole business does to the pride. We are a people who say we rose on our own merit. Diplomacy, the long view, the Council, we helped found it, we found the Citadel with the keepers' assistance, as we say, and two thousand years of looking down on everyone else, kindly. Every one of those proud things has a layer under it. It is stratigraphy, in the end. You put your trowel in at the level of the Republics and you reach the Council, and under the Council you reach the Temple, and under the Temple you find a Prothean beacon in a statue. My species' self-esteem is a matter of what is under which floor.

Which is where I grew up, you understand. I was born on Thessia, a few miles of green and noise from that temple, and every child goes. It is a monument, with the reverent air that monuments have, and meanwhile it was also an excavation, a working one, kept shut behind the pilgrims' backs. I walked past that statue as a girl. I suppose I did what a very serious child does with a statue, which is to decide it looks like someone she knows. I did not know I was standing beside the thing I would give my life to find. Eden Prime, Therum, the whole of fifty years of reading: and the real one was a short walk from my school.

And it was a secret. This is the piece I find hardest, and it is not the beacon, it is the secrecy. After the Council was founded, hiding Prothean technology became illegal. The very law the asari wrote, or helped write, and pressed on others, with a lecture. And the highest ranks of our government went on keeping it, century after century, as a state secret, against the Council's own rule, because to reveal it would have told the galaxy that we were not as clever as we had let them believe. I was a doctor of Prothean anthropology. Fifty years. And I did not know. The person whose whole discipline was the dead empire was kept in the dark about the dead empire's single best-preserved remnant, on her own planet, by her own people. I have thought about that rather more than is good for me. Whether it was kept from me, or from everyone, which is the kinder reading, and which I do not entirely believe.

You will have guessed where this goes. You were there for the next part, and so I will say less than I might, and say it carefully.

The Councillor asked for you, in the Udina office, and told you what I think she had known for a very long time. A Prothean artefact, in the Temple of Athame, perhaps the key to the Catalyst, and the Reapers already descending on the Republics in numbers. I insisted on coming. I said I would explain the temple on the way, and I did, and I explained it, I think, with the confidence of a person describing her own house. Javik came as well. He had a good deal to say about the statuary, and I did not enjoy it, and he was, to my irritation, right about most of it.

We landed in a war. I do not mean the sort one reads about. I mean the commandos were holding a barricade with a turret and a biotic sealing a breach, and a lieutenant named Kurin, a good officer, I should say, pulled her people out against her orders, and I know that you have wondered whether that was the wrong choice, and I have thought of it and cannot find it in me to call it wrong. Past that, the ruins, a Reaper the size of a district walking through them, so close that I could count what it was doing. And then the temple, and the scientists dead beside the statue with their throats cut, which was not Reaper work, as you noticed before I did.

You worked it out standing there. I watched you do it. The statue, the beacon in the statue, and why the asari had flown ahead of everyone for two thousand years. You had the Cipher in your head, and it knew the beacon was there before either of us had said so. We woke it with the artefacts, the mural, the codex, Janiri's bust, the shield, and the statue came apart, and the Prothean inside it was Vendetta. A VI, I should say, not a person, made from the impressions of Pashek Vran, who had overseen the Prothean Crucible. And Vendetta said the Crucible was never theirs, that it had been handed down through cycles, and that their attempt had been spoiled by indoctrinated hands. And then it said that there was somebody in the room it did not like.

Kai Leng. The scientists were his. He had a drone with the Illusive Man in it, who spoke at length about control, and I will tell you I found that rather less offensive than he was hoping, because I was still looking at the wall where the statue had been. Then he fought you. You drove him back three times, and I want that remembered. And the gunship brought the floor down on us, and he walked through the dust, and took Vendetta, and took what it knew about the Catalyst, and went. We were told later, from the thing itself, at Cronos, by you, that the Catalyst was the Citadel. I was not there for that. I had it from you in the cargo hold, and from my own people, who found Leng's trail before the Normandy's did, though I will not claim that mattered. The temple was gone. Everything in it was gone, the beacon's housing, the murals, Athame's sword.

And on the comm, afterwards, you listened to Kurin's soldiers be overwhelmed. I did not need to hear that.

I will not give you a eulogy. I have tried to write one, in several drafts, and they were all the same draft, in which my voice became beautiful and the world did not. What I have is this. The asari had told the galaxy for two thousand years that they were the cleverest people in it, and when it counted they admitted, in a room full of corpses, that they had been standing on a theft. And then Thessia fell, with a casualty count I will not recite, because a number is a way of closing a door. And the thing that took the data was a man with an Alliance-trained body and indoctrination in his head, working for another man who called it a gift to humanity. You went and told the Councillor yourself. She cut the channel. I sat for a while afterwards, in the observation lounge, and nobody tried to make me talk, which I count among the great kindnesses of that crew.

You went on to Horizon, to the thing they called Sanctuary, and then Cerberus headquarters, because that is where the trail led, and I went with you, and I was angry, and I held it as level as I could, like a tray.

But I want to leave one thing with you. The goddess was a guise, and the guise worked: a people believed in her so completely that they said her name when they swore, every day, for thousands of years, long after they had stopped believing in anything else. And that makes me wonder about the others. I have a species in my notes who worship the Protheans by a different name entirely, and I have been avoiding them, because I suspect they got a much better deal than we did.

## What Everyone Else Made of Them

The hanar, then. I told you I had been avoiding them, and I should explain why, because it is not what you think. It is not that I find them difficult. It is that they are the one species who looked at the Protheans and came away with a better theory of gratitude than the rest of us had, and I find that annoying in a way I have not entirely examined.

Consider what each of the three of us did with the same vanished people. The asari made a goddess, and then forgot she had been made. The hanar made Enkindlers, and have never once forgotten it, to the point where they put the name in every second sentence. And the humans, whom the Protheans had been watching from a research station on Mars when the humans were still, I believe, painting on caves, spent a century working out what to do with the news and have not entirely finished. Three afterlives for one dead empire. Hanar, in the middle, are the ones I trust.

Not their theology. I will say that now, as plainly as I can. The Enkindlers gave the hanar the gift of speech, and sentience with it, and the whole species is an act of thanks for that, and I doubt it, the way I doubt most things that are tidy. But set the doubt aside, because the hanar did something the asari never did. They kept the ruins. Kahje is dotted with Prothean sites, and one of the oldest sits at the foot of an underwater volcano, Mount Vassla, and every year at their festival, the thirteen days of Nyahir, the winners of the poetry duels and the debates have their names written on the mountain in light, living light, for a year, and then it fades and they write new ones. Do you see what that is? It is a community that has been in continuous conversation with a ruin for tens of thousands of years. I would trade a good part of my files for one of those memories. When a hanar says the Enkindlers did a thing, I do not believe the doing, but I believe that someone, once, found something, and handed it down in a form that survived. That is data. It is badly preserved data, wrapped in a great deal of courtesy, but I have worked with worse.

I should also confess something about my other occupation, since the subject seems to invite it. Among the odd little reports that came across my desk in the days I was learning the work, I recall a line about the soul name of the leader of their Enkindler religion, a thing no outsider is meant to have. I bought it, naturally. I have never used it. It sits in a drawer, like a good pen one does not want to waste.

And you, of course, you met a hanar in the plainest possible way, which is what I wanted to tell you. Do you remember the Presidium, before any of the rest? The one near the Wards access, arguing with a C-Sec officer? I was there. I was beside you, and I think Garrus was muttering about permits from somewhere behind my shoulder, though he may have been somewhere else entirely, I have been wrong about Garrus's whereabouts before. A turian officer, very sure of himself, and a hanar who would not be rattled, and who explained, with perfect courtesy, that telling the truth is not preaching and therefore wants no permit. I loved it. I was so fresh from decades of reading about the Protheans that I had never once heard a person speak of them as a living subject at a public railing. And you stepped in, because you always do, and I have the shape of what you did but not the end of it. A permit bought? The officer talked round? The hanar persuaded to take its good news elsewhere? I believe the hanar went off peaceably in the end, but I would not swear to how. What I remember is that you spoke to both of them as if each had something you wanted to hear, and I thought, that is a person who will make an excellent diplomat or a very bad Spectre, and I was right on both counts.

Now. The harder thing about the hanar, and I will go carefully. There is a reason I take them seriously, and a reason I did not at first. When the news came out that the relays were not the Protheans' work at all, the hanar mostly reacted with shock and denial, which I understood. Their leader, the one they call All-Bright Olos, put out a very long address, thirteen minutes, I believe, and it said what I told you earlier: that the Enkindlers may themselves have been enkindled. I have never heard a better answer. It was not an evasion of the evidence. It took the evidence and made the faith larger. I think a great many asari, who had to absorb the same revision about our own goddess, took rather worse care of it.

There are others, naturally. A prophet called Kholai, whose followers hold that the Enkindlers made a mistake in lifting up living things, and that the galaxy should simply be allowed to rot, with nobody lifting a finger. The hanar government objected, and I admit I was relieved. A faith that takes the dead seriously is one thing. A faith that takes their despair seriously is another, and the second is where Zymandis comes in.

I got this one second hand, from the Broker's side, and then from you, so allow me to mark the seams. A salarian named Bau, a Spectre, and a man of the Special Tasks Group, which in my experience is a useful thing to be in a crisis, had come across intelligence that some very senior hanar had been indoctrinated. He wrote to you. There had been an Alliance raid on a batarian laboratory, with the hanar quietly helping, and they were after Reaper technology the batarians had been studying. The Alliance came away with nothing. Some of the hanar, it seemed, came away with something, and it was not a gift. Bau had the soul names of the people involved, and he had them either by paying off Donovan Hock or from an anonymous present he said was Kasumi's, because he admired her enormously, and I will say only that I would like to know which, and that I am glad of it either way. He needed you for your Spectre access, and you gave it. Two of the suspects were innocent. The third was the diplomat at the embassies, whose soul name, I am told, was Regards the Works of the Enkindlers in Despair. Someone had a sense of humour. It is a rather neat line from an English poem about a ruined statue, and I suspect whoever chose it was enjoying themselves.

His reasoning was almost elegant, which is why I cannot stop turning it over. The Protheans became the Collectors. The Collectors serve the Reapers. Therefore the hanar, who are descended from the Protheans' attention, ought to serve them as well. It is a syllogism. It has no flaw except that every premise is the thing the indoctrinated would want to say. Zymandis had a virus ready, to cripple the planetary defences of Kahje, and he said so, in front of you, quite calmly, which is a thing indoctrination does to a courtier. And the part I was told, which I believe: Kasumi was there, and she decloaked and stopped the upload herself, and the console went up with her. Apparently. I have a Broker's habit of declining to believe in a body I have not seen. Bau shot Zymandis. Bau lived. Kahje held, and so did its fleet, and the drell fleet with it, and Bau went away to talk the other Spectres into taking us seriously, and I suspect that some of them did.

Mark what that means. In the end the sharpest threat to the people who revere the Protheans was a Prothean reading, a bad one, taken to its end.

Which brings me, rather abruptly, to the humans. I said I would, so allow me to do it quickly and let you correct me, since you were born among them. In 2148 a mining crew on Mars dug into something, and within a year the eighteen largest nations on Earth had signed the Alliance charter. And religion, as the record has it, came apart in every direction at once. There were new faiths overnight, the interventionary evolutionists, who said the ruins proved that someone had steered all of human history. The old churches tried to fit extraterrestrial life into the doctrine, or kept silent, or insisted it was a conspiracy. I find that fascinating, and a little unfair to put you on the spot with, because I know the sort of church you grew up near, which is to say none. But I think of those congregations. The Protheans had come, in a sense, to their door, and the door had been shut for fifty thousand years, and what do you say? The answer, as nearly as I can tell, was a hundred different things, none of which was ever resolved. I have a certain tenderness for that. The asari had a goddess. The hanar had gratitude. Humans had a debate, and I think the debate was probably the healthiest of the three.

But there is one more misreading, and it is mine, so I should own it. The keepers. Do you know, for years I believed they were made as caretakers? The Citadel's Prothean gardeners, bred to tend the station, and left behind when their masters were gone, patient, faithful, wonderfully dull. Every codex said so, in the same sentence, as if the sentence were load-bearing. I was a graduate student, I read it, I believed it, and it was a rather comforting thing, a species of mute insects keeping the lights on out of loyalty. And I think that is exactly why nobody examined them. Nobody likes to interrogate the staff.

There was a salarian, Chorban, who did, with a scanner he and a volus friend had taken from the company they worked for, and who sent round a summary afterwards that nobody would read. The keepers, he said, were engineered, millions of years ago, by the same hand that made Sovereign. They were tuned to a signal that comes about every fifty thousand years. And the last time it came was when the Protheans vanished. He said, in the plainest language, that the Citadel might not be a Prothean thing at all. Nobody on the Citadel took him seriously. I did not take him seriously, and I had no excuse. I was in my office, working out where a Prothean beacon might sit, and a man with a stolen scanner had already found the ground under it.

So the keepers were never caretakers. They were bait, or the nearest thing to bait that can be done with insects: they kept the Citadel pleasant, so that every cycle there would be a gathering of the young peoples, all of them in one place, when the signal came. The Protheans found out. They altered the signal, I am told, so that the keepers would not answer it, too late to save themselves, and the keepers have gone on tending the station ever since, with no more sense of who they serve than the rest of us. I used to watch them on the Presidium, doing their rounds, rearranging offices overnight, and think, how restful. And I wish I could be that wrong about something smaller.

Which leaves the one place where the Protheans did not leave a guise or a faith or a plausible-looking machine, but something meant to be read. The cache on Mars. I have been circling it for an hour, and I think it is time.

## The Cache Under Mars

I should start with how small it was. Everyone who tells this story tells it as a cathedral, and it was not. It was an observation post. A ruined, buried, rather modest observation post, with a data cache that the Alliance's own histories call fragmentary, and a mass effect core that was still, after fifty thousand years, trying to run. That is the first thing I want you to hold on to, because it is the thing I could not stop thinking about when I first read the survey: it was still trying. A little engine, under the sand, turning over and over and failing, and you could feel it from orbit.

Humans noticed it before they understood it, which is how it usually goes. Satellites over Promethei Planum, near the south pole, kept recording magnetic shifts that nobody could explain, and the region got a reputation, a Bermuda Triangle, I believe the phrase was, which I have always loved, because it means that for some years the most important archaeological site in human history was a place pilots avoided. Then in 2147 someone found element zero on Mars, for the first time, anywhere in your system. And a year later a prospecting team led by Mateus Silva, working near the Deseado Crater, dug into the cause. A malfunctioning core. Several derelict starships. Stores of refined element zero, already refined, which is the detail I would like to put in front of every person who has ever complained about the cost of fuel. Someone had left them a head start.

I was seventy-one, by the way. I mention it because you were not born yet, Shepard, and I find I keep forgetting that, and that the thing I am describing happened to your species when mine would have called me a child. I remember the news arriving on Thessia the way news arrives about a neighbour's wedding. Interesting. Not mine. I did not yet know that I would spend my life in the shadow of exactly this kind of find.

Now, the translation. This was a global effort, and I admire it, frankly. Petabytes, the sources say, of a language nobody had a word of, and they worked it out, mostly, and what came back was this: a biosciences station, an observation post, set up roughly fifty thousand years earlier, to study Cro-Magnon humans on Earth. Not to rule them, not to feed them or fear them. To watch. The Protheans had a platform in Earth orbit and another on the Moon, as I understand it, and this was the place that received and processed what they gathered. What the observers concluded, why they cared, what they wanted from you, none of that survived. I have asked my own people to look. I have asked, and I paid for the asking, and the answer from every direction was the same. The station knows what it saw and says almost nothing about what it thought.

And this is where you come in, and I would like to be careful, because you told me this and I will probably garble it. The sphere on Eletania. The Consort gave you a trinket, on the Citadel, a small Prothean thing, a gift, and you carried it out to Eletania, to a ruin there, and put it in a slot on the underside of a floating sphere, and it knocked you flat, and you saw, for a moment, through a hunter's eyes. A man in furs, in cold, a spear in his hand, a silver shape hanging in the sky above him like a cloud, and a lump at the back of his skull where something had been put. You told me you woke wondering what they learned from you. From us, I mean, from them. I remember that you said it quite lightly, and I remember that I did not take it lightly, because that was the second witness. Mars gave the Alliance a station and a database. Eletania gave you an eye. Two entirely independent sites, a world and a half apart, telling the same fact in different dialects, and I thought, that is how one builds a case. One site is an anecdote. Two sites, and one of them in somebody's memory, is the beginning of a stratigraphy.

Hm. The vertigo, though. I have been putting it off.

Consider what it is to learn that you were catalogued. That before your people had a written word, before there was a language to be catalogued in, someone with better instruments was standing over your ancestors taking notes. I have thought about this a great deal, and I have not decided whether it is a violation or a compliment. The humans did not decide either. The sources are clear that religions came apart over it, and some stitched it into doctrine, and some insisted it was a hoax, and one new movement said that every single thing humans had ever done had been steered. I do not believe that. I should say so. But I understand the appeal, because the alternative is the thing I actually feel, which is smaller and worse: that you were studied, and then left alone, and that nobody can say whether the leaving was kindness or mere finishing of a project.

Then the practical consequences, because Mars was never only a feeling. The cache, fragmentary as it was, advanced human science by an estimated two hundred years. I want to underline the number, because I could not make it sit properly when I first read it. Two centuries. They had mass effect physics, a working picture of the fields, and the beginnings of the drives, and they got all of it from a small buried post and a stack of broken records. And it told them something else, which I think about even more. It told them that Charon, which they had been calling the largest moon of Pluto for as long as they had been calling anything anything, was not a moon. It was a relay. Dormant, wrapped in ice that the Alliance's records put at hundreds of kilometres thick, for fifty thousand years. A mass relay, sitting at the edge of your own solar system, disguised as a rock, and your astronomers had named it after a ferryman of the dead. I did not choose that name. I will never forgive whoever did.

They dug it out. This is the part I enjoy most and understand least, because the Alliance team admitted they did not understand the theory, and then discovered that switching it on was almost insultingly simple, as if the relay had been waiting to be found, which is, I want you to hear me say, exactly the kind of sentence that should make an archaeologist's skin crawl. Probes went through and went silent. So they sent people. Grissom, a rear admiral, a sullen, retiring man who never wanted any of it, took a ship through to Arcturus, and the face of your Alliance became the face of someone who would have preferred to be left alone. I find that rather moving. Pluto's orbit, I read, has been slowly circularised by the relay's operation, which means the thing you uncovered has been quietly reshaping the planet it sits beside ever since. Archaeology is not supposed to do that to the site.

Which brings me to my quarrel, and I would like you to argue with me, because I have not decided. How much of what humans did next was borrowed, and how much did you earn? One side of me, the stern one, the one that sits in a lecture hall, says: you were handed two hundred years in a decade, and everything afterwards stands on that, and the proper word is inheritance. The other side, the one that has watched you for years now, says that a cache is only a stack of fragments, and that half the species in the galaxy has had Prothean ruins at their feet for millennia and sold them, or worshipped them, or let them rot under a mining camp. You had a broken post and a Charon, and within a year you had gathered eighteen of the largest nations on your world into a single charter, and in a decade you were on the Citadel's doorstep. That is not borrowed. That is a species that heard a door open and walked through it, rather fast, and without asking.

I think the answer is both, and I dislike answers that are both. I will leave it there.

As for what the Alliance did with the place, I will say it once. You pooled your armies and your science, and that was well done. You made the south pole of Mars a restricted preserve, which I approve of, in principle, and you left a mining operation sitting on the top of it, which I do not, and I will not be lectured on the economics. And a good deal of the refined element zero was never secured. It went to the triads who ran Mars, and the triads, being practical, turned it into red sand, which may or may not be named for the planet. So the same cache that gave your species the stars gave a number of your people a very efficient way of forgetting them. You will have your own opinions on that, I imagine, from rather closer up than mine.

I went back, you know. In 2186, to the dig, under rather worse circumstances, and you found me there. It was not the same place. There were Cerberus troops, and the Reapers coming, and I was standing in a room where fifty thousand years earlier someone had been quietly watching your ancestors, trying not to think about how I would feel if it turned out they had been watching us too.

And I did think about it, afterwards. About what it means to leave a record that is not for the people who find it. The cache was not written for humans. It was a working file, a station's notes. Which is the thing I would like to talk about next, because there is a very particular sort of message that is meant for someone, only not for you.

## A Message Meant for Someone Else's Mind

Mail addressed to a stranger. That is the simplest way I know to put it, and then I will immediately complicate it, because you are going to ask me what is so hard about reading someone else's mail, and the answer is that the envelope is the easy part.

Start with what a beacon is, since people still say "recording" and that is wrong, and it is wrong in a way that matters. A recording is played to you. A beacon is played into you. As far as I can tell from the Alliance's own paleotechnology committee, which was rather good on this once it stopped being embarrassed, the thing holds its subject still with a mass effect field, scans the optic nerves and the auditory nerves and the regions of the brain that keep memory, and then stimulates them directly, with very fine energy, so that you do not watch the images so much as remember them. Remember a thing that never happened to you. The medical officer on your ship, Chakwas, said it delivered in seconds what a simstim would need hours for, to a species whose physiology its builders had never seen. I find that sentence unbearable, and I mean it as praise. They built something that would write into a stranger's head on a guess.

And it is a network node. Do hold on to that, because it changes the whole picture. These were not curiosities left on hilltops; they were points on a system, a kind of galactic extranet, near-instant, running between worlds of an empire. So when you stood in front of the one on Eden Prime, you were not looking at a monument. You were standing at the end of a wire.

Now, I was not there. I want to be exact about my sources, so: the beacon part is yours, mostly, told to me in pieces and never in order, and the rest I rebuilt afterwards from Alliance reports and from my own people, who were quite thorough once they knew it mattered. A shakedown run, which it was not. Nihlus and Anderson telling you so in the comm room, with the dreadnought already hanging over the colony in the footage, and Nihlus going ahead alone, and Saren shooting him in the back near the spaceport, a friend, a fellow Spectre. Jenkins dead in the first minutes to the drones. The husks on those spikes, and I will say that I did not enjoy having that one described to me. Ashley Williams, the only one left of her unit, and a dockworker called Powell by Nihlus's body. The beacon moved from the dig to the spaceport, the tram, the charges you disarmed, and then Kaidan and Ashley walking up to it, and one of them being drawn in, and you throwing them clear and taking their place. You told me that part rather lightly. I did not take it lightly.

Hm. And it is worth saying what it cost, since the geth did sack the colony, and there were hundreds of civilians, and the beacon was by then a wreck anyway. It is why you saw what you saw. Whatever the Protheans on Ilos meant it to be, an orderly warning, a message of hope sent out in the dark to anyone left, what arrived in you was fragments. Fifteen hours unconscious, and nightmares for a long time after. You woke in the med bay with Chakwas fretting, you told Anderson what you had seen, machines slaughtering the living, and the ship turned for the Citadel. And what you had, from that, was evidence against Saren, and the beginning of a hearing nobody wanted to have.

But the question I care about is the reverse one. Why was it unreadable? And here I have to confess to the name.

I called it the cipher. After Jung, I am afraid, and a little too proud of it at the time. The argument is that a Prothean message is not made of images alone; it assumes an enormous amount before it begins. What a figure in a doorway means. What a shape on a horizon feels like to a body with four eyes and a hand of three fingers. What counts as a threat, as a home, as a family, without anyone needing to say. Archetypes, instincts, a shared inheritance. Anything you could say to a Prothean in a moment, they would hear in the room of all the things they already knew. And I do not mean they were being clever. They coded the thing that way deliberately, so that only a Prothean mind could take it, because the Reapers might be listening, and the people on Ilos did not yet understand indoctrination. They did not imagine the one reader who might be a Reaper's creature and still, somehow, understand.

That is the hardest problem in my field. I would put it ahead of dating, ahead of translation. How do you find the context a message assumes when everyone who held that context is dead? A language you can reconstruct, with patience and a good deal of luck. A shared sense of what a doorway is? You cannot dig for it. And it was exactly the thing I had told you could not be had, that it might be impossible. I said so, I believe, rather confidently, on the Normandy, with Kaidan looking at me sideways. I had not met the Thorian yet.

I should say, I was on Feros with you, so this part is mine. I have said I was thinking of getting out. That was true of the lair; it was not true of the colony. Zhu's Hope was something else: the people of that place, Fai Dan among them, moving as if they were one thing, and a man I had spoken to an hour before looking through me. ExoGeni had been running them as a control group, and one representative, Jeong, had known and let it go on. You used the gas; you would not have them killed. Fai Dan, forced to turn on you, shot himself instead, and I have not decided what I think about that. And then the thing in the lair, and the green asari it pushed out to speak for it, wearing a commando's uniform, which is a detail I still find more disturbing than the rest. It could not talk except through them. It had been on Feros long before the Protheans, sleeping for thousands of years and waking for a few frantic centuries, and when they built their city over it, it watched. It took some of them as thralls. It took some of them when they died. And it kept what it ate. Fifty thousand years, a plant at the bottom of a skyscraper, holding the inside of the Protheans' minds.

Shiala, you know. She had been Benezia's acolyte for close to two centuries, and Saren had given her to the thing in exchange for the cipher, and had it from her, and then tried to destroy the Thorian so that you could not follow. He nearly managed it. After you cut the thing down she came out of that pod still herself, and the first thing she did, I remember, was to be sorry about its dying. A unique life, she said, more or less. I did not know what to do with that either. Then she touched your mind, and you came back from it rather shaken, and she told you it would take time.

It did. I want to be honest about the part I am least proud of, which is that I wanted it. I had spent decades on this subject, and here was the one living person who could read the vision, and my first thought, the moment I knew, was not for you. It was: tell me what you see. And you did, as well as you could, and I sat on the edge of that bed, and some of it was colour explained to someone with no eyes, which is the phrase I have always used for it, and some of it was words I knew. I could read the language, now that you could hear it. We were the two halves of an interpreter. And I will say, since I am confessing, that I had touched your mind before, in the meld, and it tired me out, more than anyone else's had; you resist, as humans do, and you resist harder than most, and I have wondered since whether the beacon was not overloaded, whether you simply would not let it in.

So there is the part I cannot make comfortable. A key to a dead people's minds came through a person. One person, at a time, at a cost to that person, and the one other who had it was Saren. A cipher is not a lock that opens for the righteous. It opened for him, and it opened for you, and that is the thing I dislike most about it. Later it let you read Vigil's broken warning on Ilos, and in 2186 it let you watch what Javik's footage showed while I saw only static, and you told me what the pods were doing, and the lifeboats. I was standing beside you and, as I said, saw only noise. I have not entirely forgiven that, either of us.

Shiala wrote to you in the war, you know. She had stayed, and the colony with her, every last one of them; you remember Jeong came round. She said the spores had left them able to feel one another, and that it helped, in a fight. Which is a Thorian's gift to a people it had enslaved, and I cannot say whether that is a mercy or the same hand. I am no longer sure there is a difference worth the argument.

What I wanted to say, though, is that the beacon was not the only message of its kind. There is one I have been circling. A message for someone, in a place that would not let anyone else hear it.

## Cut Out of a Wall

Ilos, yes. I will come to Ilos. But I have just spent a quarter of an hour on messages that would not open for the wrong reader, and I realise I have left out the one place where I was the thing that would not open. Or rather, the thing that could not get out. You know where I am going. Do not smile yet; give me the paragraph first, and then you may smile.

Therum. I want to tell you about it as a dig, because that is what it was before it was a rescue, and nobody, you included, has ever asked me about the dig. They ask about the barrier. Everyone wants the barrier.

So. The planet is rich in heavy metals, which is the reason humanity cares about it at all; a good part of the manufacturing boom on Earth came out of that rock. The mining corporations had been through the surface for years. Their boreholes, though, were the best stratigraphy anyone could have wished for. Core samples, with fossils of simple silicon-based organisms in them, which is how we knew the world had been kinder once, and why the Protheans might have wanted it. I am aware of how that sounds. The people who gutted the site also drew me its cross-section, free, in the course of looking for something else. I used their cores. I used their shafts to get down. I should say that I did not thank them, and I do not think I would have, even now.

And the ruin itself, the tower, was the one thing in that system that had come through. Most of it had been looted before I arrived; that is the rule, not the exception. What was left was a structure that the Protheans built so well that lava had poured into it and it simply stood there. Ashley said as much, later, in her own words, about builders who built to last. She also dared Kaidan to spit over the side of the lift, I am told. I was not present for that part; I was occupied.

Now, the self-mockery, which I have been saving. The system is called Knossos. Named, probably, for the Minoan palace, the one with the labyrinth, and I did not notice the joke until a good while afterwards. An archaeologist goes down into a labyrinth after fifty years of reading about what lived there, hides from the geth, touches the wrong panel, and ends up suspended in a field of light unable to scratch her own nose. I had a doctorate. I had, I believe, a cup of tea going cold on the ledge above. And the thing that finally came for me was a human with a shotgun and a mining laser, which is, if you think about it, the exact profession I had spent my life resenting. The vandals cut me out. I had it in a footnote later, in my notes, and I crossed it out and wrote it again.

What did I think I was looking at, that morning? I thought I was in a research station, a place where the Protheans had been working on something. I would have defended it. Forgive me, I would have argued it with a student. What I know now is rather more humbling. I had no idea what I was in. I knew a barrier had gone up when I brushed the wrong control and the geth came through. I did not know that the ones on the other side of the wall had been, in effect, the same war I was studying, a few thousand years late. Zeioph crypts on Armeni, in the same system. Prothean presence on Therum. Two peoples, at least, who had kept something here, and I assumed the one I had come to study was the only one that mattered. My contacts say the crypts have been picked over too. I am not in a position to confirm it.

Then the tremor. I told you the laser caused it, in the moment, because I wanted to be useful and there was a tremor and a laser. I had no evidence. It is possible the old structure had simply been waiting. It is possible the geth had weakened it. I wrote it down as the laser, and the Council's turian Councilor asked you afterward why you had destroyed a Prothean site, and I let that sit on you rather longer than I should have. That is a debt, and I know it. A tower that had stood for fifty thousand years, which I had reached by lift, and I watched it come down running beside you.

My mother. I have been going around her. Benezia taught philosophy and religion, and she persuaded; that was what she was for, and she believed that if you were patient and clever, you could steer the strong. I went the other way. I went to the dead, who do not answer back. For years I told myself that archaeology was my escape from her, and now I am not so certain. We were doing the same thing. She read the living for what they would not say, and I read what the dead left in the walls. I will say that when Aethyta and I spoke, much later, she told me that Benezia had thought it all rather a joke, the Protheans, and then someone else said she had been protecting me, and I do not know which to believe. I would like to hold the second a little more tightly than the first.

And my theory. Cyclical extinction. I said it on your ship with great conviction, and Kaidan pressed me on whether I was Saren's, and Joker was sarcastic, and I was so very pleased with myself. What I would say now is that the evidence for the cycle was the absence of evidence. There was so little of the Protheans. I read that as someone erasing them. A rival of mine, a patient one, who lives entirely in my head and sounds rather like Dr. Joshawn correcting my dating, says: no. Nothing erased them. Time did, and miners, and looters. Fifty thousand years is plenty. You invented a villain to excuse a thin site. And then you, Shepard, arrived with the villain. I have never forgiven the universe for confirming me. It is deeply bad for the discipline.

Still. Notice how little of a Prothean site is Prothean. Therum: miners, geth, a Krogan Battlemaster who had no business in a seminar room. Kahje, later: a Cerberus Phantom had been there first, and killed the staff, and turned the defences on my shuttle. The Illusive Man denied it afterwards, which tells you what the denial was worth. The Protheans are the least of what is in the ground. The dig is mostly everyone who came after, and I have started to think that is the real subject.

Which is how I come to Ilos. A message left, finally, in a place that kept everyone out.
