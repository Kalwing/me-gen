You are still not going to tell me what you would have dated first, and I have decided to count that as a vote for the Alpha relay, since nobody has ever voted against it. Good. Put the dust away. I have one more thing, and it is the one I have been avoiding all evening by being rigorous at you for an hour, which is a technique I learned from the best scholars on Serrice and would not recommend.

It is the book.

You know about the book. Everyone on the ship knows about the book, because I have been sketching titles for it since before you died the first time, and Glyph has a folder, and Joker has twice asked it to read the list aloud in the cockpit in a voice I am told is not at all like mine. So. Let me be honest about the titles, because they are the most honest thing I have written.

The first one was "The Protheans." Just that. It is what a young woman with a doctorate and a great deal of confidence writes on a blank page, a title that is also a claim: here is the subject, it is closed, you may now be told about it. I rejected it because I had, by then, stood in a ruin on Therum that turned out to be mostly other people's looting, and the title had no room in it for that. Then "The Prothean Empire," which is better, and which I rejected the day Javik walked off a pod and informed me that the word I was using for it was too polite. Then "A Living Legacy," which I think I wrote on a very good day, and which I now find so embarrassing that I will say only this: I was fond of the word living, and I had not yet met anything that was. There is one on the list that simply reads, I Despise Thinking Up Titles For My Own Academic Research, and I left it in, because it is the only one that has never been wrong. And then, last, the one he made me write down. "Journeys with the Prothean." Mm. I want you to know I dislike that title. I dislike it in the particular way you dislike a thing that is correct. He has agreed to consider it, which from Javik is a binding contract, and I have agreed to consider it back, which from me is a sworn deposition.

What I did not expect, when I started the list, was that the title would keep changing because the subject kept changing. I had a species in mind, wise and patient, keepers of wisdom, and what I had on the table was a people who were frightened and good at it, who built an empire out of other people's fear and then spent their last centuries losing a war in a very organised fashion. I put that in my private notes, the ones the Broker's terminal keeps for me, and the entry I reread most is not the angry one. It is a short one, where I admit I had romanticised a whole species out of a few fragments and a great deal of longing, and that the fault was mine and not theirs. I would like the next reader to find that entry first. Before the dates, before the arithmetic, before anyone's quarrel with the inusannon.

Now. Here is what I have actually concluded, and I have been walking toward it all lecture.

Everything the Protheans left that is still standing is a thing that was meant to be found. Not preserved, found. That is a different kind of object. A beacon is a message in a form that a particular mind could receive, and they coded it so only a Prothean mind would understand it, because they did not want the Reapers reading it, which was sensible, and which also meant that when the message reached you it arrived as a handful of images and fifteen hours lying on a floor. I have told you that I once thought that was a mistake. It was a lock with the key in a drawer on Feros, and the drawer was Shiala, and a Thorian had to be dealt with before she opened it. Vigil sat in the dark under Ilos for fifty thousand years, patient to the point of insult, waiting for one visitor, and he is the clearest example I have: a mind built to be addressed. The cache under Mars was an observation post, a small room, a lot of data, no obvious grandeur, and it did more to the human species in a few decades than the whole of the rest of the Protheans' rubble did to anybody. Because it was found by people who could use it.

And then I think of everything on the other side of the ledger. The asari kept a beacon in a statue and told no one for the whole of our recorded history, including the Council that had made hiding it illegal, and it did us precisely as much good as a sealed letter does. It took Vendetta, and the temple, and a great deal going wrong, to make the thing speak. Vendetta sat in that statue among the pilgrims and said nothing to any of them until you walked in with the right artefacts. A record is not a pile of facts, it is an open door that has to be walked through by someone who has been taught how the door works. So if I leave anything, it will not be a very large archive. I will leave a short one, addressed to a particular stranger, with the key attached where they will trip on it.

I did that, as it happens. You know about the capsule. I sat down in the evenings, between operations, and tried to write it for somebody I could not picture, and I found I could not write for posterity at all. Posterity is a very bad audience. It does not lean forward. I wrote it for whoever would be sitting where I was sitting when I found the Mars cache, tired and a little frightened and holding an incomplete thing, and who would need to be told what we had actually been like. Not our dates. Us.

Which is where I owe you the part I have been dreading, because I want the next reader to know about this cycle, and about you, and I am going to say it badly, sideways, and with a joke at my own expense, since that is the only form in which I can say it at all.

I have the file on the Citadel. Of course I do. After the coup, after the party, when somebody had been wearing your face around the Wards. I have Glyph's account of the Archives, delivered in the tone of a small drone that would like it understood it was not the one who got locked in a vault, and I have Traynor's, which is shorter and a good deal more pointed, because it was Traynor the false orders were sent to, in your voice, to ready the ship for launch. The Spectre codes were a copy of a copy. Brooks, who had assembled the dossiers once, for the other mission, and had stopped believing in the man she worked for, had woken a thing that had been kept in a tank as spare parts, and let it learn to behave like a person from a set of implants. It thought itself the real one, freed of everything that makes you slow: the friends, the guilt, the people who wait. It shut you in iridium and went for the docks, and Glyph, who I will concede may be in the right about itself, let you out, and it was settled at the docks, where you tried to hold on to him and he let go. That is as far as I have it. I was not in that room. I heard it afterwards, in pieces, from people who each said it differently, which is how I know it happened.

Here is the joke. I have spent fifty years on a record of a vanished people, and the entire time I believed that the thing I wanted was a more complete record. More fragments, more cipher, a bigger dig. And the clearest lesson I have ever been handed about records came from a man grown in a tank who had every one of your files, every code, every habit, the walk, the voice, the whole dossier. Perfect copy. Better preserved than anything I have ever pulled out of a wall. And he was nothing like you, because the part of you that mattered was not in the file. It was in what happens to you when somebody is in trouble. I am an archaeologist of some distinction, and I needed a clone to tell me that the artefact is not the point. I would like it recorded that I find this humiliating, and that I have put it in a footnote, and that the footnote has a footnote.

Which is what Javik meant, I think, though he would be appalled to hear me say he meant anything gentle. He said something of the sort once, in the tone he uses for the weather. I have lost the wording, which is unlike me; the sense was that a record means nothing unless someone is moved by it, and that his people had kept very good records. He was the proof, a perfectly good archive of a civilisation, standing in my cargo hold, complaining about the food. It was meant to be a cruelty. I decided to take it as an instruction, which is the only way anybody on this ship has ever gotten anything out of him.

So this is my answer, and then I will stop, because the lights are doing the thing that means we have overrun.

He is right that a record that moves no one is a very good way of being forgotten. But I do not think the moving is separate from the keeping. It is the keeping. Every Prothean thing that lasted was built to move someone, to be found by the one who could carry it, and the ones that moved nobody, the sealed ones, the withheld ones, are the ones that failed. So if the book is ever finished, it will not be a history of the Protheans. It will be a letter, in the way the beacon was a letter, to someone who is tired and halfway through something. It will be about what a cycle is like from inside, and the thing it will say is the one I could not get into any of the titles. That nobody here was certain. That we argued, and ate badly, and kept a drone named Glyph, and drank something Garrus called calibrations. That we were frightened and did it anyway, and that the person at the middle of it, whom I am addressing and who is looking very uncomfortable at being addressed, never once behaved as though he was in a record.

I think that might be the title, actually. I have never said that aloud. Do not tell Javik, he will want his name on the first line.

There. I have run over by twenty minutes. I had a better ending, and I will have it five minutes from now.
