Which is an uncomfortable thing to say, I realise, directly before I start talking about the one set of ruins that did tell us exactly what to do. I am going to be unfair to it, a little. I want to approach it through the Obelisk, because the Obelisk is where I first understood that we were no longer excavating anything.

You will remember the Obelisk of Karza, because you went and got it, off Kopis, after the Hades Nexus was open to anyone who did not mind the neighbourhood. I told you about the finding earlier, so I will not do the finding again. What I did not say is what it was for. A researcher on the Presidium wanted it, a man on a bench whose own team had run out of time, and I assumed, when I first heard, that he wanted it the way I would have wanted it at nineteen: as a Rosetta stone, a key to the language, the thing that lets a thousand datapads be read at last. And it was that. But the Alliance engineers who took it were not interested in poetry. They wanted the technical terms and the measurements, the words for units and tolerances, because they were trying to decrypt a blueprint, and a blueprint is written in the one dialect in which nobody writes anything beautiful. I have spent my life wanting the Protheans to speak, and when I finally got a text they had inscribed on purpose, it was a glossary for building a machine. I was thrilled and offended in roughly equal parts, which is, I notice, a recurring condition of mine.

The same goes for the sphere on Gei Hinnom, the one the refugee in the Docks, Bay E28 if I have the number right, begged a turian guard for food to hear about. You let him sell it to the Alliance; his family ate, and so did some people he did not know. I had a file on him. I have a file on everybody, it is a deformity. And I remember thinking that this was the first time in my career that a piece of Prothean material had a quantified urgency, a clock, and that I could not honestly say which mattered more to me, the man's dinner or the data. I would like to say the dinner. I believe I would have said the dinner. I would also have asked you, afterwards, what the sphere looked like.

And of course Kopis was not new to you. You had been there before, in the Cerberus year, with the Hammerhead, past the dead Blue Suns, and you had read what the expedition left behind: Cayce, O'Loy, the colony O'Loy sold the dig to protect, Cayce deciding that what was still in his head would not survive him. I did not witness any of that. I have the logs, and I cannot tell you on what date they reached me, which tells you how many things were reaching me. But I have wondered since whether anyone on that moon was digging for knowledge at all, or whether they were all being quietly used. That is the wartime question about every site I touched in those years. The dig is not the point. The dig is the thing someone else is waiting to take away.

So. The Crucible. Allow me to be careful, because I have been careful about every number so far and I am not going to stop at the one that matters.

Nobody knows who began it. That is the sentence I would put at the top of any paper, if they let me. The design is the work of countless species, over millions of years, each of which added something and none of which lived to finish. Every one of them was wiped out before the last piece. The Reapers believed all trace of it had been destroyed, and it was not, because it was passed on, secretly, from one cycle to the next, a kind of inheritance that no heir ever saw in full. The Protheans were the latest to try. They actually built the thing, which no one before them had, and then they lost it, not to the Reapers, not at first, but to an argument. One faction wanted to destroy the Reapers. The other wanted to control them, and I will say as plainly as I can that the second faction turned out to be indoctrinated. You know who else has argued that, in our own cycle, at length, with a very good lighting design.

Now, here is where I start to lose my footing, and I would rather you watch it happen. Millions of years. If a cycle is fifty thousand years, give or take, then millions of years is dozens of cycles, a staircase longer than anything I can see the bottom of. But the dates my sources give me for the older civilisations do not fit that arithmetic, and I will not pretend they do. Some of what I know about whoever came before the Protheans I know from pages I would call, very generously, unreliable. I have a source I trust, and a source I do not, and the two disagree about whether the cycles were even regular. So when I say countless species, I mean it as a confession of the count. I mean that I cannot give you the number, and I distrust anyone who can.

Which brings me to Mars, and I should admit what it did to my sense of the thing I was doing there.

The Mars archive was a dig. Humanity had been studying it for decades, and the people who designed the Alliance's base there, a low circle with a shaft down the middle, built to survive the sandstorms and looking, I am told, like a very clean factory, had clearly never been told it was a place anyone should be afraid of. I went because I thought it was a library. Kahje first, actually: the shrine the hanar keep under the water, with Quoyle wounded and sending me on ahead, and a set of Prothean encryption keys, and a hologram of the Illusive Man who denied sending the Phantom and offered me an alliance. I asked him to share what he knew first. He stalled, and I cut the link and told him to send an army next time, which I have been pleased with ever since, and which was, I am aware, a very small victory. Then I took the keys to Mars, to see what they opened.

They opened the plans. That was the discovery, and I knew it the moment I saw them, though I confess I did not understand them. They had been sitting there for fifty thousand years, under a planet that would one day belong to the people they were never going to meet. And what happened to the Mars dig, in my head, in the span of one afternoon, was that it stopped being an excavation. A library is what you find. This was what someone had left, deliberately, in a place they hoped would be found by a species that did not yet exist. I had gone there to dig. I had been, without knowing it, collecting a deposit.

You came, and then Eva Coré, whom the Illusive Man sent, a Cerberus mole, a woman who turned out to be an infiltration unit, and who wiped the archive as she took it. There was a chase. Vega put something into her shuttle, I have heard a truck, I have heard a shuttle, he will tell you whichever one is more flattering. You shot her down with the plans intact, and Kaidan paid for it, and went off to Huerta Memorial on the Citadel, and I have never quite forgiven myself for the ordinary, procedural way I noted it in my head that afternoon. Her body went to EDI, in time. I think about that more than I can reasonably defend.

I then stood in front of the Council, with the data, and watched the three of them decline to commit. They were not stupid. They had homeworlds under attack. But I remember thinking that every one of them was behaving exactly as the Protheans' separatists had behaved, only not indoctrinated, merely frightened, and that I could not tell the difference from the outside.

After that, Hackett. Earth fell, and he authorised the thing, every shipyard in the galaxy, each species building a section. Mordin wrote to a colleague, a message that came into my terminal, I read everything, to say that the Alliance engineers were following the Mars plans so slavishly that he doubted they needed half of the scaffolding. He was looking forward to correcting them. I kept that message longer than I kept most of my files. The scientists could tell that the device used mass relay technology and could not tell how it worked, and that it needed a final piece, the Catalyst. I had ExoGeni's people on Feros contributing, because the Feros ruins were exactly the sort of scrap the project could use, and I had been on Feros, with you, in a place that was supposed to have given up its secrets and had not. I had Conrad Verner's dissertation on dark energy and time, which helped the engineers read the Prothean instructions, and which I want you to know exists partly because you were patient with him.

Thessia gave us the rest. Vendetta, in the beacon, in the Temple of Athame: modelled on the overseer of the Prothean Crucible project, a man called Pashek Vran. He told you, and I heard it, that the Crucible was not Prothean in origin, that the pattern he saw across cycles was too regular to be accident, that the Reapers were its servants and not its authors. I will not claim to remember it as he said it. I remember that he said the cycle had reached its terminus, and that you told him to wait. Then Kai Leng took him, the Illusive Man broke what was in him, and learned that the Catalyst was the Citadel. He fled there. He told the Reapers. They moved the station to Earth. Hackett sent the fleets, and the Crucible followed behind a token defence force, to dock with it, and what it did there I will leave out, tonight. You were there. You do not need me to narrate it.

What I want to leave you with is the feeling. I have had it for months and I do not trust it. When I put the pieces side by side — a beacon on Eden Prime that fastened on you, a Cipher that made you readable to a beacon, a plan sitting for fifty thousand years under Mars, a VI whose maker had known to withhold the one thing that would get them found — it all arranges itself into a letter addressed to you. To this cycle. To us. And I have spent my whole life correcting people who read the past as a message to themselves. The hanar do it. The asari have done it, under a goddess. The Protheans did not write to you. They wrote to whoever came next, and you are merely who arrived. But I am not entirely able to make myself believe that, and I wonder, Shepard, whether you ever were.
