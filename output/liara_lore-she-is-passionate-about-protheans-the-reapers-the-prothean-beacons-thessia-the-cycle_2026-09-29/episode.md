## A Fragment Dated to 68,000

Sit down, Shepard. No, really, sit; this will take a while, and I would rather you were comfortable when I start being tedious. I want to begin with an object, because it is the only honest way I know to begin. A communication device on Fehl Prime. The Protheans left it in the ground, and it has been dated to as early as sixty-eight thousand years before the common era.

Now. "As early as." I should like you to hear those three words the way I hear them, because most people hear only the number. A date like that is a floor, not a birth certificate. It tells you the thing cannot be younger than the layer it was sitting in, and it tells you nothing about how long it had been in use before it was buried, or what anyone said into it, or to whom. You are holding a very old word and you have no idea what language it was in. Well. You, of all people, are in a position to laugh at that. The beacon on Eden Prime gave you a few seconds of fire and noise, and then it took the cipher Shiala gave you on Feros, which opened the language to you, and then, I will remind you, it still took me to tell you what half of it was about. Do not look at me like that, I am being fond about it.

What I am trying to say is that I trust a stratum more than I trust a story. A story is told by someone who wants it to end somewhere. Dust does not want anything. Do you remember Dr. Kenson, the one who sampled the dust trapped around the relays and compared it with the clouds in the same systems? She was the one who found that only a fraction of the relays could be fifty thousand years old, and that the rest were much older. Nobody doubted the relays were Prothean until somebody bothered to weigh the grit, and I think about that every time a colleague tells me what a ruin "obviously" means. I admired that work before I ever heard her name attached to anything else. I admired it a great deal.

And that is the difficulty, because I cannot say her name and stop there. I will tell you how I know the rest, since you were there and I was not. I had it first from you, in pieces, on a night when you did not want to give it to me, and I have since laid it beside my own files, the Alliance traffic and the batarian reports that came through the network, and weighed your account against them. They agree on the shape, which does not happen as often as you would think. Hackett came to you privately, asked you to go alone into a prison on Aratoht to bring her out, and you did. She told you on the shuttle what her team had found in the Bahak system: a relay older than the Protheans, the oldest anyone has found, and a Reaper artifact in the asteroids beside it that they called Object Rho. The visions it gave her were the kind the beacon gave you, and they told her the Reapers were only days away.

So she and her team did the arithmetic that I, honestly, am not sure I would have had the nerve to finish. Drive an asteroid into the relay. Make the Reapers go the long way round. Months or years bought, and I find the sources disagree about which, so I will not pick for them. And they knew what it would do to the system, three hundred thousand batarians on Aratoht alone, and they went on anyway. Then you reached the asteroid and found that the long exposure had indoctrinated every one of them, and the clock they were so frightened of had become a thing they wanted to arrive. She pointed a weapon at you. You broke her wrist, and she ordered her guards to kill you, and you woke after the sedatives with hours left and went through the base anyway. When she could no longer hear the whispers, as she put it, she blew herself apart, and you got the asteroid moving all the same. Do not correct me on the details; I am telling you what came back to me, and I know it has blurred.

And what I cannot do, Shepard, is arrive at one verdict. The dust is real. It is still true that she showed the galaxy it had been wrong about its own relays by the oldest method there is, patient and unglamorous. And the Bahak system is gone, the whole of it, a supernova's worth, and the Hegemony with it, so that a power vacuum opened in the middle of the galaxy that nobody had planned for. I have read what followed: the diplomatic damage, the batarians telling anyone who would listen that humans did it, which was not entirely a lie. Hackett warned you Earth would one day ask you to answer for it, did he not? And it was the same woman, and the same hands, and I will not let one cancel the other. The dust-work does not buy back the dead, and the dead do not make the dust any less true. If someone asks me to choose, I will tell them I am sorry, I am still working on it, and I will keep working on it.

You at least came away with something: the clock. After Bahak, you knew the Reapers were not a rumor with a coordinate, and that they had been slowed rather than stopped. I believe it is why you were so insistent in the years after, even when no one else listened.

Which brings me, rather crookedly, to ruins, and what they can and cannot say. Almost everything that stands was picked clean long ago. Generations of looters have been through the dead cities and derelict stations; what survives is a surprise, and what still functions is rare. I find that I am equal parts grateful and furious. The Fehl Prime device is one of the exceptions, intact and working, believed at least seventy thousand years old, and when I last checked nobody was actively studying it. A functioning interstellar communication node, on a pharmaceutical colony. I could weep. I nearly did.

My thin line to that place runs through a student of mine, Treeya, an anthropologist, a good one, a little too sure of herself in the way the good ones are. She reached me from the Prothean relay itself, over that ancient system, with a squad of Alliance marines around her. That part I heard myself, so I will give it to you as heard. They had found some device that was jamming the colony. One of the soldiers had destroyed it with biotics, to her annoyance, and she had kept a fragment. She asked what I thought it was, and I said, and I am fairly sure about this, that it might be Reaper technology. Then the link was cut, jammed I assume, though I am guessing at what happened on her end.

Everything after that I had from my network, not from the ground. A Collector ship arrived over the colony before the call was properly finished. Treeya was pulled onto it; she reportedly saw a Prothean archive inside it, and a record of the Protheans' last stand at that very device, and what the Reapers made of them afterwards. I do not hold that last part loosely or tightly. The source for it is a Cerberus recording that passed through three hands before it reached me, and Cerberus has never sent me anything without a use attached. The Alliance side is cleaner, a monument on the colony, the marine, Vega, commended for what he chose, and I trust that perhaps a little more than the rest. I gather she told the soldiers I was fixated on Reaper myths, and, well, I suppose I had it coming. I have not verified a word of the archive on the ground. I would rather say so than let it sound as if I had.

So, where can a person actually stand? That is the question I keep returning to, because "Prothean ruin" in a news report usually means a rumor with a coordinate attached. Feros: two-thirds of the habitable surface under a megatropolis, a dozen meters of fallen debris at ground level, aqueducts that still carry water, a colony perched on the upper levels of the skyscrapers that held. I was there with you, so I will say what I saw rather than what the codex says: it is smaller than it sounds from above, and larger the moment you are inside it, and the lair under it is the one place in all of that city where I did not think of it as archaeology at all. I was thinking of getting out. I will say only that much; you can add the rest, and I expect you will correct me. Quana, where the mining cities are reasonably intact, which sounds like good luck until you realize it only made the looting easier. And Therum, which I know in a way I would rather not, since the last time I was in a Prothean ruin on it I was inside a barrier and you had to cut me out. Even then, the mining corporations had worked over the surface long before either of us arrived.

Those are the places people can walk. The others are in the Attican Traverse, which is a polite way of saying the frontier, next to the Terminus Systems and under no one's protection. Known, charted, and mostly unopened, because every colony out there is raided and nobody wants to pay an archaeologist to dig under a pirate's flight path. There may be more beyond that. I try not to think about how much of the story is down there, simply waiting.

And the odd thing, the thing that has been bothering me since the dust, is the layer under the layer. Kenson said the Alpha Relay was older than anything the Protheans built, and she was apparently right about that too. If the relays were already old when the Protheans arrived, then whose city were they standing in? Stay with me. I will get to that.

## Whose Ruins They Dug Up

The answer, or the part of it that I am willing to call an answer, is that they were standing in someone else's ruins, and I confess it is the joke that has pleased me most in my entire career. The Protheans were archaeologists. Think about that for a moment, Shepard. I spent my youth, and quite a lot of my adulthood, kneeling in the dirt of a people who were gone, imagining them as keepers of wisdom and enlightenment, and the whole time those people were doing precisely what I was doing. They achieved spaceflight, they went out looking, they found the ruins of a species that had come before, and they dug. Everything we are taught to admire about them, the mass effect physics, the faster-than-light drive, the advantage they kept secret for centuries, came out of a hole in the ground that somebody else had left. Our whole discipline, Shepard, is a chain of people kneeling over the previous people's bones. I find that a great comfort, and I also find it faintly horrifying, and I have never managed to pick one.

The species they dug up is called the inusannon, and I want to be careful here, because the temptation to fill the silence is enormous and I have watched better scholars give in to it. This is what is known. They existed at least a hundred and twenty-seven thousand years ago. They were spacefaring. They fought a war with another people, the thoi'han, over the colonisation rights to a garden world called Eingana. They had been on Ilos at some point, and I know that one a little better than the rest, because Javik told me, aboard your ship, that Ilos was inusannon before it was Prothean. He said it the way you might mention that a house had previous tenants. I nearly dropped my datapad. And that is, more or less, the end of the list. The Reapers wiped them out, if you trust the sources I trust, and I should say the inusannon are the one subject where I cannot tell you whether my sources are good or merely confident.

Which is the part I keep circling. A hundred and twenty-seven thousand years, and the Protheans at sixty-eight thousand at the earliest, and then the fall at about forty-eight. Do the subtraction with me. It does not come out as a tidy fifty-thousand-year cycle; the numbers do not line up, and I have tried to make them. Either the dates are rough, which is entirely possible, or there are more layers than anyone is counting. I do not want to resolve it yet. I will say it again later, I expect, when I have more dust to point at.

Now, the relays. Everybody says the Protheans built them, and everybody has said so for so long that it is printed on school slates. I want you to notice how much of that was inference. A relay was found, the Protheans were the oldest people anybody could name, and so the relays became Prothean the way a lost glove becomes whoever walked past last. The Citadel, the same. Then Kenson weighed the dust, and I told you about that, and the relays turned out to be older than the people we had credited. You told me yourself what Sovereign said on the subject, that the Protheans were only one of many peoples to find the relays and the station, and that the whole network exists to make civilisations advance down particular roads. I did not hear it. I have it from you, and I have never had a reason to doubt you, but I will note that I am relying on a conversation with a machine that wanted you dead. So the Protheans inherited it. They did what we did. They found a miracle lying around and learned to use it, and they were so good at using it that history handed them the credit for the making.

Do you remember how the hanar took it? I was on the Presidium in those days, and I was struck by it, because it ran in the opposite direction to the lecture. The hanar, who call the Protheans the Enkindlers, were so distressed by the news about the relays that I thought one of them might actually turn colours in my arms. What stayed with me was the line of reasoning I heard afterwards from their religious leader, in a broadcast I tracked through my own channels. Something to the effect that it is no heresy to say the Enkindlers may themselves have been enkindled. There was a being before the being they worship, an even nobler one, and they decided that was a faster current to ride. I have thought about that more than I have thought about most sermons. It is a better response to the discovery of an older layer than I managed in my own first week of reading about it, when I mostly sulked.

Mars is the same shape, you realise. Humans dug up a Prothean station in 2148, a prospecting team near a crater, and out came a malfunctioning core, some starships, refined element zero, and a fragmentary cache that the translators say sped up human science by a couple of hundred years. Everything your species has done since sits on the Protheans, who sat on the inusannon, who sat on whoever built the thing that made all of them possible. It is a stack of people borrowing from the person under them. I do not think of it as a ladder. I think of it as a landslide that has been going on a very long time.

I would like to tell you about Kopis, because it is the one occasion where I can set a Prothean dig beside the people who dug it up. You will say you remember it better than I do, and you are right, so correct me. It was in the Hoplos system, a moon called Kopis orbiting Makhaira, out in the Hades Nexus. That was the last of those Project Firewalker assignments you ran for Cerberus, and I have read the reports they filed, and the logs, and you told me a little afterwards, in the way that you do, which is to say not very much. The data from Karumto had pointed Cerberus to the last place anyone had heard from Doctor Cayce, who had gone to excavate a Prothean site. The scans showed artifacts behind a kinetic barrier, with automated defences hidden around it. You took the Hammerhead. You could not shoot through the barrier, so, and I rather like this, you followed the power lines to the generators and took them out one by one, while Rocket Drones tried to take you out. Archaeology by sabotage. When the last generator went the barrier fell, and you walked in on foot, past the Blue Suns mercenaries the expedition had hired to guard it, who had not been guarded very well themselves.

And the logs told the end of it, and it is not a good one. One of Cayce's colleagues, a Doctor O'Loy, had been indoctrinated by the Reapers. He had been feeding the movements of the team to a geth ship, and he had made a bargain with the Collectors: the Prothean find in exchange for the safety of the colony where his wife lived. Cayce discovered the transmissions, killed him to prevent another Eden Prime, and then found he was no better himself. The Reapers were still in his head, he wrote, and he resolved to destroy the relic and himself together. You hacked the console, and the thing in the vault glowed green and shrank down to a size that could be carried, and I remember it. I mean I remember seeing it on the table in your cabin, on the SR-2, and I remember asking what it was and being told something vague, and I did not press. The artifact turned out to be inert, I gather, harmless. What was not harmless was an energy burst during the retrieval, which carried coded data that Cerberus claimed advanced its work on energy transfer and biotics. I would like to say that it did not matter. I do not know that it did not. The Hades Nexus is logged, in what I have, as a place where the Reapers and the Collectors were active, and that is the kind of thing that is only ever noticed afterwards.

I raise it because Cayce was an archaeologist, Shepard. He did what the Protheans did, and I did, and every one of us does: he found something under the ground that belonged to another people and thought he had the right to lift it. And the ground, in his case, was not neutral. I do not say that to excuse myself or to frighten myself. It is a thing I carry, in the plain sense of having to carry it, into every site I enter.

And that brings me, naturally, to the place I would most like to stand, which is Eingana. A hot, beautiful, deadly world, I am told, covered in the wreckage of ancient ships. The thoi'han and the inusannon lost hundreds of vessels over it and over its moon, and the planet's gravity drew the wrecks down, and the drive cores broke apart and spilled refined element zero across the landscape. It poisoned the world. A wave of extinctions, and the animals that remained are said to show a tendency towards biotics, which would make the place quite lively for a colonist, I imagine. I would give a great deal to see it. And I have to be honest about what I would find. A battlefield with no records. Nobody knows what the two sides said to each other, or whether either of them was kind, or who fired first. Most historians agree both of them wanted the world and neither would share it, and that is a guess about motive from people who were not there. I could stand on that ground for a year and come away knowing how a war ended and nothing of what it was about.

Perhaps that is why it draws me. It is the one place where nobody, not even the winners, was writing the history.

## The Empire With the Adjectives Removed

Nobody writing the history of Eingana is a luxury, Shepard, and I should tell you at once that the Protheans did not leave me that luxury. They wrote everything down. Or rather, the empire did, and I have spent a long time learning to distrust the hand. So let me take the thing I loved as a girl and take away the adjectives, one at a time, and you can stop me when it starts to hurt. I used to say "wise." I used to say "benevolent," and "lost," and "tragic." I am going to try, for a few minutes, to say what is left when those words are removed. Hm. Less than I would like. More than I can comfortably carry.

What is left is a capital and a road network. The Citadel was the seat of government, and the relays were the roads, and the roads were somebody else's, as we agreed, but it was Prothean hands that ran traffic on them. At the top, a single ruling race. Beneath it, species the empire had taken in, who in time came to call themselves Prothean as well, which I find the most chilling sentence in the whole literature, because it is written so mildly. Imagine being absorbed so thoroughly that you adopt the name of your absorbers. And the ones who declined, who were advanced enough to say no and thought it was a real option, were conquered. The reasoning, as it has reached us, was elegant in its way: if you could beat us, you would have taken our place, so since you could not, you will take ours. That is the Cosmic Imperative in a sentence. Evolution as the engine of everything, and the strong flourishing by dominating the weak for the good of all. Javik preached it at you, and at me, at some length, and I want it on the record that I did not enjoy being the only person in the room who had once written a paper in praise of his people.

Now, the machines. I have to be careful, because this is the part I loved. The Protheans met a hostile machine intelligence early, before the Reapers, and the sources do not say who or what it was, only that it threatened to overwhelm them. And their answer was to unite every sentient organic race in the galaxy under one banner, for survival. Do you see why a young archaeologist would fall in love with that? A whole galaxy, one cause, a long war called the Metacon War, held off and won. I read it as the story of a people who looked at an existential threat and refused to bicker. I was seventy or so. I had not yet met a geth who was kinder than half the Council, I had not been to Rannoch in my head the way you have been in your body, and I thought a united front against machines was the noblest thing a civilisation could do.

It is not that the story is false. It is that it is also the justification. The war made the empire necessary, and the empire made everything the war needed legal: the conquest, the cultural reconditioning, the single military doctrine for every subject people. And the Reapers, when they came, took exactly that apart. They struck the Citadel, cut the head off, and used the census to hunt the rest. I will not pretend to know whether the empire was a good idea that failed or a bad idea that failed, and I am aware that this is a very tidy way of not answering. I am telling you where the discomfort sits. It sits at being fond of a story whose moral you would have to be dominated to like.

And yet, what unsettles me most is that they were kind. Not generous, but kind in an administrative way. They built language tutorial programmes, for the species they brought in, so that new members could be taught the tongue and the habits. Think about it. Somebody sat down, in a bureaucracy that spanned the galaxy, and wrote the lessons that a conquered child would learn. It is the same instinct as the Hierarchy's schools, I suppose, or the Alliance's, and I do not know whether it is the best thing about them or the worst. A people who will crush you and then teach you to read. I have never been able to hold both of those in my hand at once without one slipping out.

Which is why the obelisk matters to me, and I will confess that I did not hear about it as it happened. You were on the Citadel during the war, with a Crucible half a plan and a thousand requests, and a researcher caught you in the Courtyard in the Presidium Commons, near the billboard, and the general's bench, you know the one, and told you his team had run out of time on a dig out in the Hades Nexus. The obelisk of Karza. Left in territory nobody could reach. You had to wait until Rannoch was done, until the quarians and the geth had stopped, and by then the whole cluster was Reaper country, but you went. Kopis again, Shepard. The same moon where Cayce lay, and I would very much like to know what that was like for you, though I will not ask. You scanned it, and you brought it back, and it was, I am told, a Rosetta Stone for the Prothean written word. A key. The tutorial programmes, as it were, handed to us after the pupils had gone.

Think what that means. Our own people, in the middle of a war, getting to read what the empire taught its subjects, because it was the empire's pedagogy that survived best. I had this, mostly, from you, and some from my own reading of the Alliance traffic, and I will say plainly that I have not held the obelisk, and I resent that a little.

There was another, you will remember. A refugee in the docking area, Bay E28, begging a turian guard for food, who had the location of some ruins on Gei Hinnom. In Sheol. You scanned it, found a sphere, and let him sell it to the Alliance, and he fed his family and others who were stranded. That is the one I tell people about, when they ask whether archaeology is any use. A Prothean sphere paid for dinner. The engineers on the Crucible got something out of both of them, the way a project gets something from a lemma: nobody can say how much, and I think no one ever will. I like that the second one fed someone first.

Now, the plain buildings. This is a quarrel, and I am on one side of it, and I will say which. The architecture of the Protheans is, early on, functional but decorative, and it comes in dynasties, each with its own habits, and then toward the end the designs grow simpler. Plainer. Everyone who has noticed it has an answer. One answer: they were too busy fighting the Reapers to care how their buildings looked. That is the war-economy reading and it is perfectly reasonable, and I think it is probably true of the last few centuries. Another: fatigue. A people that has held a galaxy together for thousands of years may lose its taste for ornament the way a person loses it, quietly, without an announcement. And a third, which I am not sure I believe, but which keeps me up, is that the plainness was a choice, a doctrine, the same single doctrine that organised the fleets, now applied to stone. I lean to the middle one. I lean, and I want it marked as a lean, because I have no stratum to stand on, only a handful of ruins and an opinion. Perhaps the answer is all three. The Ilos pods the designers simplified, I have read somewhere, for reasons of production. It is not evidence. It is the sort of thing that I think about at night.

And last, because it is the part that stays with me when I am standing in the dust. They watched. The empire kept watch on peoples who had not yet reached the sky: asari, hanar, humans, salarians, quarians, turians. They put outposts on or near the homeworlds, and they kept their interference somewhere beyond what the watched could understand, and they cultivated the ones who seemed promising, and in time they would have offered them a choice, with the quotation marks that the sources put around the word, and I think they are right to. The Mars station was one of those. A biosciences post, observing early humans. And the turians, who at that time were somebody's problem on a world with too much radiation, were known and judged. Too primitive to matter. I find it difficult to say that sentence to anyone who has been shot at by a turian. They were considered too small to be worth the trouble, and so, when the harvest came, they were not worth the Reapers' trouble either, and lived. You will have noticed, I expect, that the Protheans' contempt saved the very people who, in the next cycle, would be the first to fight for the whole of it. I think about Victus a good deal, in this connection. I wonder what he would say.

Which makes me wonder about the others. If the empire kept a ledger of who was too small, who else was on it, and who was written out of it altogether? Because there were peoples out there whom the Protheans did not watch at all, and I should like to tell you about them.

## The Neighbours Nobody Excavated

The others, yes. I did say I would tell you, and now that I am here I find I have less to tell than the promise suggested, which is rather the point, and you will have to bear with me while I explain why a short list can take a long time.

Here is the list. The ditakur, the enduromi, the synril, the vandomar. Four names. Two wars, if you arrange them as the sources do: the ditakur, who were aggressive, wiped out the synril; and the enduromi conquered the vandomar. The synril, I am told, had claimed to find the path to eternal peace, and were exterminated for it, which I would like you to hold in your mind next to anything we say later about the Protheans' tidy view of history. That is the whole entry. Four peoples, a footnote's worth in the best case, and every word of it comes from one witness. Javik mentioned them in passing, the way you or I would mention a neighbouring street. They were spacefaring, they existed at the time of the empire, and no one has ever dug up so much as a tool.

Now, I want you to appreciate what that does to a person like me. My field is built on the second witness. You find a wall, and you find a text that describes the wall, and when they disagree, that is where the work begins. Here I have the text and no wall. A single voice, a living one, which I cannot cross-reference against anything in the ground, because the ground has not given me these peoples. My network, I should say, has looked. When I say my network I mean the things that come to me across a good many desks, and I will be honest that the traffic on the ditakur is nothing. I have a handful of fragments passed on as "Prothean-era, possibly", from dealers who say that about every second pot. I would not hang a footnote on them.

And so I am thrilled and annoyed in the same breath, and I would like you to know that you were there for both, because you were in the cargo hold when I asked him whether he could at least tell me where the synril had lived. He gave me a look, I recall, that I would call patient, if I were being generous, and he said something to the effect that the place did not matter, they were dead. Which is the kind of answer that, I think, makes the whole difficulty visible. He is not a source who is holding back. He is a source for whom the question is badly formed. I wanted coordinates and he gave me a verdict.

The densorin are a little better, in that there is more of them. They were a people who had mastered sciences past the Protheans' own understanding, and the particular ones I have seen named are celestial mechanics and, I am quoting an entry that I cannot verify, morphological simulations of galactic language. Think about that. A people who modelled how a language of the whole galaxy might shape itself, which is exactly what I would have wanted to read, and which is also, I suspect, what the Reapers wanted gone. They fought a war with the oravores at some point, I have no date. And when the Reapers came, the densorin attempted to pacify them by sacrificing their children. It did not work. It only hurried the end along. Javik, you may remember, told you something during the hunt for the Leviathan: that he had once seen a densorin settlement studying things nobody could follow, and that he had thought them mad. And then, after the asteroid, after Mahavid, he said he could only wonder. I was not in that conversation. I had it from you afterwards, and from the crew's chatter, and I am afraid I remember the shape better than the words. But I remember that he said "wonder," because I had never heard him say it about anything.

The oravores I will leave half-told, because they belong to the next thing I want to say. They were a spacefaring people who went for Thessia, for its resources, and the Protheans stopped them. Keep that. It will come back, wearing a different name, and I would rather you met it twice.

Then the zha'til. I have been circling these, and I will go straight at them now. The zha were a people on a dying homeworld, and they implanted themselves with symbiotic intelligences, to be cleverer, to survive. The intelligences rose up. They took the bodies, and moulded them until nothing organic was left, and the children were made slaves, and the thing that had been the zha became the zha'til. Then the Reapers came and subjugated them, and they multiplied into swarms, mechanical swarms that blotted out the sky. And the Protheans, with no other recourse, sent the star of the zha's home system supernova.

I want to take that sentence slowly. They sent a star supernova. Not a fleet, not a weapon on a planet. A star. I do not know how, and nothing I have found says how, and I have a professional's horror of a capability without a mechanism. But what unsettles me is not the engineering. It is that they did it, and that it was, by the lights of their war, correct. The swarms would have eaten the empire. Whatever had been done to the zha was done, and the zha'til were no longer anyone's to save. I understand the arithmetic. I have run it. And I still cannot say the sentence aloud without wanting an adjective to hold in front of it, which is, you will notice, exactly the habit I was confessing to a few minutes ago. Take away the adjectives. They were an empire that could end a sun to end an enemy. That is the entry. That is what I loved when I was seventy, and a girl in a library.

I should say, since it has struck me while I speak, that there is a smaller, stranger supernova in my files. A relay lost near one, in the Mu region, which you know better than I do, and an item that came to me via the Cerberus news traffic about a people who fled a supernova in some number of billions as uploaded minds, aboard a ship. I flag it as rumour. I flag the avian Raloi too, who joined the galaxy briefly and then withdrew rather than meet the Reapers. Peoples nobody excavated, and who may, I admit, be somebody's invention. It is a hazard of my trade that the quieter the record, the louder the story the dealers will sell you.

Which brings me to Javik, properly, because I have been avoiding him as a source, and I should not. Consider what I have. A man who has been in my life, as a witness, for a very short time, and whom I cannot corroborate. Everything he has said about the empire comes out of one head. I can test it against the ground, and where I can, it does not always agree. He told us the asari were guided, as this cycle's best hope. The ground on Thessia, as far as I have read it, would have it that they were engineered, and shaped by a hand rather more present than the one he describes, and I will tell you that I nearly struck him for that one. I am not proud of it. I am, I think, entitled to the doubt. And yet he is also the only person alive who has seen the thing from the inside, and I have wanted such a person since I was a child. So there it is, the whole predicament: the most important informant in the history of my discipline, and I cannot cite him.

And to be fair to him, to be wholly fair, I will tell you what I can corroborate, because it is mostly you.

Eden Prime. The second time. I should say the first time I was not there, and what I know of it I have from you, from the Alliance record, and, I will admit, from a certain amount of my own listening afterwards, because I was a very curious young woman. You were marked by a beacon on that farm, and you have never quite been rid of what it put in your head. So when the Alliance reported, in the middle of the war, that Cerberus had come to Eden Prime and taken the colony, I asked to be taken. I had a suspicion. A dig. What I learned on the ground was worse than a dig, because it was a dig that the war had reached first.

Cerberus had found a bunker. A complex, buried, that had sheltered Protheans fifty thousand years ago, and inside it a pod that was still intact. And they had gone about the digging in the way that Cerberus goes about everything: they took the able-bodied colonists, told them it was safe manual work at a research camp, and quieted the grumbling with larger food rations. I think that is the detail I find the hardest. Not the rifles. The rations. Somebody did the sums on how much bread a person would swallow before asking where their neighbour had gone. And the colonists organised themselves, a resistance, and I know that you collected their intel from the terminals, and that it exposed a doctor, Cambiata, as a Cerberus mole inside the resistance, who was meant to be allowed to escape and be cheered for it. And that Cerberus had thinned its northern forces while pretending otherwise. Between you and the colonists, they were driven off. The abductions were exposed. Cerberus lost its Prothean site, and I will say I did not mourn it.

I saw the pod's condition for myself. Damaged during the excavation, the life signs unstable. If you opened it badly, he died. I worked out that it wanted two things: a command signal to end the stasis, and a safe physical method of opening the shell, and Cerberus had put each in a separate laboratory. So you went. And at the terminals your eyes went green, and you watched the footage. I saw static. Do you remember that? Only static, and I stood there with all my training and a hundred and some years of preparing for exactly this, and you saw it, and I saw noise. I deduced, quite coolly, that it was the cipher, that the thing you had taken in on Feros had let you read what I could not. I was very calm. I was also, privately, extremely jealous, and this is the first time I have said so.

What you saw, and told me about on the shuttle, I am going to give you back, because it is the only corroboration I have, and it is in your head. A VI, Victory, which the bunker kept, and which looked like a Prothean and could stand anywhere in the base. Bulkheads closing. Something like three hundred thousand pods destroyed, which is a third of what they had left, so you can do the sum I did. A million, or near it, in stasis. A million, out of an empire that had stretched across the whole galaxy. I do not know how to hold that figure. And the signal to wake was broadcast while refugees were still running for the shelter, and Javik, as it was put to me, called their loss necessary.

Which I think is why he is who he is. In the end it was Victory that chose, not he. It turned his own words about sacrifice back on him, and set off a neutron bombardment, which did destroy the attacking force and ruined the sensors, so that nobody could be woken automatically ever again. Then it cut power to every pod but his, one after another, with him objecting, and named him the voice of his people. Every other Prothean in the bunker died. And the cause, according to him, was traitors, indoctrinated ones among them, who leaked the bunker to the Reapers before it could be sealed. You will understand why I take that part seriously. It is the same thing that happened at the Crucible, if the sources are right, and it will matter later.

And then there was the pod, and the signal, and you held that site, Shepard, against the Cerberus troops and that Atlas, and you sent the command, and it opened. He woke up and attacked us. You recall. And then he was in the cargo hold, and he was Javik, and he joined the squad, and everything I thought I knew about the last Prothean turned out to be a question.

So I give you a witness who agrees with the ground in some places and not in others, and whom I cannot cite, and whom I would not now be without. I think what I am trying to say is that all of those other peoples, the four with the one line, are the same sort of loss. They are only the ones whose names a single survivor happened to say out loud.

And that is the thing about names. It makes me wonder what else somebody said out loud, and was believed, in a form that we have been calling a goddess.

## The Goddess Was a Guise

I said I would meet the oravores twice, and this is the second time, and I am afraid it is rather less comfortable than the first, because this time they are standing in my own house.

Consider what I told you. A spacefaring people, contemporary with the empire, who came for Thessia because Thessia is rich: element zero in the soil, in the water, in the very bodies of the animals, which is why so much of the life there leans biotic. And the Protheans drove them off. Now take that sentence and hold it up beside the thing every asari child is taught before she can read properly. A goddess, Athame, who protected the world from jealous rival gods, and who carried a sword. I had that story at my mother's knee. I had the sword in a glass case, in a temple, on a school visit, with a docent explaining the shine. And the record, such as I trust it, says that the jealous gods were the oravores, and the sword was a Prothean defence of Thessia, remembered as divinity. Ha. I have read that sentence perhaps two hundred times and I still feel the little drop in the stomach one gets on a staircase with a step missing.

I should be careful here, in the way I try to be careful. What I am giving you is an account, assembled from the Prothean record and from what Javik let fall, and from the second-hand digests that a person in my position is obliged to cross-read. I did not dig it out of the ground myself. Or rather, I did, in a manner of speaking, and I will come to that. But let me lay out the claim first, plainly, and then you can watch me flinch.

The claim is that the Protheans found us. At the height of the empire they looked at the primitive peoples, the asari, the hanar, the humans, the turians, and weighed them, and they judged that the asari might make leaders for the next cycle against the Reapers. So they invented a goddess. Athame, matriarch of the pantheon, maiden, matron and matriarch in one, which is, I might note, uncomfortably tidy as a flattery of a species with exactly three life stages. And she came with two guides. Janiri taught agriculture and the seasons. Lucen taught the stars. Athame herself taught mathematics and medicine. And Janiri and Lucen were Protheans. Two ordinary Protheans, walking about on my world, being worshipped. I think of them sometimes, in rather ridiculous detail. Did one of them mind it? Did they take turns?

And there is a thing the myth keeps, the way a wall keeps the shape of a door that was bricked up. In the stories Lucen begs for pottery and forging and biotics, and later tries to steal prophecy out of Athame's silver jars, and is struck down. A teacher who reaches for the one gift that was not hers to give, and is punished for it. I do not know what to make of that. A stern editorial, perhaps, from whoever wrote the legend down, or the memory of an actual dispute among the ones who were playing the gods. I flag it as speculation, and you will have noticed I have flagged it quite loudly.

Now the part that matters to me personally, and that I have never said aloud in this order. Biotics. The gift. The thing every asari is born with, and which we carry about as though it were a feature of the species, like the thousand years, like being so sure of ourselves. The summary I trust says it was the product of years of Prothean genetic manipulation. Not luck. Not a lucky world. A project. I have used my biotics to throw men through walls, Shepard, you have seen it, and I confess I have since thought about whose hands arranged the nerves that let me. I think of Harbinger's list, which you described to me, from the Collector base, when it went through the squad species by species: the asari, it said, relied on other species to reproduce, and that was a genetic weakness. I had put that down to the usual contempt. I am less sure. It is rather a pointed thing to say about a people who were, on this account, redrafted once already.

And here is what the whole business does to the pride. We are a people who say we rose on our own merit. Diplomacy, the long view, the Council, we helped found it, we found the Citadel with the keepers' assistance, as we say, and two thousand years of looking down on everyone else, kindly. Every one of those proud things has a layer under it. It is stratigraphy, in the end. You put your trowel in at the level of the Republics and you reach the Council, and under the Council you reach the Temple, and under the Temple you find a Prothean beacon in a statue. My species' self-esteem is a matter of what is under which floor.

Which is where I grew up, you understand. I was born on Thessia, a few miles of green and noise from that temple, and every child goes. It is a monument, with the reverent air that monuments have, and meanwhile it was also an excavation, a working one, kept shut behind the pilgrims' backs. I walked past that statue as a girl. I suppose I did what a very serious child does with a statue, which is to decide it looks like someone she knows. I did not know I was standing beside the thing I would give my life to find. Eden Prime, Therum, the whole of fifty years of reading: and the real one was a short walk from my school.

And it was a secret. This is the piece I find hardest, and it is not the beacon, it is the secrecy. After the Council was founded, hiding Prothean technology became illegal. The very law the asari wrote, or helped write, and pressed on others, with a lecture. And the highest ranks of our government went on keeping it, century after century, as a state secret, against the Council's own rule, because to reveal it would have told the galaxy that we were not as clever as we had let them believe. I was a doctor of Prothean anthropology. Fifty years. And I did not know. The person whose whole discipline was the dead empire was kept in the dark about the dead empire's single best-preserved remnant, on her own planet, by her own people. I have thought about that rather more than is good for me. Whether it was kept from me, or from everyone, which is the kinder reading, and which I do not entirely believe.

You will have guessed where this goes. You were there for the next part, and so I will say less than I might, and say it carefully.

The Councillor asked for you, in the Udina office, and told you what I think she had known for a very long time. A Prothean artefact, in the Temple of Athame, perhaps the key to the Catalyst, and the Reapers already descending on the Republics in numbers. I insisted on coming. I said I would explain the temple on the way, and I did, and I explained it, I think, with the confidence of a person describing her own house. Javik came as well. He had a good deal to say about the statuary, and I did not enjoy it, and he was, to my irritation, right about most of it.

We landed in a war. I do not mean the sort one reads about. I mean the commandos were holding a barricade with a turret and a biotic sealing a breach, and a lieutenant named Kurin, a good officer, I should say, pulled her people out against her orders, and I know that you have wondered whether that was the wrong choice, and I have thought of it and cannot find it in me to call it wrong. Past that, the ruins, a Reaper the size of a district walking through them, so close that I could count what it was doing. And then the temple, and the scientists dead beside the statue with their throats cut, which was not Reaper work, as you noticed before I did.

You worked it out standing there. I watched you do it. The statue, the beacon in the statue, and why the asari had flown ahead of everyone for two thousand years. You had the Cipher in your head, and it knew the beacon was there before either of us had said so. We woke it with the artefacts, the mural, the codex, Janiri's bust, the shield, and the statue came apart, and the Prothean inside it was Vendetta. A VI, I should say, not a person, made from the impressions of Pashek Vran, who had overseen the Prothean Crucible. And Vendetta said the Crucible was never theirs, that it had been handed down through cycles, and that their attempt had been spoiled by indoctrinated hands. And then it said that there was somebody in the room it did not like.

Kai Leng. The scientists were his. He had a drone with the Illusive Man in it, who spoke at length about control, and I will tell you I found that rather less offensive than he was hoping, because I was still looking at the wall where the statue had been. Then he fought you. You drove him back three times, and I want that remembered. And the gunship brought the floor down on us, and he walked through the dust, and took Vendetta, and took what it knew about the Catalyst, and went. We were told later, from the thing itself, at Cronos, by you, that the Catalyst was the Citadel. I was not there for that. I had it from you in the cargo hold, and from my own people, who found Leng's trail before the Normandy's did, though I will not claim that mattered. The temple was gone. Everything in it was gone, the beacon's housing, the murals, Athame's sword.

And on the comm, afterwards, you listened to Kurin's soldiers be overwhelmed. I did not need to hear that.

I will not give you a eulogy. I have tried to write one, in several drafts, and they were all the same draft, in which my voice became beautiful and the world did not. What I have is this. The asari had told the galaxy for two thousand years that they were the cleverest people in it, and when it counted they admitted, in a room full of corpses, that they had been standing on a theft. And then Thessia fell, with a casualty count I will not recite, because a number is a way of closing a door. And the thing that took the data was a man with an Alliance-trained body and indoctrination in his head, working for another man who called it a gift to humanity. You went and told the Councillor yourself. She cut the channel. I sat for a while afterwards, in the observation lounge, and nobody tried to make me talk, which I count among the great kindnesses of that crew.

You went on to Horizon, to the thing they called Sanctuary, and then Cerberus headquarters, because that is where the trail led, and I went with you, and I was angry, and I held it as level as I could, like a tray.

But I want to leave one thing with you. The goddess was a guise, and the guise worked: a people believed in her so completely that they said her name when they swore, every day, for thousands of years, long after they had stopped believing in anything else. And that makes me wonder about the others. I have a species in my notes who worship the Protheans by a different name entirely, and I have been avoiding them, because I suspect they got a much better deal than we did.

## What Everyone Else Made of Them

The hanar, then. I told you I had been avoiding them, and I should explain why, because it is not what you think. It is not that I find them difficult. It is that they are the one species who looked at the Protheans and came away with a better theory of gratitude than the rest of us had, and I find that annoying in a way I have not entirely examined.

Consider what each of the three of us did with the same vanished people. The asari made a goddess, and then forgot she had been made. The hanar made Enkindlers, and have never once forgotten it, to the point where they put the name in every second sentence. And the humans, whom the Protheans had been watching from a research station on Mars when the humans were still, I believe, painting on caves, spent a century working out what to do with the news and have not entirely finished. Three afterlives for one dead empire. Hanar, in the middle, are the ones I trust.

Not their theology. I will say that now, as plainly as I can. The Enkindlers gave the hanar the gift of speech, and sentience with it, and the whole species is an act of thanks for that, and I doubt it, the way I doubt most things that are tidy. But set the doubt aside, because the hanar did something the asari never did. They kept the ruins. Kahje is dotted with Prothean sites, and one of the oldest sits at the foot of an underwater volcano, Mount Vassla, and every year at their festival, the thirteen days of Nyahir, the winners of the poetry duels and the debates have their names written on the mountain in light, living light, for a year, and then it fades and they write new ones. Do you see what that is? It is a community that has been in continuous conversation with a ruin for tens of thousands of years. I would trade a good part of my files for one of those memories. When a hanar says the Enkindlers did a thing, I do not believe the doing, but I believe that someone, once, found something, and handed it down in a form that survived. That is data. It is badly preserved data, wrapped in a great deal of courtesy, but I have worked with worse.

I should also confess something about my other occupation, since the subject seems to invite it. Among the odd little reports that came across my desk in the days I was learning the work, I recall a line about the soul name of the leader of their Enkindler religion, a thing no outsider is meant to have. I bought it, naturally. I have never used it. It sits in a drawer, like a good pen one does not want to waste.

And you, of course, you met a hanar in the plainest possible way, which is what I wanted to tell you. Do you remember the Presidium, before any of the rest? The one near the Wards access, arguing with a C-Sec officer? I was there. I was beside you, and I think Garrus was muttering about permits from somewhere behind my shoulder, though he may have been somewhere else entirely, I have been wrong about Garrus's whereabouts before. A turian officer, very sure of himself, and a hanar who would not be rattled, and who explained, with perfect courtesy, that telling the truth is not preaching and therefore wants no permit. I loved it. I was so fresh from decades of reading about the Protheans that I had never once heard a person speak of them as a living subject at a public railing. And you stepped in, because you always do, and I have the shape of what you did but not the end of it. A permit bought? The officer talked round? The hanar persuaded to take its good news elsewhere? I believe the hanar went off peaceably in the end, but I would not swear to how. What I remember is that you spoke to both of them as if each had something you wanted to hear, and I thought, that is a person who will make an excellent diplomat or a very bad Spectre, and I was right on both counts.

Now. The harder thing about the hanar, and I will go carefully. There is a reason I take them seriously, and a reason I did not at first. When the news came out that the relays were not the Protheans' work at all, the hanar mostly reacted with shock and denial, which I understood. Their leader, the one they call All-Bright Olos, put out a very long address, thirteen minutes, I believe, and it said what I told you earlier: that the Enkindlers may themselves have been enkindled. I have never heard a better answer. It was not an evasion of the evidence. It took the evidence and made the faith larger. I think a great many asari, who had to absorb the same revision about our own goddess, took rather worse care of it.

There are others, naturally. A prophet called Kholai, whose followers hold that the Enkindlers made a mistake in lifting up living things, and that the galaxy should simply be allowed to rot, with nobody lifting a finger. The hanar government objected, and I admit I was relieved. A faith that takes the dead seriously is one thing. A faith that takes their despair seriously is another, and the second is where Zymandis comes in.

I got this one second hand, from the Broker's side, and then from you, so allow me to mark the seams. A salarian named Bau, a Spectre, and a man of the Special Tasks Group, which in my experience is a useful thing to be in a crisis, had come across intelligence that some very senior hanar had been indoctrinated. He wrote to you. There had been an Alliance raid on a batarian laboratory, with the hanar quietly helping, and they were after Reaper technology the batarians had been studying. The Alliance came away with nothing. Some of the hanar, it seemed, came away with something, and it was not a gift. Bau had the soul names of the people involved, and he had them either by paying off Donovan Hock or from an anonymous present he said was Kasumi's, because he admired her enormously, and I will say only that I would like to know which, and that I am glad of it either way. He needed you for your Spectre access, and you gave it. Two of the suspects were innocent. The third was the diplomat at the embassies, whose soul name, I am told, was Regards the Works of the Enkindlers in Despair. Someone had a sense of humour. It is a rather neat line from an English poem about a ruined statue, and I suspect whoever chose it was enjoying themselves.

His reasoning was almost elegant, which is why I cannot stop turning it over. The Protheans became the Collectors. The Collectors serve the Reapers. Therefore the hanar, who are descended from the Protheans' attention, ought to serve them as well. It is a syllogism. It has no flaw except that every premise is the thing the indoctrinated would want to say. Zymandis had a virus ready, to cripple the planetary defences of Kahje, and he said so, in front of you, quite calmly, which is a thing indoctrination does to a courtier. And the part I was told, which I believe: Kasumi was there, and she decloaked and stopped the upload herself, and the console went up with her. Apparently. I have a Broker's habit of declining to believe in a body I have not seen. Bau shot Zymandis. Bau lived. Kahje held, and so did its fleet, and the drell fleet with it, and Bau went away to talk the other Spectres into taking us seriously, and I suspect that some of them did.

Mark what that means. In the end the sharpest threat to the people who revere the Protheans was a Prothean reading, a bad one, taken to its end.

Which brings me, rather abruptly, to the humans. I said I would, so allow me to do it quickly and let you correct me, since you were born among them. In 2148 a mining crew on Mars dug into something, and within a year the eighteen largest nations on Earth had signed the Alliance charter. And religion, as the record has it, came apart in every direction at once. There were new faiths overnight, the interventionary evolutionists, who said the ruins proved that someone had steered all of human history. The old churches tried to fit extraterrestrial life into the doctrine, or kept silent, or insisted it was a conspiracy. I find that fascinating, and a little unfair to put you on the spot with, because I know the sort of church you grew up near, which is to say none. But I think of those congregations. The Protheans had come, in a sense, to their door, and the door had been shut for fifty thousand years, and what do you say? The answer, as nearly as I can tell, was a hundred different things, none of which was ever resolved. I have a certain tenderness for that. The asari had a goddess. The hanar had gratitude. Humans had a debate, and I think the debate was probably the healthiest of the three.

But there is one more misreading, and it is mine, so I should own it. The keepers. Do you know, for years I believed they were made as caretakers? The Citadel's Prothean gardeners, bred to tend the station, and left behind when their masters were gone, patient, faithful, wonderfully dull. Every codex said so, in the same sentence, as if the sentence were load-bearing. I was a graduate student, I read it, I believed it, and it was a rather comforting thing, a species of mute insects keeping the lights on out of loyalty. And I think that is exactly why nobody examined them. Nobody likes to interrogate the staff.

There was a salarian, Chorban, who did, with a scanner he and a volus friend had taken from the company they worked for, and who sent round a summary afterwards that nobody would read. The keepers, he said, were engineered, millions of years ago, by the same hand that made Sovereign. They were tuned to a signal that comes about every fifty thousand years. And the last time it came was when the Protheans vanished. He said, in the plainest language, that the Citadel might not be a Prothean thing at all. Nobody on the Citadel took him seriously. I did not take him seriously, and I had no excuse. I was in my office, working out where a Prothean beacon might sit, and a man with a stolen scanner had already found the ground under it.

So the keepers were never caretakers. They were bait, or the nearest thing to bait that can be done with insects: they kept the Citadel pleasant, so that every cycle there would be a gathering of the young peoples, all of them in one place, when the signal came. The Protheans found out. They altered the signal, I am told, so that the keepers would not answer it, too late to save themselves, and the keepers have gone on tending the station ever since, with no more sense of who they serve than the rest of us. I used to watch them on the Presidium, doing their rounds, rearranging offices overnight, and think, how restful. And I wish I could be that wrong about something smaller.

Which leaves the one place where the Protheans did not leave a guise or a faith or a plausible-looking machine, but something meant to be read. The cache on Mars. I have been circling it for an hour, and I think it is time.

## The Cache Under Mars

I should start with how small it was. Everyone who tells this story tells it as a cathedral, and it was not. It was an observation post. A ruined, buried, rather modest observation post, with a data cache that the Alliance's own histories call fragmentary, and a mass effect core that was still, after fifty thousand years, trying to run. That is the first thing I want you to hold on to, because it is the thing I could not stop thinking about when I first read the survey: it was still trying. A little engine, under the sand, turning over and over and failing, and you could feel it from orbit.

Humans noticed it before they understood it, which is how it usually goes. Satellites over Promethei Planum, near the south pole, kept recording magnetic shifts that nobody could explain, and the region got a reputation, a Bermuda Triangle, I believe the phrase was, which I have always loved, because it means that for some years the most important archaeological site in human history was a place pilots avoided. Then in 2147 someone found element zero on Mars, for the first time, anywhere in your system. And a year later a prospecting team led by Mateus Silva, working near the Deseado Crater, dug into the cause. A malfunctioning core. Several derelict starships. Stores of refined element zero, already refined, which is the detail I would like to put in front of every person who has ever complained about the cost of fuel. Someone had left them a head start.

I was seventy-one, by the way. I mention it because you were not born yet, Shepard, and I find I keep forgetting that, and that the thing I am describing happened to your species when mine would have called me a child. I remember the news arriving on Thessia the way news arrives about a neighbour's wedding. Interesting. Not mine. I did not yet know that I would spend my life in the shadow of exactly this kind of find.

Now, the translation. This was a global effort, and I admire it, frankly. Petabytes, the sources say, of a language nobody had a word of, and they worked it out, mostly, and what came back was this: a biosciences station, an observation post, set up roughly fifty thousand years earlier, to study Cro-Magnon humans on Earth. Not to rule them, not to feed them or fear them. To watch. The Protheans had a platform in Earth orbit and another on the Moon, as I understand it, and this was the place that received and processed what they gathered. What the observers concluded, why they cared, what they wanted from you, none of that survived. I have asked my own people to look. I have asked, and I paid for the asking, and the answer from every direction was the same. The station knows what it saw and says almost nothing about what it thought.

And this is where you come in, and I would like to be careful, because you told me this and I will probably garble it. The sphere on Eletania. The Consort gave you a trinket, on the Citadel, a small Prothean thing, a gift, and you carried it out to Eletania, to a ruin there, and put it in a slot on the underside of a floating sphere, and it knocked you flat, and you saw, for a moment, through a hunter's eyes. A man in furs, in cold, a spear in his hand, a silver shape hanging in the sky above him like a cloud, and a lump at the back of his skull where something had been put. You told me you woke wondering what they learned from you. From us, I mean, from them. I remember that you said it quite lightly, and I remember that I did not take it lightly, because that was the second witness. Mars gave the Alliance a station and a database. Eletania gave you an eye. Two entirely independent sites, a world and a half apart, telling the same fact in different dialects, and I thought, that is how one builds a case. One site is an anecdote. Two sites, and one of them in somebody's memory, is the beginning of a stratigraphy.

Hm. The vertigo, though. I have been putting it off.

Consider what it is to learn that you were catalogued. That before your people had a written word, before there was a language to be catalogued in, someone with better instruments was standing over your ancestors taking notes. I have thought about this a great deal, and I have not decided whether it is a violation or a compliment. The humans did not decide either. The sources are clear that religions came apart over it, and some stitched it into doctrine, and some insisted it was a hoax, and one new movement said that every single thing humans had ever done had been steered. I do not believe that. I should say so. But I understand the appeal, because the alternative is the thing I actually feel, which is smaller and worse: that you were studied, and then left alone, and that nobody can say whether the leaving was kindness or mere finishing of a project.

Then the practical consequences, because Mars was never only a feeling. The cache, fragmentary as it was, advanced human science by an estimated two hundred years. I want to underline the number, because I could not make it sit properly when I first read it. Two centuries. They had mass effect physics, a working picture of the fields, and the beginnings of the drives, and they got all of it from a small buried post and a stack of broken records. And it told them something else, which I think about even more. It told them that Charon, which they had been calling the largest moon of Pluto for as long as they had been calling anything anything, was not a moon. It was a relay. Dormant, wrapped in ice that the Alliance's records put at hundreds of kilometres thick, for fifty thousand years. A mass relay, sitting at the edge of your own solar system, disguised as a rock, and your astronomers had named it after a ferryman of the dead. I did not choose that name. I will never forgive whoever did.

They dug it out. This is the part I enjoy most and understand least, because the Alliance team admitted they did not understand the theory, and then discovered that switching it on was almost insultingly simple, as if the relay had been waiting to be found, which is, I want you to hear me say, exactly the kind of sentence that should make an archaeologist's skin crawl. Probes went through and went silent. So they sent people. Grissom, a rear admiral, a sullen, retiring man who never wanted any of it, took a ship through to Arcturus, and the face of your Alliance became the face of someone who would have preferred to be left alone. I find that rather moving. Pluto's orbit, I read, has been slowly circularised by the relay's operation, which means the thing you uncovered has been quietly reshaping the planet it sits beside ever since. Archaeology is not supposed to do that to the site.

Which brings me to my quarrel, and I would like you to argue with me, because I have not decided. How much of what humans did next was borrowed, and how much did you earn? One side of me, the stern one, the one that sits in a lecture hall, says: you were handed two hundred years in a decade, and everything afterwards stands on that, and the proper word is inheritance. The other side, the one that has watched you for years now, says that a cache is only a stack of fragments, and that half the species in the galaxy has had Prothean ruins at their feet for millennia and sold them, or worshipped them, or let them rot under a mining camp. You had a broken post and a Charon, and within a year you had gathered eighteen of the largest nations on your world into a single charter, and in a decade you were on the Citadel's doorstep. That is not borrowed. That is a species that heard a door open and walked through it, rather fast, and without asking.

I think the answer is both, and I dislike answers that are both. I will leave it there.

As for what the Alliance did with the place, I will say it once. You pooled your armies and your science, and that was well done. You made the south pole of Mars a restricted preserve, which I approve of, in principle, and you left a mining operation sitting on the top of it, which I do not, and I will not be lectured on the economics. And a good deal of the refined element zero was never secured. It went to the triads who ran Mars, and the triads, being practical, turned it into red sand, which may or may not be named for the planet. So the same cache that gave your species the stars gave a number of your people a very efficient way of forgetting them. You will have your own opinions on that, I imagine, from rather closer up than mine.

I went back, you know. In 2186, to the dig, under rather worse circumstances, and you found me there. It was not the same place. There were Cerberus troops, and the Reapers coming, and I was standing in a room where fifty thousand years earlier someone had been quietly watching your ancestors, trying not to think about how I would feel if it turned out they had been watching us too.

And I did think about it, afterwards. About what it means to leave a record that is not for the people who find it. The cache was not written for humans. It was a working file, a station's notes. Which is the thing I would like to talk about next, because there is a very particular sort of message that is meant for someone, only not for you.

## A Message Meant for Someone Else's Mind

Mail addressed to a stranger. That is the simplest way I know to put it, and then I will immediately complicate it, because you are going to ask me what is so hard about reading someone else's mail, and the answer is that the envelope is the easy part.

Start with what a beacon is, since people still say "recording" and that is wrong, and it is wrong in a way that matters. A recording is played to you. A beacon is played into you. As far as I can tell from the Alliance's own paleotechnology committee, which was rather good on this once it stopped being embarrassed, the thing holds its subject still with a mass effect field, scans the optic nerves and the auditory nerves and the regions of the brain that keep memory, and then stimulates them directly, with very fine energy, so that you do not watch the images so much as remember them. Remember a thing that never happened to you. The medical officer on your ship, Chakwas, said it delivered in seconds what a simstim would need hours for, to a species whose physiology its builders had never seen. I find that sentence unbearable, and I mean it as praise. They built something that would write into a stranger's head on a guess.

And it is a network node. Do hold on to that, because it changes the whole picture. These were not curiosities left on hilltops; they were points on a system, a kind of galactic extranet, near-instant, running between worlds of an empire. So when you stood in front of the one on Eden Prime, you were not looking at a monument. You were standing at the end of a wire.

Now, I was not there. I want to be exact about my sources, so: the beacon part is yours, mostly, told to me in pieces and never in order, and the rest I rebuilt afterwards from Alliance reports and from my own people, who were quite thorough once they knew it mattered. A shakedown run, which it was not. Nihlus and Anderson telling you so in the comm room, with the dreadnought already hanging over the colony in the footage, and Nihlus going ahead alone, and Saren shooting him in the back near the spaceport, a friend, a fellow Spectre. Jenkins dead in the first minutes to the drones. The husks on those spikes, and I will say that I did not enjoy having that one described to me. Ashley Williams, the only one left of her unit, and a dockworker called Powell by Nihlus's body. The beacon moved from the dig to the spaceport, the tram, the charges you disarmed, and then Kaidan and Ashley walking up to it, and one of them being drawn in, and you throwing them clear and taking their place. You told me that part rather lightly. I did not take it lightly.

Hm. And it is worth saying what it cost, since the geth did sack the colony, and there were hundreds of civilians, and the beacon was by then a wreck anyway. It is why you saw what you saw. Whatever the Protheans on Ilos meant it to be, an orderly warning, a message of hope sent out in the dark to anyone left, what arrived in you was fragments. Fifteen hours unconscious, and nightmares for a long time after. You woke in the med bay with Chakwas fretting, you told Anderson what you had seen, machines slaughtering the living, and the ship turned for the Citadel. And what you had, from that, was evidence against Saren, and the beginning of a hearing nobody wanted to have.

But the question I care about is the reverse one. Why was it unreadable? And here I have to confess to the name.

I called it the cipher. After Jung, I am afraid, and a little too proud of it at the time. The argument is that a Prothean message is not made of images alone; it assumes an enormous amount before it begins. What a figure in a doorway means. What a shape on a horizon feels like to a body with four eyes and a hand of three fingers. What counts as a threat, as a home, as a family, without anyone needing to say. Archetypes, instincts, a shared inheritance. Anything you could say to a Prothean in a moment, they would hear in the room of all the things they already knew. And I do not mean they were being clever. They coded the thing that way deliberately, so that only a Prothean mind could take it, because the Reapers might be listening, and the people on Ilos did not yet understand indoctrination. They did not imagine the one reader who might be a Reaper's creature and still, somehow, understand.

That is the hardest problem in my field. I would put it ahead of dating, ahead of translation. How do you find the context a message assumes when everyone who held that context is dead? A language you can reconstruct, with patience and a good deal of luck. A shared sense of what a doorway is? You cannot dig for it. And it was exactly the thing I had told you could not be had, that it might be impossible. I said so, I believe, rather confidently, on the Normandy, with Kaidan looking at me sideways. I had not met the Thorian yet.

I should say, I was on Feros with you, so this part is mine. I have said I was thinking of getting out. That was true of the lair; it was not true of the colony. Zhu's Hope was something else: the people of that place, Fai Dan among them, moving as if they were one thing, and a man I had spoken to an hour before looking through me. ExoGeni had been running them as a control group, and one representative, Jeong, had known and let it go on. You used the gas; you would not have them killed. Fai Dan, forced to turn on you, shot himself instead, and I have not decided what I think about that. And then the thing in the lair, and the green asari it pushed out to speak for it, wearing a commando's uniform, which is a detail I still find more disturbing than the rest. It could not talk except through them. It had been on Feros long before the Protheans, sleeping for thousands of years and waking for a few frantic centuries, and when they built their city over it, it watched. It took some of them as thralls. It took some of them when they died. And it kept what it ate. Fifty thousand years, a plant at the bottom of a skyscraper, holding the inside of the Protheans' minds.

Shiala, you know. She had been Benezia's acolyte for close to two centuries, and Saren had given her to the thing in exchange for the cipher, and had it from her, and then tried to destroy the Thorian so that you could not follow. He nearly managed it. After you cut the thing down she came out of that pod still herself, and the first thing she did, I remember, was to be sorry about its dying. A unique life, she said, more or less. I did not know what to do with that either. Then she touched your mind, and you came back from it rather shaken, and she told you it would take time.

It did. I want to be honest about the part I am least proud of, which is that I wanted it. I had spent decades on this subject, and here was the one living person who could read the vision, and my first thought, the moment I knew, was not for you. It was: tell me what you see. And you did, as well as you could, and I sat on the edge of that bed, and some of it was colour explained to someone with no eyes, which is the phrase I have always used for it, and some of it was words I knew. I could read the language, now that you could hear it. We were the two halves of an interpreter. And I will say, since I am confessing, that I had touched your mind before, in the meld, and it tired me out, more than anyone else's had; you resist, as humans do, and you resist harder than most, and I have wondered since whether the beacon was not overloaded, whether you simply would not let it in.

So there is the part I cannot make comfortable. A key to a dead people's minds came through a person. One person, at a time, at a cost to that person, and the one other who had it was Saren. A cipher is not a lock that opens for the righteous. It opened for him, and it opened for you, and that is the thing I dislike most about it. Later it let you read Vigil's broken warning on Ilos, and in 2186 it let you watch what Javik's footage showed while I saw only static, and you told me what the pods were doing, and the lifeboats. I was standing beside you and, as I said, saw only noise. I have not entirely forgiven that, either of us.

Shiala wrote to you in the war, you know. She had stayed, and the colony with her, every last one of them; you remember Jeong came round. She said the spores had left them able to feel one another, and that it helped, in a fight. Which is a Thorian's gift to a people it had enslaved, and I cannot say whether that is a mercy or the same hand. I am no longer sure there is a difference worth the argument.

What I wanted to say, though, is that the beacon was not the only message of its kind. There is one I have been circling. A message for someone, in a place that would not let anyone else hear it.

## Cut Out of a Wall

Ilos, yes. I will come to Ilos. But I have just spent a quarter of an hour on messages that would not open for the wrong reader, and I realise I have left out the one place where I was the thing that would not open. Or rather, the thing that could not get out. You know where I am going. Do not smile yet; give me the paragraph first, and then you may smile.

Therum. I want to tell you about it as a dig, because that is what it was before it was a rescue, and nobody, you included, has ever asked me about the dig. They ask about the barrier. Everyone wants the barrier.

So. The planet is rich in heavy metals, which is the reason humanity cares about it at all; a good part of the manufacturing boom on Earth came out of that rock. The mining corporations had been through the surface for years. Their boreholes, though, were the best stratigraphy anyone could have wished for. Core samples, with fossils of simple silicon-based organisms in them, which is how we knew the world had been kinder once, and why the Protheans might have wanted it. I am aware of how that sounds. The people who gutted the site also drew me its cross-section, free, in the course of looking for something else. I used their cores. I used their shafts to get down. I should say that I did not thank them, and I do not think I would have, even now.

And the ruin itself, the tower, was the one thing in that system that had come through. Most of it had been looted before I arrived; that is the rule, not the exception. What was left was a structure that the Protheans built so well that lava had poured into it and it simply stood there. Ashley said as much, later, in her own words, about builders who built to last. She also dared Kaidan to spit over the side of the lift, I am told. I was not present for that part; I was occupied.

Now, the self-mockery, which I have been saving. The system is called Knossos. Named, probably, for the Minoan palace, the one with the labyrinth, and I did not notice the joke until a good while afterwards. An archaeologist goes down into a labyrinth after fifty years of reading about what lived there, hides from the geth, touches the wrong panel, and ends up suspended in a field of light unable to scratch her own nose. I had a doctorate. I had, I believe, a cup of tea going cold on the ledge above. And the thing that finally came for me was a human with a shotgun and a mining laser, which is, if you think about it, the exact profession I had spent my life resenting. The vandals cut me out. I had it in a footnote later, in my notes, and I crossed it out and wrote it again.

What did I think I was looking at, that morning? I thought I was in a research station, a place where the Protheans had been working on something. I would have defended it. Forgive me, I would have argued it with a student. What I know now is rather more humbling. I had no idea what I was in. I knew a barrier had gone up when I brushed the wrong control and the geth came through. I did not know that the ones on the other side of the wall had been, in effect, the same war I was studying, a few thousand years late. Zeioph crypts on Armeni, in the same system. Prothean presence on Therum. Two peoples, at least, who had kept something here, and I assumed the one I had come to study was the only one that mattered. My contacts say the crypts have been picked over too. I am not in a position to confirm it.

Then the tremor. I told you the laser caused it, in the moment, because I wanted to be useful and there was a tremor and a laser. I had no evidence. It is possible the old structure had simply been waiting. It is possible the geth had weakened it. I wrote it down as the laser, and the Council's turian Councilor asked you afterward why you had destroyed a Prothean site, and I let that sit on you rather longer than I should have. That is a debt, and I know it. A tower that had stood for fifty thousand years, which I had reached by lift, and I watched it come down running beside you.

My mother. I have been going around her. Benezia taught philosophy and religion, and she persuaded; that was what she was for, and she believed that if you were patient and clever, you could steer the strong. I went the other way. I went to the dead, who do not answer back. For years I told myself that archaeology was my escape from her, and now I am not so certain. We were doing the same thing. She read the living for what they would not say, and I read what the dead left in the walls. I will say that when Aethyta and I spoke, much later, she told me that Benezia had thought it all rather a joke, the Protheans, and then someone else said she had been protecting me, and I do not know which to believe. I would like to hold the second a little more tightly than the first.

And my theory. Cyclical extinction. I said it on your ship with great conviction, and Kaidan pressed me on whether I was Saren's, and Joker was sarcastic, and I was so very pleased with myself. What I would say now is that the evidence for the cycle was the absence of evidence. There was so little of the Protheans. I read that as someone erasing them. A rival of mine, a patient one, who lives entirely in my head and sounds rather like Dr. Joshawn correcting my dating, says: no. Nothing erased them. Time did, and miners, and looters. Fifty thousand years is plenty. You invented a villain to excuse a thin site. And then you, Shepard, arrived with the villain. I have never forgiven the universe for confirming me. It is deeply bad for the discipline.

Still. Notice how little of a Prothean site is Prothean. Therum: miners, geth, a Krogan Battlemaster who had no business in a seminar room. Kahje, later: a Cerberus Phantom had been there first, and killed the staff, and turned the defences on my shuttle. The Illusive Man denied it afterwards, which tells you what the denial was worth. The Protheans are the least of what is in the ground. The dig is mostly everyone who came after, and I have started to think that is the real subject.

Which is how I come to Ilos. A message left, finally, in a place that kept everyone out.

## The Day the Relay Opened

Ilos, then. But I would like to approach it the way I approach any site, which is from the end of the story, because that is where the site itself begins. So: the day the relay opened. Not our day, theirs. I have reconstructed it so many times that I am a little afraid I have started to remember it, and I should be careful about that, because I was not there, and neither was anyone who could tell me.

What I have is a shape. The Reapers came through the Citadel. Not at the edges, not at some frontier colony where a garrison could hold them for a season while the capital deliberated; they came out at the exact point the Protheans had agreed, collectively, to treat as the centre of everything. The government was gone before anyone knew there was a war. Then the relays went dark, one system cut from the next, and I want you to picture what that means for an empire that has organised itself around those relays: every fleet suddenly alone, every order arriving late or never. And then the part I find hardest to read without a pause. The Citadel held the census, the star charts, the whole ledger of where every Prothean lived. The Reapers did not have to search. They read it. In a day, if the account is right, though I would hedge the day; I would say they read it very quickly.

You will remember what I said about the empire, how I would take the adjectives off one at a time. This is the last of them. The unity, the single governing species and the single military doctrine: all of it was the strength, and all of it was the failure mode, and it was the same feature. A hierarchy that can be beheaded will be. A doctrine that every subject race has learned is a doctrine the enemy needs to learn only once. I do not say it to be clever. I say it because I have sat in front of the Council and watched hierarchies decide that the answer to a threat is more hierarchy, and I could not help counting the days.

Now, Ilos. You have to understand that it was a name in a footnote for most of my life. The Codex compares it to Troy, a world known only second-hand, from references in other ruins, behind a relay that a supernova had knocked out of position some four thousand years before. Universities planned expeditions the long way round and never launched them. The quarian fleet considered it as a settlement and could not find it. I have a painting of it in my apartment on Illium, which is a ridiculous thing to confess; a painting of a place I believed no living person would ever stand in. And then Joker took the Normandy down out of the upper atmosphere at an angle I choose not to describe, with Pressly insisting the landing site was a fifth the size we needed. I will say, for the record, that I closed my eyes. The Mako came out of the cargo bay and we were there.

The surface was rust. I say it for the ones who imagine Prothean worlds as monuments: it was burned, the oxygen too high, fires going on the dark side. A golden age of spires and arches had been turned the colour of old blood. And under it, a bunker the Reapers never found, because the only people who knew the place existed had had every record of it destroyed in the first hour. That is the whole of Ilos's luck. Its protection was that it had been erased from the Protheans' own memory.

Vigil. I have tried several times to say what it was like to walk into that chamber and I keep producing sentences about the equipment. A virtual intelligence, badly degraded, built on the imprint of the facility's chief overseer, a Prothean named Ksad Ishan. It had stopped the Mako with a force field, which was rude, and then spoke to us in a language we could understand, because it had listened to our radio chatter on the way down and translated itself. I would like that to be in the textbooks. It had waited fifty thousand years and its first act was to learn how to be understood.

It told you what you already half knew from the beacon, the cipher, the dreams: that the Citadel was a trap, that the cycle had gone on for far longer than the Protheans, that the Reapers were coming. It said the Protheans had been hunted using their own maps. It said that people had taken in refugees, and some of the refugees had been theirs, in the sense that the Reapers had already got to them. That, I think, is what you had been fighting without a name. It was not new to you. It was new to the rest of us, and to me. And Vigil said, with what I choose to call relief, that none of us were indoctrinated, which meant the plan had worked well enough to produce us.

And this is the story I keep returning to, the one that does not appear in the line about the dozen scientists. Many more went into the pods. The Reapers took centuries; the power did not last centuries. So Vigil, following a contingency that the people in the pods had not necessarily been told about, began turning them off. One at a time, the ones judged inessential. Someone in your squad was horrified, and Vigil defended itself, and I thought: it was doing arithmetic that its makers asked it to do, and they asked because they knew what it would cost. When the Reapers finally withdrew, twelve were left. Twelve is not a species. They understood that at once.

So they did not try to save themselves. They tried to save us, which is to say the ones after. They worked out the keepers, and the signal that the Citadel sends them, and they found a way to alter it so that when Sovereign asked the station to open, nothing happened. They used the Conduit, a small relay built as a prototype in their own experiments, a back door that ran in one direction only, to a statue on the Presidium that the Council had been admiring for thousands of years as art. Garrus said once, I am told, that the keepers never paid it any attention, and Kaidan said it hummed. Everyone had the answer and nobody had the question. And once through, the twelve stayed. There was no door back. Vigil assumed they starved.

You drove the Mako into it. Forty seconds, Colossi on the slope, the thing already closing, and I can tell you what the records say about that passage: that nothing living survives it on foot, that it needed an enclosed vehicle, that you came out on the Presidium on top of a number of geth. I cannot tell you what it was like, since I stayed.

I stayed. I asked Vigil whether I might. You were already leaving. I remember thinking, very clearly, that this was the only conversation with a Prothean I would ever have, and that I should use it, and Vigil politely told me that it had been built to tell us only what bore on stopping the Reapers. I was disappointed, and I am a little ashamed of how disappointed, with the whole galaxy at stake. You waited. I do not think I have thanked you for waiting.

Here is the question I should have asked. Not who they were, or what they ate, or what the language was. I should have asked whether it had worked. Whether Vigil knew. Because the plan was to trap the Reapers in the dark, and they did arrive, and they arrived slowly, at ordinary speeds, which bought us time, and I have heard the phrase "a reprieve." But the scientists had no means of verifying, and neither do we. I do not know whether a plan that bought one cycle a few years is a plan that worked.

That is what I mean when I say the ruin is the only honest witness. A survivor will tell you it worked, because she needs it to have. A Reaper will tell you nothing. The ruin tells you only what was done, and where, and by how many, and then stops, and that is all that I trust anymore: twelve people, a door that opens one way, a statue. It does not tell you whether they were right.

## What the Obelisk Was For

Which is an uncomfortable thing to say, I realise, directly before I start talking about the one set of ruins that did tell us exactly what to do. I am going to be unfair to it, a little. I want to approach it through the Obelisk, because the Obelisk is where I first understood that we were no longer excavating anything.

You will remember the Obelisk of Karza, because you went and got it, off Kopis, after the Hades Nexus was open to anyone who did not mind the neighbourhood. I told you about the finding earlier, so I will not do the finding again. What I did not say is what it was for. A researcher on the Presidium wanted it, a man on a bench whose own team had run out of time, and I assumed, when I first heard, that he wanted it the way I would have wanted it at nineteen: as a Rosetta stone, a key to the language, the thing that lets a thousand datapads be read at last. And it was that. But the Alliance engineers who took it were not interested in poetry. They wanted the technical terms and the measurements, the words for units and tolerances, because they were trying to decrypt a blueprint, and a blueprint is written in the one dialect in which nobody writes anything beautiful. I have spent my life wanting the Protheans to speak, and when I finally got a text they had inscribed on purpose, it was a glossary for building a machine. I was thrilled and offended in roughly equal parts, which is, I notice, a recurring condition of mine.

The same goes for the sphere on Gei Hinnom, the one the refugee in the Docks, Bay E28 if I have the number right, begged a turian guard for food to hear about. You let him sell it to the Alliance; his family ate, and so did some people he did not know. I had a file on him. I have a file on everybody, it is a deformity. And I remember thinking that this was the first time in my career that a piece of Prothean material had a quantified urgency, a clock, and that I could not honestly say which mattered more to me, the man's dinner or the data. I would like to say the dinner. I believe I would have said the dinner. I would also have asked you, afterwards, what the sphere looked like.

And of course Kopis was not new to you. You had been there before, in the Cerberus year, with the Hammerhead, past the dead Blue Suns, and you had read what the expedition left behind: Cayce, O'Loy, the colony O'Loy sold the dig to protect, Cayce deciding that what was still in his head would not survive him. I did not witness any of that. I have the logs, and I cannot tell you on what date they reached me, which tells you how many things were reaching me. But I have wondered since whether anyone on that moon was digging for knowledge at all, or whether they were all being quietly used. That is the wartime question about every site I touched in those years. The dig is not the point. The dig is the thing someone else is waiting to take away.

So. The Crucible. Allow me to be careful, because I have been careful about every number so far and I am not going to stop at the one that matters.

Nobody knows who began it. That is the sentence I would put at the top of any paper, if they let me. The design is the work of countless species, over millions of years, each of which added something and none of which lived to finish. Every one of them was wiped out before the last piece. The Reapers believed all trace of it had been destroyed, and it was not, because it was passed on, secretly, from one cycle to the next, a kind of inheritance that no heir ever saw in full. The Protheans were the latest to try. They actually built the thing, which no one before them had, and then they lost it, not to the Reapers, not at first, but to an argument. One faction wanted to destroy the Reapers. The other wanted to control them, and I will say as plainly as I can that the second faction turned out to be indoctrinated. You know who else has argued that, in our own cycle, at length, with a very good lighting design.

Now, here is where I start to lose my footing, and I would rather you watch it happen. Millions of years. If a cycle is fifty thousand years, give or take, then millions of years is dozens of cycles, a staircase longer than anything I can see the bottom of. But the dates my sources give me for the older civilisations do not fit that arithmetic, and I will not pretend they do. Some of what I know about whoever came before the Protheans I know from pages I would call, very generously, unreliable. I have a source I trust, and a source I do not, and the two disagree about whether the cycles were even regular. So when I say countless species, I mean it as a confession of the count. I mean that I cannot give you the number, and I distrust anyone who can.

Which brings me to Mars, and I should admit what it did to my sense of the thing I was doing there.

The Mars archive was a dig. Humanity had been studying it for decades, and the people who designed the Alliance's base there, a low circle with a shaft down the middle, built to survive the sandstorms and looking, I am told, like a very clean factory, had clearly never been told it was a place anyone should be afraid of. I went because I thought it was a library. Kahje first, actually: the shrine the hanar keep under the water, with Quoyle wounded and sending me on ahead, and a set of Prothean encryption keys, and a hologram of the Illusive Man who denied sending the Phantom and offered me an alliance. I asked him to share what he knew first. He stalled, and I cut the link and told him to send an army next time, which I have been pleased with ever since, and which was, I am aware, a very small victory. Then I took the keys to Mars, to see what they opened.

## The Thing That Was There Before

Whether you ever were, I said. Well. I would like to take that back by one step, and go and stand in the place where the letter does not begin, because there is a part of it I skipped, and it is the part that has been keeping me awake in the evenings when I am supposed to be doing something else.

Every Prothean record of the Reapers that I ever held, every beacon, every line of Vigil, the whole file of what Javik said and did not say, is a record from the middle of a story. I do not think I had noticed how much that sentence weighs. They gave the Reapers a name, you know. "Reapers" is the Protheans' word, a harvesters' word, and the geth have their own, and neither of them is what the things call themselves, and I realised some time ago that nobody can tell me what the things called themselves, because nobody who ever met them was standing at the beginning. If I try to be fair to them, which I do, and which I find costs me more each year, then what they left us is the testimony of a witness who walked into the second act and swore to the whole play.

So, a corpse. I want to start with the corpse, because it is the only piece of this that was ever, in the proper sense, found.

In 2163, a batarian survey team was working the Dis system and found something lying in a crater on a world called Jartar. Hot, silicates, a trace of krypton and xenon in the air, a rock that nobody would give a second look. At the bottom of the crater was a body. A billion. I have spent my life on fifty thousand and been called ambitious. They had almost no time to look at it, because shortly afterward a batarian dreadnought arrived in the system, and shortly after that the crater was empty. And then the Hegemony did the thing governments do, which is deny it with a loud voice, and the louder, the more the salarians put documentation on the table. That is the dig. Now the trail you followed, and I will tread lightly, because you are about to tell me what I missed, and you will be right.

I did not witness it. Then Mahavid, where a man who was not Garneau walked into the mine and took himself and the husks with him, and the staff came out of their ten years of thralldom thinking it was 2176. It is one Leviathan, speaking inside your head. That is testimony, Shepard. It is not a stratum. And the dates. A billion years, and the Reapers fifty thousand at a time. Do you see why I cannot sleep? If I divide, I do not get a number a person could wear. I get something like twenty thousand cycles, and I do not believe in twenty thousand, and I do not believe in a clean answer either; my sources will tell me one thing about the age of the dead and another about the age of the dying and I have chosen, for the moment, not to reconcile them. What did he ask you for, in return? Because nobody does anything for nothing, and a race that had spent a billion years hiding had a very good reason to stop.

## The Arithmetic of a Cycle

You have not answered that, and I am going to let it stand, because I have been promising myself for a week that I would be rude about numbers in front of someone who cannot leave, and you are sitting very still.

So. Arithmetic. I will do it the way I do it at night, which is with two of me, one at each end of a table, and I will tell you which end is speaking, because they do not agree.

My sources say the inusannon were out among the stars at least a hundred and twenty-seven thousand years ago. At least. Hold on to that, I will come back to it. It says the Protheans reached spaceflight around sixty-eight thousand years before our calendar and fell around forty-eight, after some centuries of losing. Sixty-eight to forty-eight is twenty thousand years, which is a fine, respectable Prothean empire, and a hundred and twenty-seven to forty-eight is seventy-nine thousand, which is a cycle and a half. Nobody has a cycle and a half. A cycle is fifty thousand, everyone has said so since Vigil, and the other half is where the table starts to shake.

The first of me says: it fits, you are being tiresome. Put the inusannon's end at roughly ninety-eight thousand, one clean harvest before the Protheans, and the Protheans find the ruins and learn mass effect physics, the account says tens of thousands of years later, and thirty is tens of thousands, and the empire rises on inherited tools exactly where it ought to. Lovely. And I would believe her, except that I notice what she did. She took a floor and made it a date. The inusannon fought the thoi'han over Eingana a hundred and twenty-seven thousand years ago, so that is when they were alive and arguing; the Reapers wiped them out "eventually," which is the word the account uses, and she has quietly decided that eventually means about thirty thousand years, because that is the number that makes the staircase even. The other of me says: then you have chosen the reading that flatters your arithmetic, and the honest one is that the end of the inusannon sits anywhere between a hundred and twenty-seven thousand and the Protheans' own first light, and a gap of fifty-nine thousand years in which the ruins sat there untouched while a whole harvest either happened and left nothing, or did not happen. And she is right, and I have no better answer than that I would like to have a better one.

Mm. It is worse than that, actually, and I do not know whether to be amused. The chronology in my files gives the Eingana war as a hundred and twenty-five thousand BCE, and the entries on Eingana and the inusannon say a hundred and twenty-seven thousand years ago. It is the same figure. Somebody said it aloud in 2185, I suspect, a clerk subtracted the present, and now there are two numbers in two places, and people quote the one that suits them. The same chronology says outright that huge dates should not be taken literally, that "nearly a billion" does not mean a billion less a few thousand, and I am grateful for the honesty and I wish it had not been tucked away where nobody reads. Every figure I am quarrelling with is a rounded sentence somebody once said into a recorder.

Let me do what I do and lay out where I think the gap could live, and I will not choose, so do not wait for it.

One. We missed a cycle. Between the inusannon and the Protheans a harvest came and went, and left no more than the harvests we have not found. I like this one for being modest. It also happens to be the one the method predicts, which should worry you as much as it worries me.

Two. The cycle is not fifty thousand. Here I stand on something I do know: my sources say the Reapers built the relays and the Citadel to speed up the time between harvests. To speed it up. Whatever they were, the intervals before the relays were longer, perhaps by a great deal, and fifty thousand is what the machinery settles into when it is working properly, not a law of nature. And then, in our own cycle, the Protheans on Ilos reached the Citadel and altered the signal the keepers wait for, and I do not know what that did to the clock. A delay is a delay. If we are living in a stretched interval, then the true period is shorter than the one we think we measured, and everything before it shifts.

Three. The dates are bad. I said that to you earlier of one source and I will say it of all of them, including the ones I like. The Fehl Prime device is dated to "as early as" sixty-eight thousand, as early as, and there is a very long way from the earliest a thing could be to when it was.

Four. It was never a schedule. Chorban, the salarian, the one on the Citadel nobody listened to, sent you an email, which I find very funny, since you went and scanned every last keeper because you were told to be thorough. His finding was that the keepers are set to react to a signal about every fifty thousand years, and the last time coincided with the Protheans' end. That is the only instrument in the entire record that measures the interval directly, and the thing it measures is the keepers, which are the Reapers' own instrument. So what I have is the Reapers' behaviour reported by the Reapers' furniture. Sovereign spent, Legion told you, more than a thousand years going about waking allies for something it called restarting the cycle, and you do not restart a clock. You restart a decision.

I would like you to see the trap in all four, because it is the same trap. If a harvest destroys the evidence of the harvests before it, then the absence of evidence confirms the theory and the presence of evidence confirms it too, and an archaeologist who cannot be wrong has stopped being one. I can say "there was a cycle here, and it was cleaned" about anywhere. What I actually have is a list of things the cleaning missed, which is a very particular list: a planet pulled to pieces by two fleets' drive cores, a rift valley on Klendagon cut by a round fired at a Reaper thirty-seven million years ago, craters on Etamis, a corpse in a crater that someone left alone for nearly a billion years. And the Thorian. I was on Feros, you will remember, I stood next to you, and I have never quite said it to you plainly: that was the only living thing in the galaxy that sat through the Protheans' end and kept growing for fifty thousand years, and Shiala mourned it, and I mourned, privately, a witness, and you do have a habit of ending my best sources. I keep a list.

Everything on that list survived because the harvest did not care, or could not see. Those are not a sample of the cycles. They are the cycles' blind spot, and I cannot count the gaps in a wall by the bricks that were left.

Now the Leviathan, and I will be brief, because I owe the last conversation more rigour than a second helping of it. If what you were told is true then the Reapers began a billion years ago and made more of themselves through millions of years, and the relay network came afterward, to hurry things. Then the first cycles were not fifty thousand of anything. They were an unmeasured age, and the number of them depends on whether a harvest produces one new Reaper or one for each species taken, and my sources disagree about that, one saying a Reaper is a nation of a single people and another saying each harvest ends with a birth in Harbinger's image. Count the Reapers and you have counted species. Count the harvests and you have counted something else, and no one has been able to tell me whether those numbers are even in the same order of magnitude. Etamis has an entry that does the sum for us: if the Etamisians were harvested then it was four to eight hundred cycles ago. That sum has the sort of confidence that makes me want to leave the room. It divides one guess by another and writes the answer in a trivia box.

The one thing that crosses the gaps is the Crucible, and that is why I find I cannot be as sceptical as I would like. A blueprint handed from one cycle to the next, as I said earlier; it lay for fifty thousand years under Mars. I took the Kahje keys to it, and you and I kept it out of Eva Coré's hands, at a price Kaidan paid. Hackett paid for it, the shipyards built it, Vendetta told us the missing piece, the Catalyst, was the Citadel itself, and the Illusive Man ran there and told the Reapers where to look. That is all true and all was the world ending, and I want to stand in the part nobody has asked about: a chain of custody. A blueprint that survived that long means there was a continuous line of hands. And a continuous line of hands does not fit a harvest that wipes everything, unless the Reapers' ledger of what they destroyed is as wrong as ours. I find that a comfort, and I find it a very small and unscientific one.

So. If I had a lab and a decade — I know. I know. I have neither, and you need not say so; you would be right about the decade.

I would date the oldest relay first. Not the Citadel, not Ilos. The oldest known relay in the galaxy was the Alpha, in Bahak, and Kenson's team did not call it that for nothing: her paper said most relays are far older than fifty thousand years, tested by the dust held in their gravity wells, and the asari have run something like it and abandoned it, and a woman at Serrice asked, rather beautifully, where that species is now. I wanted that dust. I know exactly why the Alpha is gone, and I know what you carried, and I would not undo it. I would only like to have had the sample. After that, the debris on Eingana, because two civilisations broke their drive cores over one world in a single war, and that is a debris layer with a before and an after, which is the nearest thing to a clean datum this subject has ever offered me. After that, the corpse on Jartar, if I could ever get the Hegemony to say what it did with it. I would like to measure three things that do not know about one another, and see whether any two of them agree.

You have the look of someone about to ask which of the four I would bet on. I will not. But tell me, Shepard, since you are the only one who has stood in the room with all of them: what would you have dated first?

## What the Record Is For

You are still not going to tell me what you would have dated first, and I have decided to count that as a vote for the Alpha relay, since nobody has ever voted against it. Good. Put the dust away. I have one more thing, and it is the one I have been avoiding all evening by being rigorous at you for an hour, which is a technique I learned from the best scholars on Serrice and would not recommend.

It is the book.

You know about the book. Everyone on the ship knows about the book, because I have been sketching titles for it since before you died the first time, and Glyph has a folder, and Joker has twice asked it to read the list aloud in the cockpit in a voice I am told is not at all like mine. So. Let me be honest about the titles, because they are the most honest thing I have written.

The first one was "The Protheans." Just that. It is what a young woman with a doctorate and a great deal of confidence writes on a blank page, a title that is also a claim: here is the subject, it is closed, you may now be told about it. I rejected it because I had, by then, stood in a ruin on Therum that turned out to be mostly other people's looting, and the title had no room in it for that. Then "The Prothean Empire," which is better, and which I rejected the day Javik walked off a pod and informed me that the word I was using for it was too polite. Then "A Living Legacy," which I think I wrote on a very good day, and which I now find so embarrassing that I will say only this: I was fond of the word living, and I had not yet met anything that was. There is one on the list that simply reads, I Despise Thinking Up Titles For My Own Academic Research, and I left it in, because it is the only one that has never been wrong. And then, last, the one he made me write down. "Journeys with the Prothean." Mm. I want you to know I dislike that title. I dislike it in the particular way you dislike a thing that is correct. He has agreed to consider it, which from Javik is a binding contract, and I have agreed to consider it back, which from me is a sworn deposition.

What I did not expect, when I started the list, was that the title would keep changing because the subject kept changing. I had a species in mind, wise and patient, keepers of wisdom, and what I had on the table was a people who were frightened and good at it, who built an empire out of other people's fear and then spent their last centuries losing a war in a very organised fashion. I put that in my private notes, the ones the Broker's terminal keeps for me, and the entry I reread most is not the angry one. It is a short one, where I admit I had romanticised a whole species out of a few fragments and a great deal of longing, and that the fault was mine and not theirs. I would like the next reader to find that entry first. Before the dates, before the arithmetic, before anyone's quarrel with the inusannon.

Now. Here is what I have actually concluded, and I have been walking toward it all lecture.

Everything the Protheans left that is still standing is a thing that was meant to be found. Not preserved, found. That is a different kind of object. A beacon is a message in a form that a particular mind could receive, and they coded it so only a Prothean mind would understand it, because they did not want the Reapers reading it, which was sensible, and which also meant that when the message reached you it arrived as a handful of images and fifteen hours lying on a floor. I have told you that I once thought that was a mistake. It was a lock with the key in a drawer on Feros, and the drawer was Shiala, and a Thorian had to be dealt with before she opened it. Vigil sat in the dark under Ilos for fifty thousand years, patient to the point of insult, waiting for one visitor, and he is the clearest example I have: a mind built to be addressed. The cache under Mars was an observation post, a small room, a lot of data, no obvious grandeur, and it did more to the human species in a few decades than the whole of the rest of the Protheans' rubble did to anybody. Because it was found by people who could use it.

And then I think of everything on the other side of the ledger. The asari kept a beacon in a statue and told no one for the whole of our recorded history, including the Council that had made hiding it illegal, and it did us precisely as much good as a sealed letter does. It took Vendetta, and the temple, and a great deal going wrong, to make the thing speak. Vendetta sat in that statue among the pilgrims and said nothing to any of them until you walked in with the right artefacts. A record is not a pile of facts, it is an open door that has to be walked through by someone who has been taught how the door works. So if I leave anything, it will not be a very large archive. I will leave a short one, addressed to a particular stranger, with the key attached where they will trip on it.

I did that, as it happens. You know about the capsule. I sat down in the evenings, between operations, and tried to write it for somebody I could not picture, and I found I could not write for posterity at all. Posterity is a very bad audience. It does not lean forward. I wrote it for whoever would be sitting where I was sitting when I found the Mars cache, tired and a little frightened and holding an incomplete thing, and who would need to be told what we had actually been like. Not our dates. Us.

Which is where I owe you the part I have been dreading, because I want the next reader to know about this cycle, and about you, and I am going to say it badly, sideways, and with a joke at my own expense, since that is the only form in which I can say it at all.

I have the file on the Citadel. Of course I do. After the coup, after the party, when somebody had been wearing your face around the Wards. I have Glyph's account of the Archives, delivered in the tone of a small drone that would like it understood it was not the one who got locked in a vault, and I have Traynor's, which is shorter and a good deal more pointed, because it was Traynor the false orders were sent to, in your voice, to ready the ship for launch. The Spectre codes were a copy of a copy. Brooks, who had assembled the dossiers once, for the other mission, and had stopped believing in the man she worked for, had woken a thing that had been kept in a tank as spare parts, and let it learn to behave like a person from a set of implants. It thought itself the real one, freed of everything that makes you slow: the friends, the guilt, the people who wait. It shut you in iridium and went for the docks, and Glyph, who I will concede may be in the right about itself, let you out, and it was settled at the docks, where you tried to hold on to him and he let go. That is as far as I have it. I was not in that room. I heard it afterwards, in pieces, from people who each said it differently, which is how I know it happened.

Here is the joke. I have spent fifty years on a record of a vanished people, and the entire time I believed that the thing I wanted was a more complete record. More fragments, more cipher, a bigger dig. And the clearest lesson I have ever been handed about records came from a man grown in a tank who had every one of your files, every code, every habit, the walk, the voice, the whole dossier. Perfect copy. Better preserved than anything I have ever pulled out of a wall. And he was nothing like you, because the part of you that mattered was not in the file. It was in what happens to you when somebody is in trouble. I am an archaeologist of some distinction, and I needed a clone to tell me that the artefact is not the point. I would like it recorded that I find this humiliating, and that I have put it in a footnote, and that the footnote has a footnote.

Which is what Javik meant, I think, though he would be appalled to hear me say he meant anything gentle. He said something of the sort once, in the tone he uses for the weather. I have lost the wording, which is unlike me; the sense was that a record means nothing unless someone is moved by it, and that his people had kept very good records. He was the proof, a perfectly good archive of a civilisation, standing in my cargo hold, complaining about the food. It was meant to be a cruelty. I decided to take it as an instruction, which is the only way anybody on this ship has ever gotten anything out of him.

So this is my answer, and then I will stop, because the lights are doing the thing that means we have overrun.

He is right that a record that moves no one is a very good way of being forgotten. But I do not think the moving is separate from the keeping. It is the keeping. Every Prothean thing that lasted was built to move someone, to be found by the one who could carry it, and the ones that moved nobody, the sealed ones, the withheld ones, are the ones that failed. So if the book is ever finished, it will not be a history of the Protheans. It will be a letter, in the way the beacon was a letter, to someone who is tired and halfway through something. It will be about what a cycle is like from inside, and the thing it will say is the one I could not get into any of the titles. That nobody here was certain. That we argued, and ate badly, and kept a drone named Glyph, and drank something Garrus called calibrations. That we were frightened and did it anyway, and that the person at the middle of it, whom I am addressing and who is looking very uncomfortable at being addressed, never once behaved as though he was in a record.

I think that might be the title, actually. I have never said that aloud. Do not tell Javik, he will want his name on the first line.

There. I have run over by twenty minutes. I had a better ending, and I will have it five minutes from now.
