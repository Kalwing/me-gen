# Liara — Mass Effect (Part 3 of 3)

## The Day the Relay Opened

Ilos, then. But I would like to approach it the way I approach any site, which is from the end of the story, because that is where the site itself begins. So: the day the relay opened. Not our day, theirs. I have reconstructed it so many times that I am a little afraid I have started to remember it, and I should be careful about that, because I was not there, and neither was anyone who could tell me.

What I have is a shape. The Reapers came through the Citadel. Not at the edges, not at some frontier colony where a garrison could hold them for a season while the capital deliberated; they came out at the exact point the Protheans had agreed, collectively, to treat as the centre of everything. The government was gone before anyone knew there was a war. Then the relays went dark, one system cut from the next, and I want you to picture what that means for an empire that has organised itself around those relays: every fleet suddenly alone, every order arriving late or never. And then the part I find hardest to read without a pause. The Citadel held the census, the star charts, the whole ledger of where every Prothean lived. The Reapers did not have to search. They read it. In a day, if the account is right, though I would hedge the day; I would say they read it very quickly.

You will remember what I said about the empire, how I would take the adjectives off one at a time. This is the last of them. The unity, the single governing species and the single military doctrine: all of it was the strength, and all of it was the failure mode, and it was the same feature. A hierarchy that can be beheaded will be. A doctrine that every subject race has learned is a doctrine the enemy needs to learn only once. I do not say it to be clever. I say it because I have sat in front of the Council and watched hierarchies decide that the answer to a threat is more hierarchy, and I could not help counting the days.

Now, Ilos. You have to understand that it was a name in a footnote for most of my life. The Codex compares it to Troy, a world known only second-hand, from references in other ruins, behind a relay that a supernova had knocked out of position some four thousand years before. Universities planned expeditions the long way round and never launched them. The quarian fleet considered it as a settlement and could not find it. I have a painting of it in my apartment on Illium, which is a ridiculous thing to confess; a painting of a place I believed no living person would ever stand in. And then Joker took the Normandy down out of the upper atmosphere at an angle I choose not to describe, with Pressly insisting the landing site was a fifth the size we needed. I will say, for the record, that I closed my eyes. The Mako came out of the cargo bay and we were there.

The surface was rust. I say it for the ones who imagine Prothean worlds as monuments: it was burned, the oxygen too high, fires going on the dark side. A golden age of spires and arches had been turned the colour of old blood. And under it, a bunker the Reapers never found, because the only people who knew the place existed had had every record of it destroyed in the first hour. That is the whole of Ilos's luck. Its protection was that it had been erased from the Protheans' own memory.

Vigil. I have tried several times to say what it was like to walk into that chamber and I keep producing sentences about the equipment. A virtual intelligence, badly degraded, built on the imprint of the facility's chief overseer, a Prothean named Ksad Ishan. It had stopped the Mako with a force field, which was rude, and then spoke to us in a language we could understand, because it had listened to our radio chatter on the way down and translated itself. I would like that to be in the textbooks. It had waited fifty thousand years and its first act was to learn how to be understood.

It told you what you already half knew from the beacon, the cipher, the dreams: that the Citadel was a trap, that the cycle had gone on for far longer than the Protheans, that the Reapers were coming. It said the Protheans had been hunted using their own maps. It said that people had taken in refugees, and some of the refugees had been theirs, in the sense that the Reapers had already got to them. That, I think, is what you had been fighting without a name. It was not new to you. It was new to the rest of us, and to me. And Vigil said, with what I choose to call relief, that none of us were indoctrinated, which meant the plan had worked well enough to produce us.

And this is the story I keep returning to, the one that does not appear in the line about the dozen scientists. Many more went into the pods. The Reapers took centuries; the power did not last centuries. So Vigil, following a contingency that the people in the pods had not necessarily been told about, began turning them off. One at a time, the ones judged inessential. Someone in your squad was horrified, and Vigil defended itself, and I thought: it was doing arithmetic that its makers asked it to do, and they asked because they knew what it would cost. When the Reapers finally withdrew, twelve were left. Twelve is not a species. They understood that at once.

So they did not try to save themselves. They tried to save us, which is to say the ones after. They worked out the keepers, and the signal that the Citadel sends them, and they found a way to alter it so that when Sovereign asked the station to open, nothing happened. They used the Conduit, a small relay built as a prototype in their own experiments, a back door that ran in one direction only, to a statue on the Presidium that the Council had been admiring for thousands of years as art. Garrus said once, I am told, that the keepers never paid it any attention, and Kaidan said it hummed. Everyone had the answer and nobody had the question. And once through, the twelve stayed. There was no door back. Vigil assumed they starved.

You drove the Mako into it. Forty seconds, Colossi on the slope, the thing already closing, and I can tell you what the records say about that passage: that nothing living survives it on foot, that it needed an enclosed vehicle, that you came out on the Presidium on top of a number of geth. I cannot tell you what it was like, since I stayed.

I stayed. I asked Vigil whether I might. You were already leaving. I remember thinking, very clearly, that this was the only conversation with a Prothean I would ever have, and that I should use it, and Vigil politely told me that it had been built to tell us only what bore on stopping the Reapers. I was disappointed, and I am a little ashamed of how disappointed, with the whole galaxy at stake. You waited. I do not think I have thanked you for waiting.

Here is the question I should have asked. Not who they were, or what they ate, or what the language was. I should have asked whether it had worked. Whether Vigil knew. Because the plan was to trap the Reapers in the dark, and they did arrive, and they arrived slowly, at ordinary speeds, which bought us time, and I have heard the phrase "a reprieve." But the scientists had no means of verifying, and neither do we. I do not know whether a plan that bought one cycle a few years is a plan that worked.

That is what I mean when I say the ruin is the only honest witness. A survivor will tell you it worked, because she needs it to have. A Reaper will tell you nothing. The ruin tells you only what was done, and where, and by how many, and then stops, and that is all that I trust anymore: twelve people, a door that opens one way, a statue. It does not tell you whether they were right.

## What the Obelisk Was For

Which is an uncomfortable thing to say, I realise, directly before I start talking about the one set of ruins that did tell us exactly what to do. I am going to be unfair to it, a little. I want to approach it through the Obelisk, because the Obelisk is where I first understood that we were no longer excavating anything.

You will remember the Obelisk of Karza, because you went and got it, off Kopis, after the Hades Nexus was open to anyone who did not mind the neighbourhood. I told you about the finding earlier, so I will not do the finding again. What I did not say is what it was for. A researcher on the Presidium wanted it, a man on a bench whose own team had run out of time, and I assumed, when I first heard, that he wanted it the way I would have wanted it at nineteen: as a Rosetta stone, a key to the language, the thing that lets a thousand datapads be read at last. And it was that. But the Alliance engineers who took it were not interested in poetry. They wanted the technical terms and the measurements, the words for units and tolerances, because they were trying to decrypt a blueprint, and a blueprint is written in the one dialect in which nobody writes anything beautiful. I have spent my life wanting the Protheans to speak, and when I finally got a text they had inscribed on purpose, it was a glossary for building a machine. I was thrilled and offended in roughly equal parts, which is, I notice, a recurring condition of mine.

The same goes for the sphere on Gei Hinnom, the one the refugee in the Docks, Bay E28 if I have the number right, begged a turian guard for food to hear about. You let him sell it to the Alliance; his family ate, and so did some people he did not know. I had a file on him. I have a file on everybody, it is a deformity. And I remember thinking that this was the first time in my career that a piece of Prothean material had a quantified urgency, a clock, and that I could not honestly say which mattered more to me, the man's dinner or the data. I would like to say the dinner. I believe I would have said the dinner. I would also have asked you, afterwards, what the sphere looked like.

And of course Kopis was not new to you. You had been there before, in the Cerberus year, with the Hammerhead, past the dead Blue Suns, and you had read what the expedition left behind: Cayce, O'Loy, the colony O'Loy sold the dig to protect, Cayce deciding that what was still in his head would not survive him. I did not witness any of that. I have the logs, and I cannot tell you on what date they reached me, which tells you how many things were reaching me. But I have wondered since whether anyone on that moon was digging for knowledge at all, or whether they were all being quietly used. That is the wartime question about every site I touched in those years. The dig is not the point. The dig is the thing someone else is waiting to take away.

So. The Crucible. Allow me to be careful, because I have been careful about every number so far and I am not going to stop at the one that matters.

Nobody knows who began it. That is the sentence I would put at the top of any paper, if they let me. The design is the work of countless species, over millions of years, each of which added something and none of which lived to finish. Every one of them was wiped out before the last piece. The Reapers believed all trace of it had been destroyed, and it was not, because it was passed on, secretly, from one cycle to the next, a kind of inheritance that no heir ever saw in full. The Protheans were the latest to try. They actually built the thing, which no one before them had, and then they lost it, not to the Reapers, not at first, but to an argument. One faction wanted to destroy the Reapers. The other wanted to control them, and I will say as plainly as I can that the second faction turned out to be indoctrinated. You know who else has argued that, in our own cycle, at length, with a very good lighting design.

Now, here is where I start to lose my footing, and I would rather you watch it happen. Millions of years. If a cycle is fifty thousand years, give or take, then millions of years is dozens of cycles, a staircase longer than anything I can see the bottom of. But the dates my sources give me for the older civilisations do not fit that arithmetic, and I will not pretend they do. Some of what I know about whoever came before the Protheans I know from pages I would call, very generously, unreliable. I have a source I trust, and a source I do not, and the two disagree about whether the cycles were even regular. So when I say countless species, I mean it as a confession of the count. I mean that I cannot give you the number, and I distrust anyone who can.

Which brings me to Mars, and I should admit what it did to my sense of the thing I was doing there.

The Mars archive was a dig. Humanity had been studying it for decades, and the people who designed the Alliance's base there, a low circle with a shaft down the middle, built to survive the sandstorms and looking, I am told, like a very clean factory, had clearly never been told it was a place anyone should be afraid of. I went because I thought it was a library. Kahje first, actually: the shrine the hanar keep under the water, with Quoyle wounded and sending me on ahead, and a set of Prothean encryption keys, and a hologram of the Illusive Man who denied sending the Phantom and offered me an alliance. I asked him to share what he knew first. He stalled, and I cut the link and told him to send an army next time, which I have been pleased with ever since, and which was, I am aware, a very small victory. Then I took the keys to Mars, to see what they opened.

They opened the plans. That was the discovery, and I knew it the moment I saw them, though I confess I did not understand them. They had been sitting there for fifty thousand years, under a planet that would one day belong to the people they were never going to meet. And what happened to the Mars dig, in my head, in the span of one afternoon, was that it stopped being an excavation. A library is what you find. This was what someone had left, deliberately, in a place they hoped would be found by a species that did not yet exist. I had gone there to dig. I had been, without knowing it, collecting a deposit.

You came, and then Eva Coré, whom the Illusive Man sent, a Cerberus mole, a woman who turned out to be an infiltration unit, and who wiped the archive as she took it. There was a chase. Vega put something into her shuttle, I have heard a truck, I have heard a shuttle, he will tell you whichever one is more flattering. You shot her down with the plans intact, and Kaidan paid for it, and went off to Huerta Memorial on the Citadel, and I have never quite forgiven myself for the ordinary, procedural way I noted it in my head that afternoon. Her body went to EDI, in time. I think about that more than I can reasonably defend.

I then stood in front of the Council, with the data, and watched the three of them decline to commit. They were not stupid. They had homeworlds under attack. But I remember thinking that every one of them was behaving exactly as the Protheans' separatists had behaved, only not indoctrinated, merely frightened, and that I could not tell the difference from the outside.

After that, Hackett. Earth fell, and he authorised the thing, every shipyard in the galaxy, each species building a section. Mordin wrote to a colleague, a message that came into my terminal, I read everything, to say that the Alliance engineers were following the Mars plans so slavishly that he doubted they needed half of the scaffolding. He was looking forward to correcting them. I kept that message longer than I kept most of my files. The scientists could tell that the device used mass relay technology and could not tell how it worked, and that it needed a final piece, the Catalyst. I had ExoGeni's people on Feros contributing, because the Feros ruins were exactly the sort of scrap the project could use, and I had been on Feros, with you, in a place that was supposed to have given up its secrets and had not. I had Conrad Verner's dissertation on dark energy and time, which helped the engineers read the Prothean instructions, and which I want you to know exists partly because you were patient with him.

Thessia gave us the rest. Vendetta, in the beacon, in the Temple of Athame: modelled on the overseer of the Prothean Crucible project, a man called Pashek Vran. He told you, and I heard it, that the Crucible was not Prothean in origin, that the pattern he saw across cycles was too regular to be accident, that the Reapers were its servants and not its authors. I will not claim to remember it as he said it. I remember that he said the cycle had reached its terminus, and that you told him to wait. Then Kai Leng took him, the Illusive Man broke what was in him, and learned that the Catalyst was the Citadel. He fled there. He told the Reapers. They moved the station to Earth. Hackett sent the fleets, and the Crucible followed behind a token defence force, to dock with it, and what it did there I will leave out, tonight. You were there. You do not need me to narrate it.

What I want to leave you with is the feeling. I have had it for months and I do not trust it. When I put the pieces side by side — a beacon on Eden Prime that fastened on you, a Cipher that made you readable to a beacon, a plan sitting for fifty thousand years under Mars, a VI whose maker had known to withhold the one thing that would get them found — it all arranges itself into a letter addressed to you. To this cycle. To us. And I have spent my whole life correcting people who read the past as a message to themselves. The hanar do it. The asari have done it, under a goddess. The Protheans did not write to you. They wrote to whoever came next, and you are merely who arrived. But I am not entirely able to make myself believe that, and I wonder, Shepard, whether you ever were.

## The Thing That Was There Before

Whether you ever were, I said. Well. I would like to take that back by one step, and go and stand in the place where the letter does not begin, because there is a part of it I skipped, and it is the part that has been keeping me awake in the evenings when I am supposed to be doing something else.

Every Prothean record of the Reapers that I ever held, every beacon, every line of Vigil, the whole file of what Javik said and did not say, is a record from the middle of a story. I do not think I had noticed how much that sentence weighs. They gave the Reapers a name, you know. "Reapers" is the Protheans' word, a harvesters' word, and the geth have their own, and neither of them is what the things call themselves, and I realised some time ago that nobody can tell me what the things called themselves, because nobody who ever met them was standing at the beginning. The Protheans arrived when the machine was already running. They did not know it had makers. They could not have known it. If I try to be fair to them, which I do, and which I find costs me more each year, then what they left us is the testimony of a witness who walked into the second act and swore to the whole play.

So, a corpse. I want to start with the corpse, because it is the only piece of this that was ever, in the proper sense, found.

In 2163, a batarian survey team was working the Dis system and found something lying in a crater on a world called Jartar. Hot, silicates, a trace of krypton and xenon in the air, a rock that nobody would give a second look. At the bottom of the crater was a body. A gigantic body, and the first guess was that it had been a starship, a genetically engineered one, organic, unlike anything in known space, and the dating put it at nearly a billion years old. A billion. I have spent my life on fifty thousand and been called ambitious. They had almost no time to look at it, because shortly afterward a batarian dreadnought arrived in the system, and shortly after that the crater was empty. And then the Hegemony did the thing governments do, which is deny it with a loud voice, and the louder, the more the salarians put documentation on the table. The salarian researchers had made their records before it vanished. I wish I knew who they were, by the way, and I have a feeling that one day I will, because I have not stopped looking. The Hegemony said there had never been anything there, and the more they were shown, the more vigorously they said it. That is not a denial. That is a flinch. I know the sound.

What happened to it afterward, I have in two pieces, and I will tell you which is which. From my files on the Hegemony, and I will not tell you whose hands those files passed through, I have that the body went to Khar'shan, which agrees with the batarian who told you about it, the one who tried to drop an asteroid on Terra Nova and whom you let walk away alive, because you would not risk the hostages, and who I think said it in so many words: that the thing was a dead Reaper. I was not in the room, so take that as what I received from you and from my own traffic, which agree, which is not the same as being certain. After Terra Nova failed they pushed the research harder. Scientists, officials, the kind of people who hold the keys, spent years in the presence of that corpse, and a corpse can still do this, and the result was a government that, when the Reapers came in 2186, had its own defences quietly pulled out from under it by its own officers. Days. They were overrun in days. I have thought about the batarians more since than I did when I was young and certain, and I notice it is mostly the Hegemony's own people who paid for that, not its enemies.

That is the dig. Now the trail you followed, and I will tread lightly, because you are about to tell me what I missed, and you will be right.

I did not witness it. I had Garret Bryson in my files before I had him from you: Task Force Aurora, Hackett's office, a small group sent to chase the legends about the Reapers from before anyone believed in them. Bryson's actual interest was never the corpse, and the Alliance records make that plain; the corpse was a clue, and what he wanted was whatever had killed it. He believed that the Reapers were trailing his teams, as if they were looking for the same thing. I had that in a log, you had it in a lab with a man dying in front of you, and I have come round to the opinion that yours is the better source. His assistant, Derek Hadley, shot him, and then there was the drawing, a crayon drawing of his daughter's with the keys hidden in it, kept close, for years. I have rarely envied the Alliance anything, but I envy him that. A man who hid a decryption key in a child's picture. I would have kept my keys in a better place, and been worse for it.

Then Mahavid, where a man who was not Garneau walked into the mine and took himself and the husks with him, and the staff came out of their ten years of thralldom thinking it was 2176. An asari miner told you it was 2176. I have asked myself what I would have said, and I think I would have asked the date three times and believed none of the answers. Then Namakli, where most of a team was dead and Ann Bryson alive, with her theory about the artifacts and the rachni, which I heard about afterward; I was not in that lab. Despoina, an ocean world, a shuttle that would not fly and a dive of some three thousand metres that you made on your own, and what you met there.

What you told me about it was this, in roughly the order you told it, and you will correct me. A race of enormous aquatic minds who ruled the galaxy before the Reapers and ruled everyone else through their thralls, the way a tide rules a beach. Their thralls built machines and the machines turned on their makers, again and again, and so the Leviathans built an Intelligence to protect life at any price, and the Intelligence concluded that the price was the Leviathans. It made the first Reaper out of them, in their image, and called that Harbinger. They went into hiding and began, rather neatly, to erase themselves, which is why nobody had ruins to find. You did not find a ruin, you found an editor. And then, because you are you, you talked one of them into fighting beside us, and they did. They disabled a Reaper capital ship to prove they could, and I have since had reports of the Collectors being cut loose from Harbinger's voice, and Ann Bryson went to work for Hackett on the Crucible, which pleases me more than it should.

I believe it. Do not misread me. I believe most of it. But here is the part where the archaeologist in me stands up and says what she is for.

It is one Leviathan, speaking inside your head. That is testimony, Shepard. It is not a stratum. There is a difference, and it is the whole of my profession: a stratum cannot lie to you about why it was laid down, and a witness can, particularly one with an interest in how it comes out. He told you that his kind had built the Intelligence out of care. Perhaps. He had a billion years to polish that, and a reason to. He was not able to tell you, and I think you noticed, what the Intelligence wanted, because he could only report what it had said it wanted. He could not check whether the cycles were what he thought they were. He could not check the Protheans. He was a prisoner of his own side of the story just as Vigil was of his, and I notice that you went and found the one source that agrees with what we most hoped for, and, forgive me, I have a file on that sort of thing.

And the dates. A billion years, and the Reapers fifty thousand at a time. Do you see why I cannot sleep? If I divide, I do not get a number a person could wear. I get something like twenty thousand cycles, and I do not believe in twenty thousand, and I do not believe in a clean answer either; my sources will tell me one thing about the age of the dead and another about the age of the dying and I have chosen, for the moment, not to reconcile them. I think the honest thing is to leave the gap there where everyone can see it. Which brings me to the one question I put to you that night, and you may have forgotten it. What did he ask you for, in return? Because nobody does anything for nothing, and a race that had spent a billion years hiding had a very good reason to stop.

## The Arithmetic of a Cycle

You have not answered that, and I am going to let it stand, because I have been promising myself for a week that I would be rude about numbers in front of someone who cannot leave, and you are sitting very still.

So. Arithmetic. I will do it the way I do it at night, which is with two of me, one at each end of a table, and I will tell you which end is speaking, because they do not agree.

My sources say the inusannon were out among the stars at least a hundred and twenty-seven thousand years ago. At least. Hold on to that, I will come back to it. It says the Protheans reached spaceflight around sixty-eight thousand years before our calendar and fell around forty-eight, after some centuries of losing. Sixty-eight to forty-eight is twenty thousand years, which is a fine, respectable Prothean empire, and a hundred and twenty-seven to forty-eight is seventy-nine thousand, which is a cycle and a half. Nobody has a cycle and a half. A cycle is fifty thousand, everyone has said so since Vigil, and the other half is where the table starts to shake.

The first of me says: it fits, you are being tiresome. Put the inusannon's end at roughly ninety-eight thousand, one clean harvest before the Protheans, and the Protheans find the ruins and learn mass effect physics, the account says tens of thousands of years later, and thirty is tens of thousands, and the empire rises on inherited tools exactly where it ought to. Lovely. And I would believe her, except that I notice what she did. She took a floor and made it a date. The inusannon fought the thoi'han over Eingana a hundred and twenty-seven thousand years ago, so that is when they were alive and arguing; the Reapers wiped them out "eventually," which is the word the account uses, and she has quietly decided that eventually means about thirty thousand years, because that is the number that makes the staircase even. The other of me says: then you have chosen the reading that flatters your arithmetic, and the honest one is that the end of the inusannon sits anywhere between a hundred and twenty-seven thousand and the Protheans' own first light, and a gap of fifty-nine thousand years in which the ruins sat there untouched while a whole harvest either happened and left nothing, or did not happen. And she is right, and I have no better answer than that I would like to have a better one.

Mm. It is worse than that, actually, and I do not know whether to be amused. The chronology in my files gives the Eingana war as a hundred and twenty-five thousand BCE, and the entries on Eingana and the inusannon say a hundred and twenty-seven thousand years ago. It is the same figure. Somebody said it aloud in 2185, I suspect, a clerk subtracted the present, and now there are two numbers in two places, and people quote the one that suits them. The same chronology says outright that huge dates should not be taken literally, that "nearly a billion" does not mean a billion less a few thousand, and I am grateful for the honesty and I wish it had not been tucked away where nobody reads. Every figure I am quarrelling with is a rounded sentence somebody once said into a recorder.

Let me do what I do and lay out where I think the gap could live, and I will not choose, so do not wait for it.

One. We missed a cycle. Between the inusannon and the Protheans a harvest came and went, and left no more than the harvests we have not found. I like this one for being modest. It also happens to be the one the method predicts, which should worry you as much as it worries me.

Two. The cycle is not fifty thousand. Here I stand on something I do know: my sources say the Reapers built the relays and the Citadel to speed up the time between harvests. To speed it up. Whatever they were, the intervals before the relays were longer, perhaps by a great deal, and fifty thousand is what the machinery settles into when it is working properly, not a law of nature. And then, in our own cycle, the Protheans on Ilos reached the Citadel and altered the signal the keepers wait for, and I do not know what that did to the clock. A delay is a delay. If we are living in a stretched interval, then the true period is shorter than the one we think we measured, and everything before it shifts.

Three. The dates are bad. I said that to you earlier of one source and I will say it of all of them, including the ones I like. The Fehl Prime device is dated to "as early as" sixty-eight thousand, as early as, and there is a very long way from the earliest a thing could be to when it was.

Four. It was never a schedule. Chorban, the salarian, the one on the Citadel nobody listened to, sent you an email, which I find very funny, since you went and scanned every last keeper because you were told to be thorough. His finding was that the keepers are set to react to a signal about every fifty thousand years, and the last time coincided with the Protheans' end. That is the only instrument in the entire record that measures the interval directly, and the thing it measures is the keepers, which are the Reapers' own instrument. So what I have is the Reapers' behaviour reported by the Reapers' furniture. Sovereign spent, Legion told you, more than a thousand years going about waking allies for something it called restarting the cycle, and you do not restart a clock. You restart a decision.

I would like you to see the trap in all four, because it is the same trap. If a harvest destroys the evidence of the harvests before it, then the absence of evidence confirms the theory and the presence of evidence confirms it too, and an archaeologist who cannot be wrong has stopped being one. I can say "there was a cycle here, and it was cleaned" about anywhere. What I actually have is a list of things the cleaning missed, which is a very particular list: a planet pulled to pieces by two fleets' drive cores, a rift valley on Klendagon cut by a round fired at a Reaper thirty-seven million years ago, craters on Etamis, a corpse in a crater that someone left alone for nearly a billion years. And the Thorian. I was on Feros, you will remember, I stood next to you, and I have never quite said it to you plainly: that was the only living thing in the galaxy that sat through the Protheans' end and kept growing for fifty thousand years, and Shiala mourned it, and I mourned, privately, a witness, and you do have a habit of ending my best sources. I keep a list.

Everything on that list survived because the harvest did not care, or could not see. Those are not a sample of the cycles. They are the cycles' blind spot, and I cannot count the gaps in a wall by the bricks that were left.

Now the Leviathan, and I will be brief, because I owe the last conversation more rigour than a second helping of it. If what you were told is true then the Reapers began a billion years ago and made more of themselves through millions of years, and the relay network came afterward, to hurry things. Then the first cycles were not fifty thousand of anything. They were an unmeasured age, and the number of them depends on whether a harvest produces one new Reaper or one for each species taken, and my sources disagree about that, one saying a Reaper is a nation of a single people and another saying each harvest ends with a birth in Harbinger's image. Count the Reapers and you have counted species. Count the harvests and you have counted something else, and no one has been able to tell me whether those numbers are even in the same order of magnitude. Etamis has an entry that does the sum for us: if the Etamisians were harvested then it was four to eight hundred cycles ago. That sum has the sort of confidence that makes me want to leave the room. It divides one guess by another and writes the answer in a trivia box.

The one thing that crosses the gaps is the Crucible, and that is why I find I cannot be as sceptical as I would like. A blueprint handed from one cycle to the next, as I said earlier; it lay for fifty thousand years under Mars. I took the Kahje keys to it, and you and I kept it out of Eva Coré's hands, at a price Kaidan paid. Hackett paid for it, the shipyards built it, Vendetta told us the missing piece, the Catalyst, was the Citadel itself, and the Illusive Man ran there and told the Reapers where to look. That is all true and all was the world ending, and I want to stand in the part nobody has asked about: a chain of custody. A blueprint that survived that long means there was a continuous line of hands. And a continuous line of hands does not fit a harvest that wipes everything, unless the Reapers' ledger of what they destroyed is as wrong as ours. I find that a comfort, and I find it a very small and unscientific one.

So. If I had a lab and a decade — I know. I know. I have neither, and you need not say so; you would be right about the decade.

I would date the oldest relay first. Not the Citadel, not Ilos. The oldest known relay in the galaxy was the Alpha, in Bahak, and Kenson's team did not call it that for nothing: her paper said most relays are far older than fifty thousand years, tested by the dust held in their gravity wells, and the asari have run something like it and abandoned it, and a woman at Serrice asked, rather beautifully, where that species is now. I wanted that dust. I know exactly why the Alpha is gone, and I know what you carried, and I would not undo it. I would only like to have had the sample. After that, the debris on Eingana, because two civilisations broke their drive cores over one world in a single war, and that is a debris layer with a before and an after, which is the nearest thing to a clean datum this subject has ever offered me. After that, the corpse on Jartar, if I could ever get the Hegemony to say what it did with it. I would like to measure three things that do not know about one another, and see whether any two of them agree.

You have the look of someone about to ask which of the four I would bet on. I will not. But tell me, Shepard, since you are the only one who has stood in the room with all of them: what would you have dated first?

## What the Record Is For

You are still not going to tell me what you would have dated first, and I have decided to count that as a vote for the Alpha relay, since nobody has ever voted against it. Good. Put the dust away. I have one more thing, and it is the one I have been avoiding all evening by being rigorous at you for an hour, which is a technique I learned from the best scholars on Serrice and would not recommend.

It is the book.

You know about the book. Everyone on the ship knows about the book, because I have been sketching titles for it since before you died the first time, and Glyph has a folder, and Joker has twice asked it to read the list aloud in the cockpit in a voice I am told is not at all like mine. So. Let me be honest about the titles, because they are the most honest thing I have written.

The first one was "The Protheans." Just that. It is what a young woman with a doctorate and a great deal of confidence writes on a blank page, a title that is also a claim: here is the subject, it is closed, you may now be told about it. I rejected it because I had, by then, stood in a ruin on Therum that turned out to be mostly other people's looting, and the title had no room in it for that. Then "The Prothean Empire," which is better, and which I rejected the day Javik walked off a pod and informed me that the word I was using for it was too polite. Then "A Living Legacy," which I think I wrote on a very good day, and which I now find so embarrassing that I will say only this: I was fond of the word living, and I had not yet met anything that was. There is one on the list that simply reads, I Despise Thinking Up Titles For My Own Academic Research, and I left it in, because it is the only one that has never been wrong. And then, last, the one he made me write down. "Journeys with the Prothean." Mm. I want you to know I dislike that title. I dislike it in the particular way you dislike a thing that is correct. He has agreed to consider it, which from Javik is a binding contract, and I have agreed to consider it back, which from me is a sworn deposition.

What I did not expect, when I started the list, was that the title would keep changing because the subject kept changing. I had a species in mind, wise and patient, keepers of wisdom, and what I had on the table was a people who were frightened and good at it, who built an empire out of other people's fear and then spent their last centuries losing a war in a very organised fashion. I put that in my private notes, the ones the Broker's terminal keeps for me, and the entry I reread most is not the angry one. It is a short one, where I admit I had romanticised a whole species out of a few fragments and a great deal of longing, and that the fault was mine and not theirs. I would like the next reader to find that entry first. Before the dates, before the arithmetic, before anyone's quarrel with the inusannon.

Now. Here is what I have actually concluded, and I have been walking toward it all lecture.

Everything the Protheans left that is still standing is a thing that was meant to be found. Not preserved, found. That is a different kind of object. A beacon is a message in a form that a particular mind could receive, and they coded it so only a Prothean mind would understand it, because they did not want the Reapers reading it, which was sensible, and which also meant that when the message reached you it arrived as a handful of images and fifteen hours lying on a floor. I have told you that I once thought that was a mistake. It was a lock with the key in a drawer on Feros, and the drawer was Shiala, and a Thorian had to be dealt with before she opened it. Vigil sat in the dark under Ilos for fifty thousand years, patient to the point of insult, waiting for one visitor, and he is the clearest example I have: a mind built to be addressed. The cache under Mars was an observation post, a small room, a lot of data, no obvious grandeur, and it did more to the human species in a few decades than the whole of the rest of the Protheans' rubble did to anybody. Because it was found by people who could use it.

And then I think of everything on the other side of the ledger. The asari kept a beacon in a statue and told no one for the whole of our recorded history, including the Council that had made hiding it illegal, and it did us precisely as much good as a sealed letter does. It took Vendetta, and the temple, and a great deal going wrong, to make the thing speak. Vendetta sat in that statue among the pilgrims and said nothing to any of them until you walked in with the right artefacts. A record is not a pile of facts, it is an open door that has to be walked through by someone who has been taught how the door works. So if I leave anything, it will not be a very large archive. I will leave a short one, addressed to a particular stranger, with the key attached where they will trip on it.

I did that, as it happens. You know about the capsule. I sat down in the evenings, between operations, and tried to write it for somebody I could not picture, and I found I could not write for posterity at all. Posterity is a very bad audience. It does not lean forward. I wrote it for whoever would be sitting where I was sitting when I found the Mars cache, tired and a little frightened and holding an incomplete thing, and who would need to be told what we had actually been like. Not our dates. Us.

Which is where I owe you the part I have been dreading, because I want the next reader to know about this cycle, and about you, and I am going to say it badly, sideways, and with a joke at my own expense, since that is the only form in which I can say it at all.

I have the file on the Citadel. Of course I do. After the coup, after the party, when somebody had been wearing your face around the Wards. I have Glyph's account of the Archives, delivered in the tone of a small drone that would like it understood it was not the one who got locked in a vault, and I have Traynor's, which is shorter and a good deal more pointed, because it was Traynor the false orders were sent to, in your voice, to ready the ship for launch. The Spectre codes were a copy of a copy. Brooks, who had assembled the dossiers once, for the other mission, and had stopped believing in the man she worked for, had woken a thing that had been kept in a tank as spare parts, and let it learn to behave like a person from a set of implants. It thought itself the real one, freed of everything that makes you slow: the friends, the guilt, the people who wait. It shut you in iridium and went for the docks, and Glyph, who I will concede may be in the right about itself, let you out, and it was settled at the docks, where you tried to hold on to him and he let go. That is as far as I have it. I was not in that room. I heard it afterwards, in pieces, from people who each said it differently, which is how I know it happened.

Here is the joke. I have spent fifty years on a record of a vanished people, and the entire time I believed that the thing I wanted was a more complete record. More fragments, more cipher, a bigger dig. And the clearest lesson I have ever been handed about records came from a man grown in a tank who had every one of your files, every code, every habit, the walk, the voice, the whole dossier. Perfect copy. Better preserved than anything I have ever pulled out of a wall. And he was nothing like you, because the part of you that mattered was not in the file. It was in what happens to you when somebody is in trouble. I am an archaeologist of some distinction, and I needed a clone to tell me that the artefact is not the point. I would like it recorded that I find this humiliating, and that I have put it in a footnote, and that the footnote has a footnote.

Which is what Javik meant, I think, though he would be appalled to hear me say he meant anything gentle. He said something of the sort once, in the tone he uses for the weather. I have lost the wording, which is unlike me; the sense was that a record means nothing unless someone is moved by it, and that his people had kept very good records. He was the proof, a perfectly good archive of a civilisation, standing in my cargo hold, complaining about the food. It was meant to be a cruelty. I decided to take it as an instruction, which is the only way anybody on this ship has ever gotten anything out of him.

So this is my answer, and then I will stop, because the lights are doing the thing that means we have overrun.

He is right that a record that moves no one is a very good way of being forgotten. But I do not think the moving is separate from the keeping. It is the keeping. Every Prothean thing that lasted was built to move someone, to be found by the one who could carry it, and the ones that moved nobody, the sealed ones, the withheld ones, are the ones that failed. So if the book is ever finished, it will not be a history of the Protheans. It will be a letter, in the way the beacon was a letter, to someone who is tired and halfway through something. It will be about what a cycle is like from inside, and the thing it will say is the one I could not get into any of the titles. That nobody here was certain. That we argued, and ate badly, and kept a drone named Glyph, and drank something Garrus called calibrations. That we were frightened and did it anyway, and that the person at the middle of it, whom I am addressing and who is looking very uncomfortable at being addressed, never once behaved as though he was in a record.

I think that might be the title, actually. I have never said that aloud. Do not tell Javik, he will want his name on the first line.

There. I have run over by twenty minutes. I had a better ending, and I will have it five minutes from now.
