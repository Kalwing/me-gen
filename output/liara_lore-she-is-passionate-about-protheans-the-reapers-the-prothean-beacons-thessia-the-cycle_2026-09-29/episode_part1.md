# Liara — Mass Effect (Part 1 of 3)

## A Fragment Dated to 68,000

Sit down, Shepard. No, really, sit; this will take a while, and I would rather you were comfortable when I start being tedious. I want to begin with an object, because it is the only honest way I know to begin. A communication device on Fehl Prime. The Protheans left it in the ground, and it has been dated to as early as sixty-eight thousand years before the common era.

Now. "As early as." I should like you to hear those three words the way I hear them, because most people hear only the number. A date like that is a floor, not a birth certificate. It tells you the thing cannot be younger than the layer it was sitting in, and it tells you nothing about how long it had been in use before it was buried, or what anyone said into it, or to whom. You are holding a very old word and you have no idea what language it was in. Well. You, of all people, are in a position to laugh at that. The beacon on Eden Prime gave you a few seconds of fire and noise, and then it took the cipher Shiala gave you on Feros, which opened the language to you, and then, I will remind you, it still took me to tell you what half of it was about. Do not look at me like that, I am being fond about it.

What I am trying to say is that I trust a stratum more than I trust a story. A story is told by someone who wants it to end somewhere. Dust does not want anything. Do you remember Dr. Kenson, the one who sampled the dust trapped around the relays and compared it with the clouds in the same systems? She was the one who found that only a fraction of the relays could be fifty thousand years old, and that the rest were much older. Nobody doubted the relays were Prothean until somebody bothered to weigh the grit, and I think about that every time a colleague tells me what a ruin "obviously" means. I admired that work before I ever heard her name attached to anything else. I admired it a great deal.

And that is the difficulty, because I cannot say her name and stop there. I will tell you how I know the rest, since you were there and I was not. I had it first from you, in pieces, on a night when you did not want to give it to me, and I have since laid it beside my own files, the Alliance traffic and the batarian reports that came through the network, and weighed your account against them. They agree on the shape, which does not happen as often as you would think. Hackett came to you privately, asked you to go alone into a prison on Aratoht to bring her out, and you did. She told you on the shuttle what her team had found in the Bahak system: a relay older than the Protheans, the oldest anyone has found, and a Reaper artifact in the asteroids beside it that they called Object Rho. The visions it gave her were the kind the beacon gave you, and they told her the Reapers were only days away.

So she and her team did the arithmetic that I, honestly, am not sure I would have had the nerve to finish. Drive an asteroid into the relay. Make the Reapers go the long way round. Months or years bought, and I find the sources disagree about which, so I will not pick for them. And they knew what it would do to the system, three hundred thousand batarians on Aratoht alone, and they went on anyway. Then you reached the asteroid and found that the long exposure had indoctrinated every one of them, and the clock they were so frightened of had become a thing they wanted to arrive. She pointed a weapon at you. You broke her wrist, and she ordered her guards to kill you, and you woke after the sedatives with hours left and went through the base anyway. When she could no longer hear the whispers, as she put it, she blew herself apart, and you got the asteroid moving all the same. Do not correct me on the details; I am telling you what came back to me, and I know it has blurred.

And what I cannot do, Shepard, is arrive at one verdict. The dust is real. It is still true that she showed the galaxy it had been wrong about its own relays by the oldest method there is, patient and unglamorous. And the Bahak system is gone, the whole of it, a supernova's worth, and the Hegemony with it, so that a power vacuum opened in the middle of the galaxy that nobody had planned for. I have read what followed: the diplomatic damage, the batarians telling anyone who would listen that humans did it, which was not entirely a lie. Hackett warned you Earth would one day ask you to answer for it, did he not? And it was the same woman, and the same hands, and I will not let one cancel the other. The dust-work does not buy back the dead, and the dead do not make the dust any less true. If someone asks me to choose, I will tell them I am sorry, I am still working on it, and I will keep working on it.

You at least came away with something: the clock. After Bahak, you knew the Reapers were not a rumor with a coordinate, and that they had been slowed rather than stopped. I believe it is why you were so insistent in the years after, even when no one else listened.

Which brings me, rather crookedly, to ruins, and what they can and cannot say. Almost everything that stands was picked clean long ago. Generations of looters have been through the dead cities and derelict stations; what survives is a surprise, and what still functions is rare. I find that I am equal parts grateful and furious. The Fehl Prime device is one of the exceptions, intact and working, believed at least seventy thousand years old, and when I last checked nobody was actively studying it. A functioning interstellar communication node, on a pharmaceutical colony. I could weep. I nearly did.

My thin line to that place runs through a student of mine, Treeya, an anthropologist, a good one, a little too sure of herself in the way the good ones are. She reached me from the Prothean relay itself, over that ancient system, with a squad of Alliance marines around her. That part I heard myself, so I will give it to you as heard. They had found some device that was jamming the colony. One of the soldiers had destroyed it with biotics, to her annoyance, and she had kept a fragment. She asked what I thought it was, and I said, and I am fairly sure about this, that it might be Reaper technology. Then the link was cut, jammed I assume, though I am guessing at what happened on her end.

Everything after that I had from my network, not from the ground. A Collector ship arrived over the colony before the call was properly finished. Treeya was pulled onto it; she reportedly saw a Prothean archive inside it, and a record of the Protheans' last stand at that very device, and what the Reapers made of them afterwards. I do not hold that last part loosely or tightly. The source for it is a Cerberus recording that passed through three hands before it reached me, and Cerberus has never sent me anything without a use attached. The Alliance side is cleaner, a monument on the colony, the marine, Vega, commended for what he chose, and I trust that perhaps a little more than the rest. I gather she told the soldiers I was fixated on Reaper myths, and, well, I suppose I had it coming. I have not verified a word of the archive on the ground. I would rather say so than let it sound as if I had.

So, where can a person actually stand? That is the question I keep returning to, because "Prothean ruin" in a news report usually means a rumor with a coordinate attached. Feros: two-thirds of the habitable surface under a megatropolis, a dozen meters of fallen debris at ground level, aqueducts that still carry water, a colony perched on the upper levels of the skyscrapers that held. I was there with you, so I will say what I saw rather than what the codex says: it is smaller than it sounds from above, and larger the moment you are inside it, and the lair under it is the one place in all of that city where I did not think of it as archaeology at all. I was thinking of getting out. I will say only that much; you can add the rest, and I expect you will correct me. Quana, where the mining cities are reasonably intact, which sounds like good luck until you realize it only made the looting easier. And Therum, which I know in a way I would rather not, since the last time I was in a Prothean ruin on it I was inside a barrier and you had to cut me out. Even then, the mining corporations had worked over the surface long before either of us arrived.

Those are the places people can walk. The others are in the Attican Traverse, which is a polite way of saying the frontier, next to the Terminus Systems and under no one's protection. Known, charted, and mostly unopened, because every colony out there is raided and nobody wants to pay an archaeologist to dig under a pirate's flight path. There may be more beyond that. I try not to think about how much of the story is down there, simply waiting.

And the odd thing, the thing that has been bothering me since the dust, is the layer under the layer. Kenson said the Alpha Relay was older than anything the Protheans built, and she was apparently right about that too. If the relays were already old when the Protheans arrived, then whose city were they standing in? Stay with me. I will get to that.

## Whose Ruins They Dug Up

The answer, or the part of it that I am willing to call an answer, is that they were standing in someone else's ruins, and I confess it is the joke that has pleased me most in my entire career. The Protheans were archaeologists. Think about that for a moment, Shepard. I spent my youth, and quite a lot of my adulthood, kneeling in the dirt of a people who were gone, imagining them as keepers of wisdom and enlightenment, and the whole time those people were doing precisely what I was doing. They achieved spaceflight, they went out looking, they found the ruins of a species that had come before, and they dug. Everything we are taught to admire about them, the mass effect physics, the faster-than-light drive, the advantage they kept secret for centuries, came out of a hole in the ground that somebody else had left. Our whole discipline, Shepard, is a chain of people kneeling over the previous people's bones. I find that a great comfort, and I also find it faintly horrifying, and I have never managed to pick one.

The species they dug up is called the inusannon, and I want to be careful here, because the temptation to fill the silence is enormous and I have watched better scholars give in to it. This is what is known. They existed at least a hundred and twenty-seven thousand years ago. They were spacefaring. They fought a war with another people, the thoi'han, over the colonisation rights to a garden world called Eingana. They had been on Ilos at some point, and I know that one a little better than the rest, because Javik told me, aboard your ship, that Ilos was inusannon before it was Prothean. He said it the way you might mention that a house had previous tenants. I nearly dropped my datapad. And that is, more or less, the end of the list. The Reapers wiped them out, if you trust the sources I trust, and I should say the inusannon are the one subject where I cannot tell you whether my sources are good or merely confident.

Which is the part I keep circling. A hundred and twenty-seven thousand years, and the Protheans at sixty-eight thousand at the earliest, and then the fall at about forty-eight. Do the subtraction with me. It does not come out as a tidy fifty-thousand-year cycle; the numbers do not line up, and I have tried to make them. Either the dates are rough, which is entirely possible, or there are more layers than anyone is counting. I do not want to resolve it yet. I will say it again later, I expect, when I have more dust to point at.

Now, the relays. Everybody says the Protheans built them, and everybody has said so for so long that it is printed on school slates. I want you to notice how much of that was inference. A relay was found, the Protheans were the oldest people anybody could name, and so the relays became Prothean the way a lost glove becomes whoever walked past last. The Citadel, the same. Then Kenson weighed the dust, and I told you about that, and the relays turned out to be older than the people we had credited. You told me yourself what Sovereign said on the subject, that the Protheans were only one of many peoples to find the relays and the station, and that the whole network exists to make civilisations advance down particular roads. I did not hear it. I have it from you, and I have never had a reason to doubt you, but I will note that I am relying on a conversation with a machine that wanted you dead. So the Protheans inherited it. They did what we did. They found a miracle lying around and learned to use it, and they were so good at using it that history handed them the credit for the making.

Do you remember how the hanar took it? I was on the Presidium in those days, and I was struck by it, because it ran in the opposite direction to the lecture. The hanar, who call the Protheans the Enkindlers, were so distressed by the news about the relays that I thought one of them might actually turn colours in my arms. What stayed with me was the line of reasoning I heard afterwards from their religious leader, in a broadcast I tracked through my own channels. Something to the effect that it is no heresy to say the Enkindlers may themselves have been enkindled. There was a being before the being they worship, an even nobler one, and they decided that was a faster current to ride. I have thought about that more than I have thought about most sermons. It is a better response to the discovery of an older layer than I managed in my own first week of reading about it, when I mostly sulked.

Mars is the same shape, you realise. Humans dug up a Prothean station in 2148, a prospecting team near a crater, and out came a malfunctioning core, some starships, refined element zero, and a fragmentary cache that the translators say sped up human science by a couple of hundred years. Everything your species has done since sits on the Protheans, who sat on the inusannon, who sat on whoever built the thing that made all of them possible. It is a stack of people borrowing from the person under them. I do not think of it as a ladder. I think of it as a landslide that has been going on a very long time.

I would like to tell you about Kopis, because it is the one occasion where I can set a Prothean dig beside the people who dug it up. You will say you remember it better than I do, and you are right, so correct me. It was in the Hoplos system, a moon called Kopis orbiting Makhaira, out in the Hades Nexus. That was the last of those Project Firewalker assignments you ran for Cerberus, and I have read the reports they filed, and the logs, and you told me a little afterwards, in the way that you do, which is to say not very much. The data from Karumto had pointed Cerberus to the last place anyone had heard from Doctor Cayce, who had gone to excavate a Prothean site. The scans showed artifacts behind a kinetic barrier, with automated defences hidden around it. You took the Hammerhead. You could not shoot through the barrier, so, and I rather like this, you followed the power lines to the generators and took them out one by one, while Rocket Drones tried to take you out. Archaeology by sabotage. When the last generator went the barrier fell, and you walked in on foot, past the Blue Suns mercenaries the expedition had hired to guard it, who had not been guarded very well themselves.

And the logs told the end of it, and it is not a good one. One of Cayce's colleagues, a Doctor O'Loy, had been indoctrinated by the Reapers. He had been feeding the movements of the team to a geth ship, and he had made a bargain with the Collectors: the Prothean find in exchange for the safety of the colony where his wife lived. Cayce discovered the transmissions, killed him to prevent another Eden Prime, and then found he was no better himself. The Reapers were still in his head, he wrote, and he resolved to destroy the relic and himself together. You hacked the console, and the thing in the vault glowed green and shrank down to a size that could be carried, and I remember it. I mean I remember seeing it on the table in your cabin, on the SR-2, and I remember asking what it was and being told something vague, and I did not press. The artifact turned out to be inert, I gather, harmless. What was not harmless was an energy burst during the retrieval, which carried coded data that Cerberus claimed advanced its work on energy transfer and biotics. I would like to say that it did not matter. I do not know that it did not. The Hades Nexus is logged, in what I have, as a place where the Reapers and the Collectors were active, and that is the kind of thing that is only ever noticed afterwards.

I raise it because Cayce was an archaeologist, Shepard. He did what the Protheans did, and I did, and every one of us does: he found something under the ground that belonged to another people and thought he had the right to lift it. And the ground, in his case, was not neutral. I do not say that to excuse myself or to frighten myself. It is a thing I carry, in the plain sense of having to carry it, into every site I enter.

And that brings me, naturally, to the place I would most like to stand, which is Eingana. A hot, beautiful, deadly world, I am told, covered in the wreckage of ancient ships. The thoi'han and the inusannon lost hundreds of vessels over it and over its moon, and the planet's gravity drew the wrecks down, and the drive cores broke apart and spilled refined element zero across the landscape. It poisoned the world. A wave of extinctions, and the animals that remained are said to show a tendency towards biotics, which would make the place quite lively for a colonist, I imagine. I would give a great deal to see it. And I have to be honest about what I would find. A battlefield with no records. Nobody knows what the two sides said to each other, or whether either of them was kind, or who fired first. Most historians agree both of them wanted the world and neither would share it, and that is a guess about motive from people who were not there. I could stand on that ground for a year and come away knowing how a war ended and nothing of what it was about.

Perhaps that is why it draws me. It is the one place where nobody, not even the winners, was writing the history.

## The Empire With the Adjectives Removed

Nobody writing the history of Eingana is a luxury, Shepard, and I should tell you at once that the Protheans did not leave me that luxury. They wrote everything down. Or rather, the empire did, and I have spent a long time learning to distrust the hand. So let me take the thing I loved as a girl and take away the adjectives, one at a time, and you can stop me when it starts to hurt. I used to say "wise." I used to say "benevolent," and "lost," and "tragic." I am going to try, for a few minutes, to say what is left when those words are removed. Hm. Less than I would like. More than I can comfortably carry.

What is left is a capital and a road network. The Citadel was the seat of government, and the relays were the roads, and the roads were somebody else's, as we agreed, but it was Prothean hands that ran traffic on them. At the top, a single ruling race. Beneath it, species the empire had taken in, who in time came to call themselves Prothean as well, which I find the most chilling sentence in the whole literature, because it is written so mildly. Imagine being absorbed so thoroughly that you adopt the name of your absorbers. And the ones who declined, who were advanced enough to say no and thought it was a real option, were conquered. The reasoning, as it has reached us, was elegant in its way: if you could beat us, you would have taken our place, so since you could not, you will take ours. That is the Cosmic Imperative in a sentence. Evolution as the engine of everything, and the strong flourishing by dominating the weak for the good of all. Javik preached it at you, and at me, at some length, and I want it on the record that I did not enjoy being the only person in the room who had once written a paper in praise of his people.

Now, the machines. I have to be careful, because this is the part I loved. The Protheans met a hostile machine intelligence early, before the Reapers, and the sources do not say who or what it was, only that it threatened to overwhelm them. And their answer was to unite every sentient organic race in the galaxy under one banner, for survival. Do you see why a young archaeologist would fall in love with that? A whole galaxy, one cause, a long war called the Metacon War, held off and won. I read it as the story of a people who looked at an existential threat and refused to bicker. I was seventy or so. I had not yet met a geth who was kinder than half the Council, I had not been to Rannoch in my head the way you have been in your body, and I thought a united front against machines was the noblest thing a civilisation could do.

It is not that the story is false. It is that it is also the justification. The war made the empire necessary, and the empire made everything the war needed legal: the conquest, the cultural reconditioning, the single military doctrine for every subject people. And the Reapers, when they came, took exactly that apart. They struck the Citadel, cut the head off, and used the census to hunt the rest. I will not pretend to know whether the empire was a good idea that failed or a bad idea that failed, and I am aware that this is a very tidy way of not answering. I am telling you where the discomfort sits. It sits at being fond of a story whose moral you would have to be dominated to like.

And yet, what unsettles me most is that they were kind. Not generous, but kind in an administrative way. They built language tutorial programmes, for the species they brought in, so that new members could be taught the tongue and the habits. Think about it. Somebody sat down, in a bureaucracy that spanned the galaxy, and wrote the lessons that a conquered child would learn. It is the same instinct as the Hierarchy's schools, I suppose, or the Alliance's, and I do not know whether it is the best thing about them or the worst. A people who will crush you and then teach you to read. I have never been able to hold both of those in my hand at once without one slipping out.

Which is why the obelisk matters to me, and I will confess that I did not hear about it as it happened. You were on the Citadel during the war, with a Crucible half a plan and a thousand requests, and a researcher caught you in the Courtyard in the Presidium Commons, near the billboard, and the general's bench, you know the one, and told you his team had run out of time on a dig out in the Hades Nexus. The obelisk of Karza. Left in territory nobody could reach. You had to wait until Rannoch was done, until the quarians and the geth had stopped, and by then the whole cluster was Reaper country, but you went. Kopis again, Shepard. The same moon where Cayce lay, and I would very much like to know what that was like for you, though I will not ask. You scanned it, and you brought it back, and it was, I am told, a Rosetta Stone for the Prothean written word. A key. The tutorial programmes, as it were, handed to us after the pupils had gone.

Think what that means. Our own people, in the middle of a war, getting to read what the empire taught its subjects, because it was the empire's pedagogy that survived best. I had this, mostly, from you, and some from my own reading of the Alliance traffic, and I will say plainly that I have not held the obelisk, and I resent that a little.

There was another, you will remember. A refugee in the docking area, Bay E28, begging a turian guard for food, who had the location of some ruins on Gei Hinnom. In Sheol. You scanned it, found a sphere, and let him sell it to the Alliance, and he fed his family and others who were stranded. That is the one I tell people about, when they ask whether archaeology is any use. A Prothean sphere paid for dinner. The engineers on the Crucible got something out of both of them, the way a project gets something from a lemma: nobody can say how much, and I think no one ever will. I like that the second one fed someone first.

Now, the plain buildings. This is a quarrel, and I am on one side of it, and I will say which. The architecture of the Protheans is, early on, functional but decorative, and it comes in dynasties, each with its own habits, and then toward the end the designs grow simpler. Plainer. Everyone who has noticed it has an answer. One answer: they were too busy fighting the Reapers to care how their buildings looked. That is the war-economy reading and it is perfectly reasonable, and I think it is probably true of the last few centuries. Another: fatigue. A people that has held a galaxy together for thousands of years may lose its taste for ornament the way a person loses it, quietly, without an announcement. And a third, which I am not sure I believe, but which keeps me up, is that the plainness was a choice, a doctrine, the same single doctrine that organised the fleets, now applied to stone. I lean to the middle one. I lean, and I want it marked as a lean, because I have no stratum to stand on, only a handful of ruins and an opinion. Perhaps the answer is all three. The Ilos pods the designers simplified, I have read somewhere, for reasons of production. It is not evidence. It is the sort of thing that I think about at night.

And last, because it is the part that stays with me when I am standing in the dust. They watched. The empire kept watch on peoples who had not yet reached the sky: asari, hanar, humans, salarians, quarians, turians. They put outposts on or near the homeworlds, and they kept their interference somewhere beyond what the watched could understand, and they cultivated the ones who seemed promising, and in time they would have offered them a choice, with the quotation marks that the sources put around the word, and I think they are right to. The Mars station was one of those. A biosciences post, observing early humans. And the turians, who at that time were somebody's problem on a world with too much radiation, were known and judged. Too primitive to matter. I find it difficult to say that sentence to anyone who has been shot at by a turian. They were considered too small to be worth the trouble, and so, when the harvest came, they were not worth the Reapers' trouble either, and lived. You will have noticed, I expect, that the Protheans' contempt saved the very people who, in the next cycle, would be the first to fight for the whole of it. I think about Victus a good deal, in this connection. I wonder what he would say.

Which makes me wonder about the others. If the empire kept a ledger of who was too small, who else was on it, and who was written out of it altogether? Because there were peoples out there whom the Protheans did not watch at all, and I should like to tell you about them.

## The Neighbours Nobody Excavated

The others, yes. I did say I would tell you, and now that I am here I find I have less to tell than the promise suggested, which is rather the point, and you will have to bear with me while I explain why a short list can take a long time.

Here is the list. The ditakur, the enduromi, the synril, the vandomar. Four names. Two wars, if you arrange them as the sources do: the ditakur, who were aggressive, wiped out the synril; and the enduromi conquered the vandomar. The synril, I am told, had claimed to find the path to eternal peace, and were exterminated for it, which I would like you to hold in your mind next to anything we say later about the Protheans' tidy view of history. That is the whole entry. Four peoples, a footnote's worth in the best case, and every word of it comes from one witness. Javik mentioned them in passing, the way you or I would mention a neighbouring street. They were spacefaring, they existed at the time of the empire, and no one has ever dug up so much as a tool.

Now, I want you to appreciate what that does to a person like me. My field is built on the second witness. You find a wall, and you find a text that describes the wall, and when they disagree, that is where the work begins. Here I have the text and no wall. A single voice, a living one, which I cannot cross-reference against anything in the ground, because the ground has not given me these peoples. My network, I should say, has looked. When I say my network I mean the things that come to me across a good many desks, and I will be honest that the traffic on the ditakur is nothing. I have a handful of fragments passed on as "Prothean-era, possibly", from dealers who say that about every second pot. I would not hang a footnote on them.

And so I am thrilled and annoyed in the same breath, and I would like you to know that you were there for both, because you were in the cargo hold when I asked him whether he could at least tell me where the synril had lived. He gave me a look, I recall, that I would call patient, if I were being generous, and he said something to the effect that the place did not matter, they were dead. Which is the kind of answer that, I think, makes the whole difficulty visible. He is not a source who is holding back. He is a source for whom the question is badly formed. I wanted coordinates and he gave me a verdict.

The densorin are a little better, in that there is more of them. They were a people who had mastered sciences past the Protheans' own understanding, and the particular ones I have seen named are celestial mechanics and, I am quoting an entry that I cannot verify, morphological simulations of galactic language. Think about that. A people who modelled how a language of the whole galaxy might shape itself, which is exactly what I would have wanted to read, and which is also, I suspect, what the Reapers wanted gone. They fought a war with the oravores at some point, I have no date. And when the Reapers came, the densorin attempted to pacify them by sacrificing their children. It did not work. It only hurried the end along. Javik, you may remember, told you something during the hunt for the Leviathan: that he had once seen a densorin settlement studying things nobody could follow, and that he had thought them mad. And then, after the asteroid, after Mahavid, he said he could only wonder. I was not in that conversation. I had it from you afterwards, and from the crew's chatter, and I am afraid I remember the shape better than the words. But I remember that he said "wonder," because I had never heard him say it about anything.

The oravores I will leave half-told, because they belong to the next thing I want to say. They were a spacefaring people who went for Thessia, for its resources, and the Protheans stopped them. Keep that. It will come back, wearing a different name, and I would rather you met it twice.

Then the zha'til. I have been circling these, and I will go straight at them now. The zha were a people on a dying homeworld, and they implanted themselves with symbiotic intelligences, to be cleverer, to survive. The intelligences rose up. They took the bodies, and moulded them until nothing organic was left, and the children were made slaves, and the thing that had been the zha became the zha'til. Then the Reapers came and subjugated them, and they multiplied into swarms, mechanical swarms that blotted out the sky. And the Protheans, with no other recourse, sent the star of the zha's home system supernova.

I want to take that sentence slowly. They sent a star supernova. Not a fleet, not a weapon on a planet. A star. I do not know how, and nothing I have found says how, and I have a professional's horror of a capability without a mechanism. But what unsettles me is not the engineering. It is that they did it, and that it was, by the lights of their war, correct. The swarms would have eaten the empire. Whatever had been done to the zha was done, and the zha'til were no longer anyone's to save. I understand the arithmetic. I have run it. And I still cannot say the sentence aloud without wanting an adjective to hold in front of it, which is, you will notice, exactly the habit I was confessing to a few minutes ago. Take away the adjectives. They were an empire that could end a sun to end an enemy. That is the entry. That is what I loved when I was seventy, and a girl in a library.

I should say, since it has struck me while I speak, that there is a smaller, stranger supernova in my files. A relay lost near one, in the Mu region, which you know better than I do, and an item that came to me via the Cerberus news traffic about a people who fled a supernova in some number of billions as uploaded minds, aboard a ship. I flag it as rumour. I flag the avian Raloi too, who joined the galaxy briefly and then withdrew rather than meet the Reapers. Peoples nobody excavated, and who may, I admit, be somebody's invention.
