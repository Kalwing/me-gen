Then he told me his. I will do it the way I do everything, as a summary, because the summary is the part I earned. His mother was the sun, he said, with no joke on it, which if you knew him for a week you will understand was an event. She danced in the kitchen. Then her partner died, by his own hand, and she started to drink, and by the time he was ten, eleven, he was the one managing her bad nights. At fourteen he knew that one of them was different, and he called for help, and she lived. What she told him afterward was that he was not her child anymore. He went to his father's. She died some years after, while he was finishing his degree, and he said the grief was larger than the peace he had made with her, which surprised him, because he had been rather proud of the peace. I knew for years, from files, that my father tended bar on Illium, and what I did with that was what I am best at, I put it in a drawer. I did give him something back, I think. I told him he could use me the way I had used that counter. Somewhere to put a thing down, nobody pouring, nothing read back to him. He took it about as far as the tea.

But let me do what I am actually known for, and give you his argument about the Protheans. I wrote it down afterward so I have it fairly well, and I should say he cited Kropotkin throughout, and gave a great deal of credit to the beavers, and to Murray Bookchin, who said very little of what Thomas had him saying. Several quotations belonged to nobody at all. And yet the philosophy was theirs, underneath. He had read them for years, he just kept knowledge differently than I do. I store facts. He had more of a... nebula. Everything he had ever read, drifting, pulling together where it wanted to. The argument it made, though, was sound, and I have not been able to dislodge it in three years. Two years? He said it in the first ship, so, yes, the better part of three. The Reapers did not defeat the Protheans, he said, they unplugged them. An empire with one center has nowhere to scatter. Take the center and the rest is a very large machine with the power off. I put this to Javik once and he said his people had been efficient, or something to that effect, and left it there, which is not a refutation, and I did point that out.

And then because he could not leave a good idea alone he asked whether I loved the Protheans for the same reason I was raised to trust the matriarchs. I do not have an answer to that yet. If anyone here has one please do not give it to me tonight.

He teased me about the matriarchs, of course. Gerontocracy, he said, and that we promote people for being lonely, or very close to that. I told him it was a gross oversimplification. And then I stood in the Temple of Athame, and there was a beacon in it. A working Prothean beacon, hidden by the matriarchs for centuries. Everything I was raised to be proud of, that we reached the stars first, that we had the Citadel seat first, that the other species came to us, all of it fed from that room, quietly, by the oldest women we have. For a hundred years I had been told it was a hobby. It was what they had been standing on. And it is his argument again, only turned over. One center, holding what everyone else needs, deciding who gets it. The Protheans died of exactly that. And we, well. We did rather well out of it, for a while.
