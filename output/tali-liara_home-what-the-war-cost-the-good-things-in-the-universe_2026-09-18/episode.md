## The Machines That Remember — Tali

## Thessia, After — Liara

Inside the temple, the science team sent to meet us was already dead — throats cut, not Reaper work, which told me someone else had gotten there first. ## What the Trial Cost — Tali

## The Last Room — Tali

You say that like you're handing me something heavy and stepping back to watch how I carry it. Maybe you are. Fine — my turn, then. Keelah, I've been dreading this since you started walking us toward it three drinks ago.

He wasn't cinematic, walking in. Anderson gave him one. I'd like to tell you it was a good number. It was the kind of number a person gives when there isn't a good one left.

Then he talked to us, whoever was still standing in that room, and it wasn't a hundred-years-for-schoolchildren speech, whatever came after. Quieter than that. I've told this so many times the edges have worn smooth, and I've stopped being sure anymore which parts are memory and which parts are just how I've decided to tell it. That's the honest answer, Liara. Cold water down the spine, if a quarian's spine ever felt water directly, which it doesn't, but you understand me.

Then he picked the squad. Me. Garrus. I remember being almost offended it wasn't a bigger decision than it looked — parts off a shelf — and then ashamed of thinking that, because it meant he trusted the two of us with the last thing there was left to trust anyone with.

The run itself is mostly noise in my memory, which I recognize, professionally, as what a mind does to protect itself, and I've made a kind of truce with not fighting it. Streets that used to be London. The Reaper Destroyer parked across the road coming apart all at once, like a hull losing structural integrity — that part I remember clean, because it was work, and work still made sense when nothing else did. ## Two Years, and After — Liara

A version of him that had not finished being gone. Yes. That is exactly the shape of it, and I did not have the phrase until you handed it to me a moment ago, which I resent slightly, since it took me years to get anywhere near it and you managed it over a drink you had already half finished.

The first time, I did not sit still for any of it. We were ambushed for it twice: once by Blue Suns the old Shadow Broker sent to slow me down, once by Cerberus, who reached us before the Broker's people did and turned the whole search into a proposition. I sat in a room with a man made mostly of confidence and cybernetic eyes and let him talk me into becoming both a source and an asset in one conversation, because he had a location and I did not. Feron got me onto Omega, then onto the Broker's own base disguised as his prisoner, which is a sentence I have never enjoyed telling nearly as much as you enjoy hearing it. We lost the body twice on the way to keeping it, and the last thing I actually did in that whole search was hand him to strangers with an agenda I did not trust, on the strength of one man's optimism, and walk away not knowing whether I had rescued him or simply chosen who got to keep him. So the first grief had an object. A body I fought for, a coordinate I could chase, an organization to be furious at that also happened to be funding the project. This time there was no body, no coordinate — a beam of light, then silence, then eventually a wall.

I did not climb all the way up your cliff to end an evening on a wall with his name cut into it, though, and I will not let you steer me there. Tell me something the war did not manage to take from you. I have a list of my own, and for once I would like to hear yours first.

## The Good Things in the Universe — Tali

You would do this. Ask me to go first, then sit there with your hands folded like an interviewer, waiting to see what I hand over before you decide what you're willing to hand over yourself. I have watched you do that to a turian arms dealer for three hours once and I recognized the pose immediately. Fine. Here is the actual list, then, the real one, not the version I'd give an admiral's debrief. Turian cooks on the Citadel have gotten good enough at Reversal that I can sit at a table with humans and eat something that tastes like actual food instead of paste, for about twenty minutes, before my stomach starts sending increasingly rude messages to the rest of me — and it is worth every minute of it, and I have never once regretted the cramp afterward, and I will fight anyone who tells me that's not a small miracle of engineering dressed up as a dinner. And the geth are the ones who built that house. And the names. ## What the Evening Keeps — Liara

I am going to make mine shorter anyway, and not to be clever. Shorter is simply what the arithmetic gives me, Tali, and you of all people should respect an honest number over a flattering one. You get to keep a list the length of a life you are living at the speed everyone around you is living it — your Fleet, your father, your own hands on a Pilgrimage gift that turned out to matter. I get to keep a list the length of a life that is going to keep going, on its own, long after the people in it have stopped. Asari do not so much remember as accumulate, and accumulation is a colder verb than I would like it to be tonight. You asked for stranger. You are getting it.

Here is the first thing, then. Somewhere in a war I have already told you too much about, I put my mind against Shepard's — properly, the way my mother would have called an act of trust, not a diagnostic — to make sense of a vision that would otherwise have unmade him from the inside. What is left of that is not memory the way your memory works. It does not fade evenly; it sits in me the way strata sit in a dig site, out of order, surfacing when something above it is disturbed, and I have learned not to go looking for it on purpose. Our people use the same joining as a farewell, when there is time for one. There was not, with him. I have thought, more than once, that I got it backward — the goodbye arrived before I understood it would need to serve as one.

The second thing is smaller and costs more to say. I am going to watch you go, Tali. And Shepard. Yes. The sky has gone from the heat-sink orange you warned me about to something nearer the color under a struck match, and then, while neither of us was watching for it, to nothing much at all — the grey-blue Tikkun leaves behind instead of stars, since the dust past the Veil never quite lets a sky finish going dark. Legion's name is on that wall too, smaller weld, you told me, and I am glad, and I will not make either of us do that arithmetic again tonight. The peace held.
