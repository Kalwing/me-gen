Tali. Before I go anywhere else. He told me about his mother the same night, more or less, low, like he was setting something down carefully so it wouldn't rattle. Brilliant, he said, and adoring, and dancing in the kitchen, and then something in her didn't survive a death that happened to someone she loved, and by ten he was the one managing her, and at fourteen he called for help and was told, one way or another, that he wasn't her child anymore. She died in 2180 — I want to say before he finished a degree, but I honestly am not certain which one, whether it was the master's or something after, and I don't think he'd mind me saying I've lost that detail, he lost plenty of his own. Two women who were the center of a child's whole sky, and both of them became something their children had to survive instead. His didn't resolve either. I don't think it is supposed to.

We argued about the Protheans more than we agreed, which he considered a victory in itself. I was raised — my whole field was raised — to read an empire that held together for fifty thousand years as an argument for hierarchy: one order, disciplined enough to outlast anything a loose confederation could manage. He thought that was exactly backward, that a structure centralized that completely had nowhere to scatter to when the center was hit, so the whole thing went dark in a single stroke, and he liked to say the Reapers didn't defeat the Protheans, they unplugged them. He asked me once — I remember this one, actually asked, not just argued — whether I'd ever considered that the reason I loved them was the same reason I'd been raised to trust the matriarchs without asking why. I didn't have an answer for him. I still don't, entirely.

Here is the part I think most of you are missing. It is the reason I got to my feet at all. He was not a soldier. People keep speaking of him as though the gun were the whole of what he was for, and it wasn't — he trained as an Alliance tech specialist, and after Akuze the Alliance put him in front of children in Rouen to teach them robotics, and he led that program and loved it, and then a master's, and then doctoral work in medical imaging he never finished, and in between all of that, cooking, always cooking. His entire training, every year of it, was in keeping a body working or teaching a child to build something, never in ending anything. I have read enough history to know what it costs a person built that way to be handed a rifle at all — it isn't a stumble, it isn't cowardice dressed as fear, it is every instinct he'd spent a decade sharpening pointed the wrong direction on purpose, over and over, because the alternative was worse. That is not a small thing to ask of someone, and I don't think anyone in this room has said it plainly yet. I don't think it should be rated at all. I think it should just be understood, and I find I am the only one still standing here, which means someone else is waiting.
