Sha'ira, known to many as the Consort, was a well-known and influential asari who worked in the Consort's Chambers on the Citadel Presidium, near the financial district. She had many famous and powerful clients and offered personal services, entertainment and conversation, but was best known for the value of her advice. Appointments could take months to secure and a visit could cost half a year's pay. She was tended by acolytes of various races, mostly asari, including Nelyna. Figures such as the elcor diplomat Xeltan and Private Fredricks discussed her around the Embassies.

In Mass Effect (2183), Sha'ira asked Commander Shepard to help with a friend and client named Septimus, a turian who was spreading lies about her after she rejected him. If Shepard also told Xeltan that Septimus had revealed his secrets, Sha'ira gave the Commander a mysterious trinket usable at a Prothean ruin on Eletania. As a reward she offered a "gift of words"; if Shepard expressed disdain for it, she instead had sex with Shepard before handing over the trinket.

In Mass Effect 2 (2185), if Shepard had helped her, she sent a message, "A Gift of Words," warning that Shepard now headed "toward an even deeper darkness" and advising the Commander to ensure those fighting alongside them did so "with clear minds and glad hearts." A Normandy crew member had an appointment with her and objected strongly to another crewman calling her a prostitute. A news report suggested she might leave the Citadel after "years of bad press and rumors of intelligence leaks."

In the Mass Effect 3: Citadel DLC, Shepard could meet her among the guests on the Silver Coast Casino floor. She confirmed she had left the Citadel for a time, retiring to a colony in the Silean Nebula, and returned after the Reaper invasion began, saying one does not flee the heart of the galaxy in desperate times, "certainly not because of some scoundrel with a video camera." She would briefly explain how one becomes a consort before excusing herself to attend a client. Trivia notes "Sha'ira" derives from an Arabic word for "poetess," fitting her gift of words, and that she resembles an ancient Greek hetaera.
