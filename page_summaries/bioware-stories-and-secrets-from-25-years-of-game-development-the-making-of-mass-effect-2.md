This page transcribed the chapter "The Making of Mass Effect 2" from the 2020 book BioWare: Stories and Secrets from 25 Years of Game Development, written by Ben Gelinas. It recorded development anecdotes about Mass Effect 2. The sequel was larger than the original in squad size and scope, and was the first BioWare game to carry player choices forward from a prior title to affect the plot. The story followed a resurrected, Cerberus-backed Commander Shepard assembling a team for a Dirty Dozen-inspired suicide mission in which any squad mate could die based on player decisions. RPG mechanics were streamlined toward action, and the Mako was replaced by the M-44 Hammerhead. Editor Karin Weekes described a creative conflict between staff who wanted to redefine the series and those who wanted a direct sequel.

Casey Hudson explained the Illusive Man was a composite: the word "illusive" came from a rejected engine name inspired by a Phantom Menace line; the superhuman aura and grey hair and blue eyes drew on Anderson Cooper; and the behind-the-scenes political influence drew on Graem Bauer from 24. Martin Sheen, their first choice, was cast and held an unlit cigarette during recording to pace his delivery.

The writing team suffered repeated setbacks. Lead writer Drew Karpyshyn left during development and Mac Walters replaced him. Patrick Weekes broke a shoulder ice skating; other writers departed for BioWare Montreal and elsewhere, leaving Luke Kristjanson, Karin Weekes, and editor Cookie Everman to finish the script. Lead level designer Dusty Everman wrote most of the Normandy crew. Over Christmas 2007 Hudson diagrammed the game's whole structure on one page.

Other notes: Mass Effect: Genesis, a Dark Horse interactive comic, let PlayStation 3 players set first-game choices; new romances included Garrus Vakarian and Thane Krios; a shotgun bug once applied full blast damage per pellet, stripping textures off a krogan; Jack's dialogue was rewritten two days before final VO by reworking locked NPC lines; Luke Kristjanson hid backward phone numbers and song references in distress-beacon codes; and the hanar Spectre Blasto originated here as a near-Easter egg.
