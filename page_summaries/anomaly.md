Anomalies were oddities picked up by shipboard sensors that warranted closer investigation. They were almost always optional and had little impact on main objectives, but usually yielded resources, items, or new missions and assignments.

In the first Mass Effect, maps of uncharted worlds carried little initial information, and anomalies were denoted by question marks that changed to an X once explored.

In Mass Effect 2, when a planet or moon held an anomaly, EDI reported detecting one as the Normandy SR-2 entered planetary or lunar orbit, and the player had to actively scan the body to locate the source. During scanning, the targeting reticle showed a white line pointing toward the anomaly, and a white dot appeared on the map once the reticle drew close enough. Launching a probe at that dot unlocked the associated assignment and allowed landing on the world. Anomalies generally produced radio chatter that grew clearer as the reticle approached, though the anomalies added by the Firewalker Pack were completely silent. Locations with anomalies in Mass Effect 2 included Sinmara, Taitus and Karumto in the Caleston Rift; Tarith and Helyme in the Crescent Nebula; Neith in the Eagle Nebula; Kopis and Gei Hinnom in the Hades Nexus; Alchera, site of the Normandy Crash Site, and Lorek in the Omega Nebula; 2175 Aeia in the Rosetta Nebula; and many others, tied to N7 assignments, Project Firewalker sites, and loyalty missions.

In Mass Effect 3, EDI again announced detected anomalies, which usually indicated Search and Rescue retrievables such as fuel, War Assets, or intel, though they could also mark mission waypoints. In Mass Effect: Andromeda, Suvi Anwar reported anomalies, mentioning surface anomalies on entering planet view and non-planet-bound anomalies such as asteroids or wrecked ships on entering or scanning a system; there was at most one anomaly per planet but potentially several per system, each requiring a separate scan.
