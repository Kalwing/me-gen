This page transcribed concept artists' gallery commentary for "The Creatures of Mass Effect: Rachni," from the 2007 Mass Effect bonus content disc; nearly all remarks were by Matt Rhodes. Rhodes said the rachni were briefed as a mix of the creatures from Alien and from the novel Ender's Game, based on insects but aiming for a distinctly alien silhouette. Early exploratory work was by Sean Smailes, who pushed hard for new, intimidating silhouettes with multiple legs and limbs, scythes, spikes and quadruped forms. The additional concept artist Sung Kim was brought in for his experience with monster and creature silhouettes and was credited with opening the character up and giving the team new directions. Rhodes noted that one Sung Kim neck shape branched into two creatures: arched over, it became the rachni; with a face and wings added, it became the carnibug.

Rhodes described a rejected concept for a transparent rachni showing glowing veins and visible organs, abandoned because the technology was unavailable, with the team joking they might manage "transparent rachni" by Mass Effect 3. The final design was painted over that transparent version, kept its shape, and added fighting tendrils. He covered a small "baby spider" version that scuttled about and bit at ankles, and a simple rachni drone with a bloody internal glow beneath a black carapace, made by removing features assumed to develop in the adult stage. For the rachni eggs the team deliberately avoided the round eggs of Aliens, trying unsettling shapes such as spear-like eggs that stabbed into the ground with a hanging egg sac and a "caltrop" version with defensive spikes, reasoning that the rachni did not care much about their young. The rachni hive was designed as a simple insect-built geometric cone with staining around its outer lips, inspired by a documentary about bees. For the rachni queen the team was required to build on the existing rachni skeleton rather than create a new creature, making it look more like a monarch insect; because the queen would only ever be seen as an obscured silhouette with glowing eyes in a murky tube, the designers had freedom and aimed for a more regal form.
