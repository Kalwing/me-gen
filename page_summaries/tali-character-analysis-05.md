The fifth and final part of a hand-corrected video-essay character analysis traced the Shepard romance and drew conclusions from Tali's arc. In Mass Effect 1 Tali developed a crush she believed she could never act on, since they were too different and she was not yet a full adult by quarian standards; Shepard's kindness without caveats or kickbacks, unlike most of her family, charmed her. After the treason trial in Mass Effect 2, Shepard found her mildly ill from a sloppy suit repair; she explained that linking suit environments is quarians' deepest gesture of trust and admitted she trusted only Shepard that much. Feeling she was now his equal, she raised the relationship herself. Before the Omega 4 relay she removed her mask despite the health risk; per Kasumi she could not stop talking about Shepard afterward. Tali: "I'm running a fever, I've got a nasty cough... and it was totally worth it."

In Mass Effect 3 the roles reversed and Tali steadied Shepard after Thessia and the Cerberus base: "When I feel that way, I reach for you." Her drunken toast to Miranda ("She never gave in to him, never changed herself to please him") revealed she saw herself in Miranda fighting her father: "When do we get to stop reacting to our parents and start living for ourselves?" This scene originated the "emergency induction port" straw joke. If Rannoch was won and Tali romanced, she left with Shepard, gave him a photo of her unmasked face, and drank with "my human boyfriend — my father would have hated you." Her last words at the London beam run called Shepard her home. The essay explained "home" carries enormous weight for a quarian, exiled 300 years; "Keelah se'lai" roughly means "by the homeworld I hope to see one day," and calling someone your home means more than "I love you."

The essayist reasoned Tali would never choose Refuse, found Control (endless synthetic rebellions) and Synthesis less fitting, and concluded that factoring in Shepard's influence she would pick Destroy. The closing lessons: get involved in things bigger than yourself but be ready to bail; inspire change; never forget the people who helped you and repay them from kindness, not obligation; remember your parents' lessons but notice when they fail to practice them; be ready to sacrifice yourself, but within reason; and place duty to a cause below the individuals within it.
