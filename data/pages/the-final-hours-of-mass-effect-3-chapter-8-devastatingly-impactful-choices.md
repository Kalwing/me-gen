The following is a transcription of the eighth chapter of Geoff Keighley's documentary work The Final Hours of Mass Effect 3, published in 2012\.

## Contents

### Devastatingly Impactful Choices

When he isn't at home painting Warhammer 40k figures or spending time with his twin 5\-year\-old daughters, Preston Watamaniuk is thinking about Mass Effect. As Hudson's right\-hand man on all three games, he can complete the original Mass Effect in a one\-hour\-and\-38\-minute speed run. (This, incidentally, is 3 minutes shy of the world record.) If anyone on the team has a question about a possible way to play through the game, they usually ask Watamaniuk, because he has completed each one about 60 or 70 times.

Besides bragging rights, Watamaniuk's hardcore play style yields a real benefit during team meetings. Since he knows all the ways to play the game, he also knows what different groups of fans are expecting. Asked to give an example, he points to a scene that was added during the final two weeks on Mass Effect 2. In one of his final play\-throughs Watamaniuk realized that the team hadn't created a payoff for players who remained faithful to their romantic interest from the first game. This, it should be pointed out, was not an easy thing to do. Ashley, Kaiden \[sic] and Liara were hardly seen in Mass Effect 2 on purpose, and temptations were introduced throughout the story.

There was no time to record dialogue for a new pay\-off scene, but Watamaniuk was adamant that the game needed to acknowledge a player's loyalty to his or her digital squeeze. The compromise was a simple yet poignant scene in which Shepard longingly looks at a photo of his love interest in his quarters. "It's the little moments like that which make our games different," says Watamaniuk. "Even if that moment only happens for a couple thousand people out of millions, it's worth it."

There was also the issue of what to do for the millions of people who fiddled around on the side during Mass Effect 2\. (You're busted: According to statistics revealed for the first time in this story, more than 70 percent of players fall into this camp.) Watamaniuk told the team that he wanted a "massive consequence" for these cheaters when they inevitably run into the romantic interest from the first game during the events of Mass Effect 3.

The consequence of cheating led to a deeper discussion about the aftermath of choices in the third game. Mass Effect 1 and 2 had decision points that changed elements of the plot and switched around characters, but it wasn't as if all those choices always had sweeping consequences. Killing Wrex, for instance, would change some dialogue and cause him to be replaced by another Krogan, \[sic] Wreav, in Mass Effect 2\. Characters could die at the end of Mass Effect 2, but their fate was usually sealed by choices made earlier in the game by the player — not a heat\-of\-the\-moment dialogue decision. For the third game the team wanted players to agonize over decisions with devastating consequences on both sides. "If we did it right players will hopefully say, 'Man, I'm really not sure if I made the right choice,'" says Walters.

Two main plot mints were identified as having the biggest opportunity for impact. The first was the resolution of the ongoing war between the Quarians \[sic] and the Geth, \[sic] the robot AIs that became sentient and revolted against their masters. The second was tied to the genophage, a long\-running theme in the series. A new cure would be manufactured by the Salarians \[sic] and it would be up to the player to decide whether it is deployed. It's one of the game's finest moments, as you agonize over the decision and have multiple opportunities to change your mind. There is no right or wrong path, but the decision has a dramatic impact on the galaxy and the player's experience during the rest of the game. In practice most players will make one choice, and then, after completing the game, head to YouTube to discover what could have happened if they pushed the story in another direction.

One other choice the team made during Mass Effect 3's production was to introduce same\-sex relationships for both male and female players. The topic was a subject of much debate both online and inside of BioWare. Ultimately the writers and designers had to balance the player's right to free will with the fact that you are playing the role of a defined character that may have its own sexual preference. Hudson and the team opted to open up the possibilities, but only after first making sure the voice actors were comfortable with the decision. "Hopefully the way we depict love in the future is the way it will be in the future," he says.

The ways players could experience Mass Effect was expanding, but the production schedule was not. Even with the addition of multiplayer, BioWare was hoping to complete the game in less than two years, by the end of 2011\. Something had to give, and by March 2011 it was time for Hudson and the team to face reality.

### Video: Mac on the Genophage Decision

**Mac Walters**: I really wanted to sort of set up this betrayal with Mordin, or Padok Wiks, earlier on, so that you actually had opportunities as you were going along to say "hey, these guys are trying to screw you over." But then, also the opportunity to say nothing. And I'm glad we\-\- and it's something we haven't done before, and nobody was quite sure that that was gonna work, and how that would feel, but it's funny; it's one of those things where people have come back since and go like "that was awesome. It's like I kinda wanted to tell him, but then I didn't." Sylvia, I think it was, or the writer working on that, had this amazing idea of why don't we use an Interrupt where, you know, Mordin or Padok are actually walking away from you slowly and you have this opportunity to shoot them in the back. And it was kind of a unique way of using the interrupts that we haven't used before, because... it's not just\-\- it's not the obvious "I gotta kill this person" Interrupt; it's the "what the hell do I do here?" And we actually slow down time and you have to think about it and it's just like... in the end, I think, you know, the debates we had over how to pull that off are great because people have played it. They're just like, "oh my god. I can't believe, you know, I was forced to make that decision. I don't even know if I made the right decision." So... You know, sort of, I think fighting over that sort of how to bring a personal sort of side to that epic decision, was one that took a long time to come to fruition. But in the end, I think we did a great job with it.
