### That Which Is True

Caroline Livingstone couldn't afford to buy her son the Nike shoes he wanted. That got to her. As a working actor in the Canadian public theater scene, Livingstone traveled with her young son from city to city around Western Canada. One week the single mother was playing the Wicked Witch of the West in a production of the  *Wizard of Oz* in Regina, Saskatchewan; the next, a role in *Spring Awakening* in Edmonton. Caroline loved the way being on stage made her feel, and if she was being honest, that was just about the only good thing about being a local theater actress. "It's a tough, poor life," she recalls.

Then one day her friend Shauna called with a proposition. Would Caroline be interested in performing the voice of the healer in Neverwinter Nights, a new game from BioWare? She said yes, one thing led to another, and before long Livingstone stepped into the role as BioWare's in\-house producer and director of voice\-overs.

Her arrival couldn't have been more perfectly timed. Hudson and the Mass Effect team had grand visions of building a game with emotional resonance, but that was possible only if the players believed the voice\-over performances. The "digital acting" in Mass Effect, like most games, was still quite primitive. But somehow the combination of well\-directed line reads with subtle effects like blinking, expressive eyes and natural, motion\-captured movements gave organic life to these virtual characters.

When Mass Effect arrived in November 2007 the digital acting and the strongly developed characters were cited in most reviews as the game's strong point. And while the game was generally well reviewed, there were complaints about the vehicle, the Mako, being hard to control, annoyances with the combat system, and extremely long level load times that were presented as elevator rides to new locations. By Christmas 2007 Hudson was happy the game was out the door and selling well, but couldn't help but feel a little disappointed. "It didn't initially hit with the impact that I probably hoped that it would," he now admits.

Still, there were signs that something special was starting to happen. Right around Christmas a letter arrived at BioWare. The writer's son, who suffered from Asperger's syndrome, had difficulty with nonverbal communication skills. But after playing Mass Effect and learning how to judge emotions in a safe environment, the boy started to be more outgoing in real life.

The line between reality and games was starting to blur. That introduced a host of new complications. One of the team's early goals with Mass Effect was to create a believable in\-game romance between two characters, assuming the player chose that storyline. Hudson imagined these romances as a three\-act love story that could play out over the entire trilogy. In the first game male protagonists could mate with Ashley; females with a male soldier named Kaiden; \[sic] and both characters could unlock a "paramour" with Liara, an Asari \[sic] female alien. Those options were good enough for most players, but same\-sex relationships were not possible. "We'd get letters from fans who would say, 'You're offering all the choices except the ones I want,'" remembers Hudson.

No one wanted to frustrate the fans, but the outcry underscored the perils of player choice. How do you balance what players want to do with the notion of defined characters? How far should a game let you take your choices before the traits of the character "Commander Shepard" disappear and he or she becomes nothing more than a mindless vessel acting out the users' whims? The exclusion of same\-sex romances was a hot button issue, but the same struggle between player agency and defined storytelling applied to many other facets of the game, such as the decision by the developers to kill off a character for story reasons when certain players don't want it to happen inside of "their" world.

The debate over the romances in Mass Effect extended as far as Fox News when, in a January 2008 report, Cooper Lawrence, a misinformed, gun\-for\-hire social pundit, blasted the game for its supposedly graphic "alien sex scenes" that were corrupting the minds of young boys. Lawrence, whom, as I pointed out on the air, had never played the game, much less developed a romance in it, was later forced to publicly recant her comments in *The New York Times*.

It was early 2008 in Jasper, Alberta. After more than four years of hard work on Mass Effect, the team members were beginning to think about a sequel and trying to throw their lives back into balance. One weekend Hudson invited Watts and lead artist Mike Trottier to hike up Pyramid Peak in Jasper, a mountain Hudson sometimes still climbed with his father. After finishing some beers and hot dogs, the trio started hiking and began to reflect on how much their had changed during Mass Effect. Each now had children and joked that they already knew what was in store for the next half decade: two more Mass Effect games and raising their kids.

Realizing how structured his life had become, Hudson decided he wanted to challenge himself to do something new. Well, something new as in something he hadn't done in a long time. Sixteen years after he last piloted a plane, he suddenly had a desire to fly again. In the spring he put a four\-seat Diamond Star XLS prop plane on order and started the process of renewing his pilot's license. Before the plane arrived he told friends that he might even get it painted the colors of the *Normandy*, the player's spaceship in Mass Effect.

Back at the office, Hudson spent time thinking about Mass Effect 2. He put together a huge spreadsheet that analyzed all the positive and negative feedback about the game from 97 different reviews. And in a black notebook labeled "SFX," he began charting out ideas for the sequel. If Mass Effect was the wake\-up call about the pending Reaper invasion and the third game was to become an all\-out war against them, he, Karpyshyn and Mac Walters needed a good second act to the story. (Karpyshyn left the team shortly after Mass Effect 2 began production to focus on the development of BioWare's latest release, the massively multiplayer game Star Wars: The Old Republic.)

Walters and Hudson centered in on a dirty dozen story in Shepard would spend the second act of the game canvassing the galaxy to recruit what he called "commandos" in his notes. This diverse group of characters — including familiar faces from the first game — would team up with Shepard to fight a mysterious force known as the Collectors. Somehow tied to the Reapers, these aliens were wiping out human colonies around the galaxy. The second game would culminate in a battle, a so\-called suicide mission in which many of the characters could — including the player's Commander Shepard.

The sequel also gave Walters a chance to bring back his idea for the shadowy organization of Cerberus, only briefly hinted at in the original game. Hudson ran with this idea and suggested that the organization be run by a character he imagined would look like Anderson Cooper from CNN and act like Jack Bauer's brother Graem from 24, a rogue charlatan who had enough power to tell the president of the United States what to do. The character would be known as the Illusive Man, a self\-appointed protector of humanity who would do whatever is necessary — even outside the political system — to ensure the race survives and controls the Reapers. Martin Sheen was selected as the voice of the character.

Other changes were on the table for the sequel. For a while, the team wanted Shepard's ship, the *Normandy*, to be destroyed at the start of the game as part of his untimely demise. (The Illusive Man would bring Shepard back to life and help him assemble a new ship, named the Leviathan, in the first act of the story.) Watts and the art team pushed back because they loved the Normandy design and the interiors, inspired by the George Clooney movie Solaris. Eventually the design team relented; the ship was blown up at the beginning, but it was replaced by the nearly identical *Normandy SR\-2*.

One element of the game that definitely needed refinement was the combat. Although the team had envisioned Mass Effect as being as easy to play as Halo, the mechanics in the first game weren't nearly as refined. Cover was difficult to use, players had to pause the game to activate special powers, and the health and ammunition systems weren't as seamless as other games. Corey Gaspur, a young designer who has his arms covered in black tattoos inspired by comic book artist Bernie Wrightson, co\-wrote a memo to Watamaniuk to suggest significant overhauls to the sequel's gameplay. Space combat was briefly considered as an addition but was dismissed early on so the team could focus on refining the core action mechanics.

BioWare wanted to keep its core role\-playing fans happy but at the same time it wanted to compete with shooters like Gears of War. Still, the debate about whether Mass Effect was a "shooter" or an "RPG" would rage on online message boards. Had BioWare abandoned its core fans to build an action game like Halo or Gears of War? "The whole debate is rather silly," offers Watamaniuk. "Battlefield 3 is a shooter with great progression and upgrades, does that make it an RPG? Both of these game genres are blending together."

First announced in early 2009, Mass Effect 2, all things considered, had a relatively smooth development process. Still, the scope of the game was monstrous, featuring up to 12 different potential squadmates and a massive endgame battle against the Collectors, who were trying to construct a new Reaper out of harvested human remains.

With so many new characters in the mix, Hudson and the writers made a conscious decision to largely exclude Ashley and Kaiden, \[sic] the main love interests from the first game. "That was all by design," Hudson explains to me one evening in his office as he looks at a photo of his wife in Times Square. "I wanted Mass Effect to be a three\-act love story, and missing your love throughout the second game was exactly how I wanted players to feel."

When it arrived in early January 2010, Mass Effect 2 received the highest scores Electronic Arts had ever seen for one of its games. (BioWare was acquired by Electronic Arts about a month before Mass Effect arrived in November 2007\.) All the great ideas from the first game — the branching plot, the memorable characters and the richly realized universe — had been made even better, and most of the key issues from game one had been addressed. Even though it arrived in late January, critics started saying Mass Effect 2 was a contender for game of the year. *Time Magazine* raved that Mass Effect was the "Avatar of videogames—except it's better written." Fox News decided to sit this one out.

Mass Effect 2 finally became the phenomenon that Hudson had hoped for the first time around. Now came the even tougher part: trying to wrap up the trilogy with an even better sequel.

### Video: Hudson on Player Reaction to Characters

**Casey Hudson**: We knew from the beginning of the Mass Effect series that this was going to be a big focus for us\-\- is technology around creating characters that could act with emotion, and then trying to use that technology in ways we've never done before, where maybe a character can just look at you and you feel an emotion from... just on that look. And so, we developed those kinds of things, and it was intentional that that would be kind of a new thing for this series, but we actually had no idea how much of a reaction that we would get from players, to these characters. We've always had romances in BioWare games going back to Baldur's Gate, but we've never really had this kind of reaction to characters in a game, where people, you know... it's almost like these are real experiences and real relationships that people want to see more of, and they demand time with certain characters in each game that we make. And so, we try and build that feedback in. And that's why some of the characters in Mass Effect 2 that had previously not been love interests, like, Garrus and Tali, in their story lines, we worked in love interests. Because, even though we couldn't conceive of it in Mass Effect 1, people saw these characters, like a character like Garrus, very interesting alien character; it wasn't meant to be a lot of interest in the beginning, but because he acts with emotion and real humanity, people want to get to know him better.
