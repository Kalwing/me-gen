The following is a transcription of the first chapter of Geoff Keighley's documentary work The Final Hours of Mass Effect 3, published in 2012\.

## Contents

### The Beginning of the End

When Casey Hudson looks to the frigid sky above his backyard in Edmonton, Alberta, he doesn't see blackness. He sees possibility. "The vastness of what could be up there is just so awesome," he suggests to me one night in January 2012, invoking his favorite adjective to conjure up thoughts of other galaxies or civilizations.

As he looks up into the minus 35 degree air, the distant light signatures of Venus and Jupiter flicker behind his white puffs of breath. Unshaven, with his curly black hair tucked inside an Edmonton Oilers baseball cap, he's come outside after 15 hours of work not to look up, but to take care of what's below: a patch of ice that he waters every night as a makeshift skating rink for his two young boys.

The steam from the warm water evaporates into the air, and Hudson has a fleeting moment of calm. He knows there isn't much time for stargazing. Not tonight. The event horizon that is the final hours of game development is an all\-consuming black hole, and Hudson's new game, Mass Effect 3, will soon be finished. Now it's just a question of how painful the last few days will be. But until it's done, tomorrow, Saturday, will be just another day at the office.

If you arrive in Edmonton and ask some folks where to find BioWare, they wouldn't be wrong if they told you, "Look for the nondescript office building with the dermatologist, pub, and hotel with a big GET FIT IN 2012! light\-up sign." Saturday morning around 9 a.m., Hudson pulls his Audi TT — with 130,000 miles on the odometer — into the front parking lot of this strange complex with no visible sign for BioWare, one of the world's most respected video game developers. The back of his car is covered in mud, completely camouflaging the Alberta license plate. The car wash can wait. Mass Effect 3 can't.

Here, on the third floor, Hudson and a mighty team of Canadian programmers, artists, designers and writers are crafting one of 2012's most ambitious and anticipated video games. Set about 175 years in the future, Mass Effect has transcended its medium with books, comics, action figures, iPad games, clothing and even a forthcoming movie from Legendary Pictures.

Ihe overarching science fiction world is memorable, but Mass Effect's real innovation comes in the way its story adapts to choices made by individual players. Like a cutting\-edge choose\-your\-own\-adventure book, a supporting character can live or die, or a world can prosper or perish, all based on the profound story you make as hero Commander Shepard. (To further increase the immersion, you can pick whether Shepard is male or female.) The ways to play through Mass Effect are as vast as the possibilities that exist in the night sky above Edmonton.

The matrix of choices is nearly infinite, but the team is focused on something more definite: the final 175 bugs or "open issues" that must be fixed. Long gone are the days of dreaming up fanciful new characters, vast worlds and emotionally piercing dialogue. As he walks through the Byzantine blue\-walled corridors on the Mass Effect floor, designer Preston Watamaniuk sums it up best: "A game is a bit like an oil tanker. The closer you are to the end of the journey the less room you have to maneuver," he says.

With so many possibilities, the testing phase of Mass Effect 3 requires scientific precision. Monitors in the hall use real\-time charts and graphs to track how many issues remain, not unlike the way TV networks update voting results on election night. At any time the chart can change and a department will be put on notice to plug up the leak in the oil tanker. Watamaniuk reinforces that point with an email he sends out to the entire group: "The policy this weekend is simple," he writes. "Everyone is on call. If you get a bug that has been triaged by the leads you must fix it immediately."

"Triaged" is a term used to describe a process in which Hudson, Watamaniuk, lead programmer David Falkner and the other core team members sit around a conference room and go through every single outstanding issue. Munching on peanut butter cookies, they sit for hours and debate bugs. Today's most pressing hitch: On the PlayStation 3 a game will crash if the player leaves it sitting on the multiplayer main menu for six straight hours. Hudson sits at the keyboard and "sends back" the problem for someone to fix. Every day the number of bugs decreases — they hope — until the team hits zero. That's when Mass Effect 3 is officially sent to certification.

Walking out of the conference room after Saturday's triage, Hudson sneaks over to the microwave to heat up his Starbucks quad Americano. You can tell he's worn down but completely focused on not disappointing BioWare's faithful fans. A quote from Mass Effect 3 perfectly sums up his state of mind. "The end of the world reminds you what you forgot to do," Admiral Anderson says as the Reaper aliens invade Earth. In this case it's the trilogy that's ending, and Casey' Hudson doesn't want to be the guy who forgot to wrap up a plot line that fans want to see resolved.

As he walks back to his desk and opens up his email, Hudson knows he is just hours away from saying goodbye to a series that has consumed nearly a quarter of his life. Now, as the final arc of Commander Shepard's story arrives, it is time to tell the tale of how this group of eclectic Canadians — including a garbage man, a furniture truck driver and a local theater actress — came together to create a franchise that has pushed the narrative potential of gaming in bold new directions. Along the way there have been moments of triumph, challenges to
overcome and dark hours of creative despair. These are the final hours of Mass Effect 3\.

### Video: Casey Describes His Typical Workday

**Casey Hudson**: So, my typical day would be... I get up, and I make breakfast for my boys. Even when when we were doing ninety\-hour weeks, I made sure that, every morning, I made breakfast for my boys, drove my son to school, and then in the evening, I'd be there for bedtime, even if I had to come home for bedtime, and then go back. Usually, my day, in a typical day will be, you know, sort of a combination of meetings. Usually, it's meetings to go over the story, or we'll sit down and look at a system that we're trying to design and, you know, I'll, you know, help make decisions about what we should be doing and try and provide some direction there, whether it's technical or creative. If it's kind of early production, maybe I'm home in time for supper and then I can spend time with my family. And otherwise, I'll come home and put the kids to sleep and then come back. And that's what we were doing in the last phase where, you know, we were just cycling through hundreds and hundreds of bugs and tasks per day; and I would just come back to work and then work until, you know, eleven or twelve o'clock at night.
