## Contents

### The Letter on the Door

What would the first day of the rest of Casey Hudson's life feel like? Today, February 13, is that day. Mass Effect 3 is finished and while there is more work to do on downloadable content, the detachment process has begun.

Casey had thought about this day over the years and was never sure what it was going to feel like. Although he doesn't want to admit it, he kind of wishes he was still working on the game. But for now, with no triage meeting on the books and his Outlook calendar strangely bare, he decides to finally take me to see his airplane at a hanger \[sic] about 20 minutes from the office.

As we drive along the snowy highways of Edmonton, Hudson begins to reminisce not about the game, but about how beautiful it is to far away from the rest of the entertainment world. He remembers the days when he and his wife — who started dating back in the seventh grade — would go to her grandparent's farm and just run around with no sense of place or time. He wants the same for his kids, and that's why he recently invested in a quarter of land — 160 acres outside of Edmonton. Nothing is on the land and that's precisely the point. "You look around and there is just space in every direction. It's like your own little world," he says as we drive down a highway with no end in sight.

At one point during the car trip I remember a letter I saw Hudson tape to his office door a few weeks ago. Lots of fan letters come from around the world to BioWare, but for some reason this neatly printed letter resonated with Hudson during the final hours. It came from a young boy named Zachary Sentz, a seventh\-grader from East Berlin, Pa. "I am just wondering if maybe you and some of your co\-workers could figure something out to make the trilogy a little longer," the young boy asked.

More than just asking the question, Zachary had some ideas. What if, he suggested, Shepard and his or her true love had a child, and, in a twist similar to *Red Dead Redemption*, you ended up playing as that child. As the child you could "wander and find a cave with a prophecy written on the wall and the child knows what he or she needs to do," he continued, ending the letter with the line, "This is all I've come up with for the idea."

There was something poignant and simple about Sentz' letter. And while he never knew it when he wrote it, his letter probably reminds Hudson of the young boy in the post\-credits sequence of the game who asks a man, played by astronaut Buzz Aldrin, if there are other tales of "The Shepard" to be told. This beautiful scene, set at night on a snowy farm in some unknown place, was an important coda for Hudson. Having recently finished the game myself, I asked him about that scene and wondered if it ties into the letter. "Everyone is going to have a different ending to some degree based on their choices and their galactic readiness," he says, "so it was important for me to have one final scene where everyone has the exact same experience."

Does the young boy's letter hint at something more, namely that the series continues? While there will definitely be more Mass Effect games and other stories told in the future, Commander Shepard's story has concluded. Hudson isn't sure where the series goes next, but he's pretty confident that he never wants to tell any stories that take place in a post\-Shepard era. "Whatever we do would likely happen before or during the events of Mass Effect 3, not after," he suggests.

As we arrive at the hanger, Hudson unlocks the door and inside. Besides his plane he is storing some friends' cars for the winter and a buddy's helicopter across the three bays. He walks up to his plane and begins to flick switches like a video game controller, making sure to show me the cool GPS system and screens that let him see the topography through the clouds as he flies over Jasper.

Sensing his fascination with the plane, technology and outer space, I ask him if perhaps his next adventure will be trying to fly into space on Virgin Galactic.

To my surprise he says that no, that doesn't really interest him at all.

"I'd rather pilot something, not be a passenger," he says as he walks around and inspects his plane. "I want to be in control of where I'm going, what I'm there to do and the choices that I'm making."

And that, right there, is exactly why players feel the way they do about Mass Effect.

### Video: Casey Discusses the Message of ME3

**Casey Hudson**: I think one of the really neat things about video games, as a medium, is that there can be a message in them, without making a statement. You know, with with a passive medium like a book or a movie, you know, it kinda has to... it can raise questions, and I think that's usually what people respond to the best is, when a movie raises a question that makes you think about it. Sometimes, it makes a statement. But the great thing about a video game is that you can explore an issue, and let a player explore their thoughts on the issue interactively. And so, you don't have to say something specifically about, for example in Mass Effect 3, the idea of organic life and synthetic life, and... whether synthetic life will ever rival our intelligence, and whether we should incorporate technology more and more into our bodies, and things like this. You can explore those ideas, and experience some of the consequences and think about, you know, what you think is right, without the game having to make a statement on it. And to me, that's more interesting.
