The following is a transcription of the fifth chapter of Geoff Keighley's documentary work The Final Hours of Mass Effect 3, published in 2012\.

## Contents

### The Possibility Space

Long before she wrote *Atlas Shrugged*, Russian\-American novelist Ayn Rand was a struggling playwright in the 1930s. Her first play produced in Los Angeles and then on Broadway was *The Night of January 16th*. The story was inspired by the life of Ivan Krueger, \[sic] a businessman who controlled the worldwide market for red phosphorous matches and supposedly committed suicide after facing bankruptcy. In the play, a fictional businessman named Bjorn falls to his death in New York City. Although he left a suicide note, the police suspect his mistress and secretary Karen might have pushed him off the edge for unknown reasons.

The purpose of the play is a trial to decide if his death was a suicide or murder. But instead of writing one ending, Rand came up with a brilliant concept. What if she wrote two endings to the trial and, in one of the first bits of interactive narrative on record, let the audience serve as the jury? Every night Broadway theatergoers voted on Karen's guilt or innocence, and the results impacted the final scenes of the play.

These days interactive narrative may not be employed frequently on Broadway, but it's often used in the game industry. In Knights of the Old Republic, for instance, Hudson and his team players a natural choice of joining the light or the dark side of the force, which impacts the ending of the game.

For Mass Effect, Hudson envisioned a burn\-your\-mouth\-on\-the\-soup level of ambition for the branching narrative. Instead of putting a fork in the road near the end of the game, what if the player could make real choices from the start that would cascade throughout not just the game, but the entire trilogy? For example, a player could choose to kill off a squad mate in the first game and he or she would disappear from the next two games. Or a player's character could fall in love in one game and then, in the sequel, the love interest would remember their relationship.

All these choices would compound each other to create a dizzying array of possible outcomes, all of which had to somehow dovetail with an overarching plot. "We had to get comfortable with the idea that a large percentage of our work might be seen by only a very small percentage of players," explains lead cinematics designer Parrish Ley, known to fans for a Facebook page devoted to his extravagant moustache during Mass Effect 2.

The vastness of possibility was staggering. It was also scary because, at the same time, Hudson wanted to significantly increase the cinematic production values. It's one thing to change a game's story when you're just dealing with text on the screen or characters with one gesture of animation per line. But for Mass Effect the team envisioned digital actors that had wrinkles in their skin, motion\-captured movements, moving eyes and dramatic voice\-over performances.

Like most role\-playing games, choice would extend to in\-game conversations in which players could steer a dialogue exchange in a variety of ways. These conversations in RPGs are supposed to be dramatic, but in most games the tension is fractured: The game pauses every time the player needs to read all the responses and, once the player picks one, the character never reads the line aloud. The Mass Effect team wanted to change this dynamic by giving the player a voice in conversations, which would build more personality and heighten the drama.

The conversation mechanic would further be enhanced by a dialogue wheel that would let players rapidly select the tenor of a response — neutral, friendly, hostile, investigative — then have the character act it out inside the game world. Just like the jury in Ayn Rand's play decides between guilty and innocent, the player would render his or her verdict in each scene by picking an option on the dialogue wheel. On top of all this the game would track the player's morality by logging whether the choices were heroic (paragon) or ruthless (renegade), eventually leading to different story paths.

All these variables would be layered on top of the usual BioWare choices: You could play as either a male or female character, and select from various alien and human characters to accompany you on missions as teammates. When it was all added up, the possible permutations and combinations were mind\-numbing. Preston Watamaniuk and his design team realized they were going to have to keep track of 700 different "plot states," a dizzying array of 1s and 0s that could impact something as small as the tone in a line of dialogue or as big as eliminating an entire character from key scenes. The idea was brilliant but terrifying, especially as these choices had to be carried over from one game to the next. "The checks we were writing on Mass Effect 1 — we had no idea how we were going to pay \[them] off down the road," admits Ley.

Ask anyone on the Mass Effect team about the way Casey Hudson directs a game and you hear one word in response: vision. Hudson is a true polymath who knows how to draw, how to program, how to design in 3D, even how to compose music. (He also dabbles in architecture and designed the house he lives in with his wife and two sons.) All these talents allow him to speak the language of the artists and programmers and to get his hands dirty when he wants something realized a certain way.

That's exactly what happened one night in 2007\. After work Hudson headed to the local supermarket, bought some slabs of meat, and spent most of the night photographing a combination of steaks, circuit boards, ribs and screws in his basement. The next day he showed up at work with the photos, which formed the backbone of a mysterious dream\-like sequence the main character, Commander Shepard, sees in Mass Effect after being exposed to an alien beacon on the planet Eden Prime.

The fact that Hudson was able to finally focus on story points underscored the progress the team was making. By early 2007 the plot was stabilizing and writers such as Mac Walters — a BioWare fan who got the job after building a user\-made add\-on to Neverwinter Nights on his Pentium laptop — started to work on defining the personalities of the various aliens. Shauna Perry, who ran a troupe of corporate improv comedians and managed a folk band on the side, began auditioning actors who might able to bring the characters to life. First choices didn't always work out as planned. A character named Ashley, the male Commander's love interest, was originally voiced by E.G. Daily from *The Powerpuff Girls*, only to be re\-cast after none of the team members felt they could fall in love with the character.

Love would play a role in Mass Effect, but the main plotline would involve Commander Shepard's "call to glory," as Hudson put it. Sent to investigate a mysterious beacon left behind by an extinct alien race, now known as the Protheans, Shepard would cross paths with Saren, a Turian \[sic] alien who double\-crosses The Alliance, a United Nations\-like body in charge of the galaxy. \[sic] Saren works on building his own army of machines, led by sentient AI robots named the Geth \[sic] that had revolted against their creators, the nomadic Quarians. \[sic] Later in the game players would realize that Saren had been brainwashed by the Reapers to do their bidding (a link back to the "master slaves" idea), the first ominous sign of the Reapers' pending return.

As development progressed, the team had to adjust certain elements of the story. The Geth, for example, just weren't scary enough. They were supposed to be cold\-blooded killing machines, but in one early scene a Geth ran over to help his robot friend was shot on the battlefield. Sitting on the "couch of truth" in the office, a place where the team leads reviewed the game's progress, Watts saw the sequence and began singing "Lean on Me." To make the Geth scarier an idea was hatched: What if they impaled dead humans on 20\-foot\-high mechanical spikes that pump the cadavers full of cybernetics and turn them into mindless killing husks? Yep, much better.

By the spring of 2007 it was clear that parts of Mass Effect's original vision, hugely ambitious from the start, would never be realized. The entire online Xbox Live trading system was scotched, and Walters' idea of a plot about a shadowy organization named Cerberus, which conducted human experiments on a planet named Misery, was designed but never made it into the game. Similarly, a volcanic mining planet named Caleston was planned as a level but cut due to production delays. (Some of those story elements folded into the player's adventures on the planet Therum.) More than anything else the game's combat system continued to be refined as the team balanced the desire for a mainstream action\-oriented experience with the company's roots in a deep role\-playing game with skills, stats and progression.

By the summer of 2007 one pivotal moment in the game was starting to feel exactly like Hudson's vision for a cinematic adventure with interactive choice and consequence. On Virmire, a humid, tropical planet seen near the end of the game, the team designed a showdown between the player and Wrex, a Krogan \[sic] mercenary that is a key part of the story. This race of dinosaur\-like creatures was facing extinction due to the genophage, a plague created by another alien species, the Salarians, \[sic] which wiped out the ability for Krogans \[sic] to procreate. Saren found a cure to the genophage and was planning to breed a Krogan army to join the Geth, but Shepard is given orders to destroy the base, the cure and, of course, Saren. Wrex, on the other hand, has different priorities. He'd rather save the cure and the future of his civilization.

Does Wrex see your side of the story or do you have to kill this character you've come to love? The outcome of the scene is entirely up to the player's dialogue choices. Other elements come into play, too. If the player had helped Wrex earlier in the game by retrieving his family armor, he trusts you and it is easier to convince him of the bigger picture. Most games have showdown sequences like this but they are a Hobson's choice — you know there is really just one way to advance the plot. (Hint: You shoot the guy and move on.) But in Mass Effect the story can go either way. The real fun comes when players talk with their friends and they discover that yes, they could have made the other choice for a different outcome.

By September the team was about to make another discovery: Mass Effect was inches away from being finished. On September 25, 2007, at 5:05 p.m., Casey Hudson emailed the team with the news. "Congratulations team! Last night's build, 635, has been submitted to Microsoft as RC2 — the first build with a real possibility of being 'the one,'" he wrote. The Mass Effect trilogy was about to officially begin.
